## I. Introduction

As one critical factor inducing urban congestion, intersection vehicular delay reduction is essential for urban traffic management. As early as in 1963, Miller [1] proposed the first signal control algorithm for adjusting signal timing dynamically. Based on the principle of Miller's model, researchers have developed various adaptive signal control systems such as SCOOT [2], SCATS [3], RHODES [4], TUC [5], and some have been widely used in urban road networks.
For existing adaptive control methods, the controller generally calculates signal timing plans based on vehicular flow information over look-ahead horizon. Recent research has shown that advanced data collection and communication significantly facilitated adaptive signal control evaluation [6], [7]. For systems, such as SCOOT and SCATS, the information collection is reactive and thus unable to anticipate changes in traffic flow. By setting detectors at upstream intersection, PREDICT algorithm [8] is capable to predict traffic flow with upstream phases and queue lengths. However, PREDICT algorithm is only effective for fixed timing control at upstream intersections. Tan et al. [9] developed an arrival time estimation algorithm with GPS data, and bus arrival time is treated as a posteriori estimation parameter. Sun and Zhang [10] proposed a model to anticipate traffic flow and turning proportion at an actuated control network. Zheng and Recker [11] employed an exponential smoothing method to predict arrival flow from upstream adaptive-actuated intersections. In these studies, "arrival" refers that vehicle

reaches the stop line or fixed loop detector. Consequently, downstream queue length was not considered in the arrival estimation, which may result in prediction deviation in high traffic demand [12]. Recently, Tiaprasert et al. [13] proposed a queue estimation algorithm based on discrete wavelet transform (DWT) and achieved satisfactory results in connected environment.

Another key point in adaptive signal control is the control strategy. Based on the predicted vehicular flow in the planning horizon, the signal control algorithm allocates phase sequence and duration to minimize expected vehicle delay. Decomposing the horizon into several intervals, Dynamic Programming (DP) is convenient for formulation. If each DP stage corresponds to a fixed time interval (e.g. 5s), phases sequence is assigned to each interval to form an optimal solution (OPAC [14], PRODYN [15]). If each stage represents a complete phase, as in COP (Controlled optimization of Phases [16]), DP is formulated to calculate the phase duration. Once the allocated phase duration is zero, the phase is skipped in the optimal solution. Thus, COP possesses more flexibility in phase sequence compared with other control methods. Moreover, mathematical programming methods were also widely used. Lin and Wang [17] formulated an enhanced 0-1 mixed-integer linear programming which could produce variable cycle length. Since the model was solved by branch and bound algorithm, whether it could perform real-time computation was still an open question. Lertworawanich et al. [18] used a goal programming model with multiple objectives to minimize total vehicle delay in an oversaturated network, but the proposed genetic algorithm (GA) needed to be implemented in a parallel computation system for online optimization.

Traditional signal control algorithm does not adopt multi-ring configuration, which prevents adaptive control consistently superior to dual-ring signal timing [19]. To bridge this gap, OPAC was reformulated to support dual-ring phase configuration [20]. Shelby [21] proposed a branch-and-bound search method to incorporate multi-ring phase configuration in signal control. Feng et al. [22] developed a bi-level model to enable COP to be compatible with NEMA. However, more complication was introduced since the lower level model enumerated all cases for phase allocation. In this study, we revised the principles of COP in a way to support NEMA without enumerating all phase combinations. Moreover, the planning horizon is shorten to integrate the proposed vehicle arrival estimation model.

The objective of this paper is to develop an improved adaptive control method for signalized intersections. Firstly, a microscopic model is proposed to estimate vehicle arrival at the end of the queue. The estimation method incorporates the moving queue dynamics, which were not considered by [23], and reveals the interaction of vehicle arrival, queue length, and possible signal control plans. The proposed control algorithm is based on DP and supports NEMA structure. The phase enumeration is removed so that the algorithm as in [16], [22] is simplified. Three separated optimization objectives involve minimization of delay, queue length and maximization of throughput. A realistic intersection is modeled to verify the proposed algorithm under various scenarios.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II proposes an estimation model to predict vehicular arrivals both in red and green durations. Then, a real-time control algorithm is formulated to perform signal optimization in Section III. Section IV presents a case study to evaluate the performance of the proposed method. Finally, conclusions and directions are summarized for further studies in Section V.

## II. Vehicle Arrival Estimation Model

In adaptive signal control, vehicle arrival and departure information is the major input for optimization. In return, any possible control decisions may affect traffic dynamics. Generally, vehicle arrival time is anticipated based on the information collected at upstream loop detectors or video cameras. If the queue is not completely discharged from stop line, vehicle travel distance is measured from the detector to the end of the last queued vehicle, regardless it is moving or not. Therefore, the maximum queue length, namely the distance from stop line to the last queued vehicle, is crucial for the arrival estimation. In this section, the vehicle arrival-departure process is modeled at a microscopic level by tracking vehicle trajectories and variance in maximum queue length. The proposed model is capable to estimate individual vehicle arrival time both in red and green time over the planning horizon.

## A. Vehicle Arrival During Red

The distance between detectors and stop line is assumed to be $D$. Vehicles cross the upstream detector with free flow speed $v_{f}$, and then reach the end of standing queue or the stop line based on estimated turning probability
[10]. For simplicity, the deceleration process is ignored. Travel time $t t_{k}$ and arrival time $t_{k}^{a}$ for vehicle $k$ are calculated as below:

$$
\begin{aligned}
t t_{k} & =\left(D-q_{k-1}^{\max }\right) / v_{f} \\
t_{k}^{a} & =t_{k}^{d}+t t_{k}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $q_{k-1}^{\text {max }}$ is the maximum queue length at the time vehicle $k-1$ joins the queue; $t_{k}^{d}$ is the time that the detected vehicle passes upstream detector. The initial maximum queue length $q_{0}^{\text {max }}=n_{0} * S, n_{0}$ is the number of queued vehicles on a single lane at the beginning of red time, $S$ is the space occupied by a queued vehicle.

Fig. 1 depicts the vehicle arrival process in red duration. Detected vehicles are projected from the upstream detector line to the end of queue. With vehicles joining the queue, the travel distance and travel time decrease as shown in Fig. 1. If the average vehicle spacing $S$ is 7m and the average free speed $v_{f}$ is $30 \mathrm{~km} / \mathrm{h}$, the travel time difference between two consecutive arriving vehicles during red time is approximated as 1 second. That is, once a vehicle arrives at the end of standing queue, travel time of the next vehicle decreases by 1s. The estimation process for red duration is summarized as Algorithm 1.

In Algorithm 1, $T_{\text {st }}$ and $T_{\text {end }}$ is the starting and ending time for red/green duration, denoting the vehicle arrival time range; $K$ is the number of detected vehicles. The set of vehicle detected time is required as input. Initially, some parameters should be set, including $t t_{1}, t_{1}^{a}$ and $A(l, t)$, which is the number of arrival vehicles at time step $t \in\left[T_{\mathrm{st}}, T_{\text {end }}\right]$ and lane $l$. If the estimated arrival time $t_{k}^{a}$ falls within the range $\left[T_{\text {st }}, T_{\text {end }}\right]$, then it is recorded and $A\left(l, t_{k}^{a}\right)=A\left(l, t_{k}^{a}\right)+1$. Meanwhile, the maximum queue length is updated for the next vehicle arrival estimation. Otherwise, Algorithm 1 is terminated.

## B. Vehicle Arrival During Green

During green time, queued vehicles do not start following preceding vehicles with $v_{q}\left(v_{q}<v_{f}\right)$ until the discharge wave passes through their stopping locations. Depending on whether the last queued vehicle starts moving or not, arrivals of the approaching vehicle were divided into two cases as shown in Fig. 2. Arrival Case 1: vehicle stops at the end of the standing queue before discharge wave stops propagating. Arrival Case 2: vehicle catches up the moving queue due to the speed gap between $v_{q}$ and $v_{f}$. The details of two cases are illustrated as below.

## III. Real-Time Signal Control Algorithm

In this section, a real-time control algorithm is proposed to determine the optimal phase sequence and duration based
on the estimated vehicle arrivals. The signal control algorithm is compatible with NEMA structure, which is more flexible compared with the traditional four-phase configuration. The objective functions include minimization of delay, queue and maximization of throughput. Signal controller performs the optimal timing on a finite horizon, which is discretized into 1s interval.

The proposed algorithm applies DP (Dynamic Programming) procedure and performs based on a rolling scheme. ## B. DP Formulation

The proposed DP algorithm is formulated in a forward and a backward recursion. The forward recursion calculates the optimal phase duration and the value of cumulative objective function. The backward recursion retrieves the optimal signal policy. Details of the forward and backward recursion are described below.

## 1) Forward Recursion

The purpose of forward recursion is to allocate phase durations to each stage in DP, as well as the optimal phase combination for groups 2 and 5. If a stage corresponds to the group with two phase combination candidates, there is no minimum green time constraint and $x_{j}^{\text {min }}=0$. For the other groups, the minimum green $x_{j}^{\text {min }}$ is set to be a fixed value (e.g. 10s). With the minimum green time constraint, the lower bound of state variable for stage $j$ is calculated with Eqs (7-8):

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_{j}^{\min } & = \begin{cases}s_{j-1}^{\min }+h\left(x_{j}^{\min }\right) & j>1 \\
\max \left\{x_{j}^{\min }-x_{e}, 0\right\} & j=1\end{cases} \\
h(x) & = \begin{cases}x+R & x>0 \\
0 & x=0\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $x_{e}$ is the elapsed green time in the previous horizon.

## C. Objective Function

For the look-ahead adaptive signal control, different objective functions generally result in different operational performance. Some objectives significantly outperform others under certain traffic demand and planning horizon. In this study, $f_{j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}, c_{j}\right)$ is formulated as sum of performance measurement across all lanes (Eqs. (10-12)), including total vehicle delay, queue length and throughput. Three objective functions are listed as follows and calculated separately within the simulation experiments:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \min \sum_{t=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}} \sum_{p=1}^{8} \sum_{l \in L_{p}} n_{l}^{q}(t) \\
& \min \sum_{p=1}^{8} \sum_{l \in L_{p}} n_{l}^{q}\left(s_{j}\right) \\
& \max \sum_{t=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}} \sum_{p=1}^{8} \sum_{l \in L_{p}} n_{l}^{d}(t) \\
n_{l}^{q}(t)= & n_{l}^{q}(t-1)-n_{l}^{d}(t)+n_{l}^{a}(t) \\
n_{l}^{d}(t)= & \min \left\{v, n_{l}^{q}(t-1)+n_{l}^{a}(t)\right\} \quad \forall l \in L_{p}, p \in c_{j} \mathrm{I} c_{j-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
n_{l}^{d}(t) & = \begin{cases}\min \left\{v, n_{l}^{q}(t-1)+n_{l}^{a}(t)\right\} & s_{j-1}+R<t \leq s_{j} \\
0 & s_{j-1} \leq t \leq s_{j-1}+R \\
\forall l & \in L_{p}, p \in c_{j} \backslash c_{j-1}\end{cases} \\
n_{l}^{d}(t) & =0 \quad \forall l \in L_{p}, p \notin c_{j} \\
n_{l}^{a}(t) & =A(l, t)
\end{aligned}
$$

Objective (10) is calculated as the summation of queue lengths of all movements from $s_{j-1}$ to $s_{j}$, because the delay is defined as the total time from a vehicle joining the queue to its departure from the stop line. Objective (11) considers the number of queued vehicles at end of the current stage $s_{j}$. Objective (12) maximizes vehicle throughput from all lanes, which is cumulative vehicle departures from $s_{j-1}$ to $s_{j}$. The time step in objective function is assumed to be 1 s.

As shown in Eq. (13), the number of queued vehicles at time step $t$ depends on the queue length at $t$-1, and the arrival and departure vehicles at time step $t$. The departure rate is calculated from Eqs. (14-16), in which $v$ is the average departure vehicle per time step, depending on the saturated headway at the stop line. If an active phase extends from one of the phases in the previous stage ( $p \in c_{j} \cap c_{j-1}$ ), vehicle departure continues from time step $s_{j-1}$ (Eq. (14)). Otherwise, phase change interval should be considered in Eq. (15). The number of departing vehicles is the smaller value between queue length and departure rate. For nonactive phases, no vehicles are discharged from stop line (Eq. (16)). For vehicle arrival, $A(l, t)$ is calculated based on Algorithms 1-2, by setting the lower bound and upper bound of prediction time interval, $T_{\text {st }}$ and $T_{\text {end }}$, as DP algorithm state value $s_{j-1}$ and $s_{j}$ respectively. Thus, the number of arriving vehicle for each time step is estimated.

## IV. Results and Discussions

## A. Experiment Set-Up

To validate the proposed adaptive control method, an isolated intersection of Jianchuan Road and Humin Road, in Shanghai, China, was used as a case study. Fig. 5 shows the intersection lane layout and the signal phase diagram, which is consistent with NEMA configuration as presented in Fig. 4. The length of left turning lanes are about 150 m. Specifically, the DP-D and DP-T achieves the best performance when the level of traffic demand is low, both producing over 20\% delay reduction compared with SYNCHRO. The figure also shows the 95\% confidence intervals of percentage changes in vehicular delay, which can be seen that the delay reduction is significant for most scenarios except DP-Q at low and medium demand level.

When the traffic demand is low, the performance of DP with and without NEMA phase configuration is nearly equal. It is expected because the advantages of NEMA control performance are not well reflected under the limited number of vehicles. Compared with the other two objectives, NEMA brings more benefits in minimizing total delay in relatively congested conditions, which means that DP-D with NEMA can better handle the unbalanced demands between opposite approaches. The statistical test shows that the significance of delay reduction is most evident in medium demand level.

To investigate the effects of planning horizon on control performance, the sensitivity test was introduced. Three control strategies were compared with each other under different planning horizons ranging from 20 s to 60 s at heavy traffic volume, as shown in TABLE IV. The results indicate that the DP-D performs better as planning horizon increases. It is because that long planning horizons enables DP-D to get a comprehensive evaluation on intersection and variations in queue length. However, more uncertainties in long planning horizon may degrade the control performance as shown in DP-Q and DP-T. Since DP-Q uses the instantaneous queue length as performance measurement, thus it requires more frequent estimations and updates, which consequently performs the best at 20-second planning horizon. Moreover, DP-T is not as sensitive to planning horizon as others. It is more likely to achieve delay reduction when planning horizon is close to the vehicle travel time from upstream detectors to stop lines, that is, 40 to 50 seconds.

## V. Conclusions

This paper proposed an improved adaptive control method for urban isolated signalized intersection. Traffic dynamics in red and green duration were modeled at microscopic level. Vehicular arrival was predicted by projecting vehicles from upstream detectors to the standing or moving queue tail in a space-time diagram. A control algorithm was developed to apply DP procedure with NEMA configuration. Three performance measurements, namely total vehicle delay, queue length and throughput were chosen as the optimization objective, individually. A real-world intersection was modeled in VISSIM to compare control performances under different optimization objective functions and control strategies.

The main contribution of this paper is to propose an improved adaptive signal control method without phase enumeration, in which vehicle arrivals at both stopping and moving queues are captured. The simulation results indicate that the proposed DP algorithm outperforms the optimal fixed timing in various demands and planning horizon scenarios. Minimizing total vehicle delay and maximizing throughput results in similar delay reduction compared with SYNCHRO. The benefits from DP-Q tend to be more obvious at a higher demand level. Meanwhile, NEMA phase structure improves the performance of adaptive signal control strategies with all objective functions. In terms of the planning horizon, DP-Q requires a shorter planning horizon to obtain promising delay reduction, compared with DP-D and DP-T, which is believed as more suitable for a compact road network.

In the proposed method, upstream vehicle detection information is critical for arrival estimation. In addition to loop detector or video camera, other emerging technologies, such as connected vehicle, may also be used for information collection as well. As a result, one direction of future work is to incorporate the connected vehicle technologies to improve the reliability and accuracy of arrival estimation. Furthermore, implicit coordination between intersections based on proposed method is another promising direction and worth further investigating.
