# A comparison between PARAMICS and VISSIM in estimating automated field-measured traffic conflicts at signalized intersections 

Mohamed Essa* and Tarek Sayed<br>Department of Civil Engineering, University of British Columbia, 6250 Applied Science Lane, Vancouver, British Columbia V6T 1Z4, Canada


#### Abstract

SUMMARY The main objective of this study is to investigate the relationship between field-measured conflicts and simulated conflicts estimated from microsimulation model (PARAMICS) using the surrogate safety assessment model. An urban signalized intersection was selected for analysis. Automated video-based computer vision techniques were used to identify field conflicts. The applicability of a two-step model calibration procedure applied to VISSIM in a recent study was investigated using PARAMICS. In the first calibration step, the PARAMICS model was calibrated to ensure that the simulation gives reasonable results of average delay times. The second calibration step used a genetic algorithm procedure to calibrate PARAMICS parameters to enhance the correlation between simulated and field-measured conflicts. Finally, the results obtained from PARAMICS were compared with results obtained from VISSIM. The comparison included three aspects: (i) the car-following model and safety-related parameters; (ii) the correlation between simulated and field-measured conflicts; and (iii) the conflict spatial distributions. The results show that the default simulation model parameters give poor correlation with the field-measured data, and therefore, using simulation models without a proper calibration should be avoided. Overall, good correlation between field-measured and simulated conflicts was obtained after calibration for both models, especially at higher timeto-collision (TTC) values. At TTC threshold of 1.5 s, PARAMICS overestimates the number of conflicts and VISSIM underestimates it. Both models overestimated the number of conflicts at TTC threshold of 3.00 s. There were major differences between field-measured and simulated conflicts spatial distributions for both simulation models. This indicates that despite the good correlation obtained from the calibration process, both PARAMICS and VISSIM do not capture the actual conflict occurrence mechanism. ## 1. INTRODUCTION

Road safety analysis has traditionally been conducted using historical collision data. This traditional approach may suffer from several shortcomings including (i) the limited availability and poor quality of collision data; (ii) the difficulty of identifying collision contributing factors and causes; and (iii) an ethical dilemma as it is required to observe an adequate number of collisions over a long time period to conduct a statistically valid safety analysis [1]. Therefore, the use of surrogate measures such as the traffic conflict technique has been advocated as a proactive and complementary approach to study road safety from a broader perspective than relying on collision-based analysis. This interest has been aided by recent advances in technologies that can be used to analyze traffic conflicts. These technologies include the use of computer vision techniques to analyze video data and identify conflicts and the use of microsimulation models for the safety assessment of road facilities by estimating conflict indicators from simulated vehicle trajectories. The main advantages of the microsimulation approach are

[^0](i) the ability to evaluate the safety of various design alternatives of road facilities before actually applying changes and (ii) the ease of estimating simulated conflicts without actually observing them.

The surrogate safety assessment model (SSAM) was recently developed to estimate traffic conflicts using simulated vehicle trajectories exported from four commonly used microscopic simulation models: VISSIM, AIMSUN, PARAMICS, and TEXAS. Several traffic conflict indicators can be calculated including the time to collision (TTC), post-encroachment time (PET), deceleration rate (DR), maximum speed (MaxS), and speed differential (DeltaS). In SSAM, conflicts are classified into three maneuver types: rear end, lane change, and crossing. Conflicts are identified based on conflict angle and specific thresholds for TTC and PET, which are predetermined by the user [2].

The SSAM approach has seen increasing use in safety evaluations recently ([3, 4], among others). However, several researchers raised two main concerns about using simulated conflicts in safety evaluation [5, 6]. First, traffic simulation models have specific rules that are aimed at avoiding vehicles collision. Therefore, it is very difficult to represent unsafe vehicle interactions and near misses. Second, traffic simulation models usually have many model parameters and several ways to model traffic (traffic arrival, priority rules, conflict areas, road geometry, etc.), and this can have a significant impact on the estimated simulated conflicts. Therefore, the results can vary significantly depending on the way used in modeling and the selected values of the model parameters.

A recent study [7] investigated the relationship between field-measured and simulated rear-end conflicts using microsimulation model (VISSIM) and the SSAM tool at a signalized intersection. Automated video-based computer vision techniques were used to extract vehicle trajectories and identify field-measured rear-end conflicts. A two-step calibration procedure was proposed to enhance the correlation between field-measured and VISSIM simulated rear-end conflicts. Good correlation between simulated and field-measured conflicts was obtained after the two-step calibration procedure, especially at higher TTC values. However, the spatial distribution of simulated and field-measured conflicts showed major differences that may indicate that VISSIM failed to capture the real vehicles interaction (conflict) mechanism [7].

The SSAM approach can be used with trajectories extracted from several traffic microsimulation models. The main objective of this study is to investigate the relationship between field-measured and simulated conflicts at signalized intersections using another microsimulation model (PARAMICS). Field-measured data were obtained from the urban signalized intersection analyzed in [7]. The applicability of the two-step calibration procedure applied to VISSIM in [7] was investigated and validated using PARAMICS. In the first calibration step, the PARAMICS model was calibrated to ensure that the simulation gives reasonable results of hourly average delay times. Then, in the second calibration step, the genetic algorithm (GA) procedure was used to calibrate PARAMICS parameters to enhance the correlation between simulated and field-measured conflicts. The results obtained from PARAMICS in this study were compared with those obtained from VISSIM. The comparison included three aspects: (i) car-following model and safety-related parameters; (ii) correlation between simulated and field-measured conflicts; and (iii) conflict spatial distribution.

This paper is organized in two main parts. In the first part, PARAMICS simulation model will be calibrated and validated. In the second part, the results obtained from PARAMICS will be compared with those obtained from VISSIM in [7].

## 2. PREVIOUS WORK

Many previous studies have examined the calibration and validation of microsimulation models for traffic safety evaluations. Gettman et al. [2] applied theoretical and field validation to assess the use of SSAM. In the theoretical validation, the relative safety of pairs of design alternatives was evaluated in 11 case studies that include intersections and interchanges. The results found that SSAM could recognize statistically significant differences in the total number of conflicts, the number of conflicts by type, and conflict severity indicators for both design alternatives under equivalent traffic conditions. In the field validation, 83 signalized intersections were modeled in VISSIM and assessed with SSAM.

The simulated conflicts of these intersections were compared with the actual crash data using five statistical tests. The results found a statistically significant correlation between the simulation conflicts data provided by SSAM and the field crash data. However, this correlation may be expected because of both simulated conflicts and crashes being highly correlated with traffic volume. As well, a significant correlation between the ranking of intersections with abnormally high conflicts and with abnormally high crashes was not found. Therefore, the validation results were considered inconclusive, and further research was recommended to improve driver behavior in simulation models, develop a composite 'safety index', study the nature of conflicts in real-world data, and collect adequate vehicle trajectory datasets [2].

Dijkstra et al. [8] used PARAMICS to model the traffic of 569 junctions in the Netherlands. The relationship between observed crashes and simulated conflicts was assessed by using generalized linear models. It was found that the number of conflicts at junctions and the number of passing motor vehicles are statistically related to the number of observed crashes [8].

Cunto and Saccomanno [9] presented a systematic four-step calibration procedure for calibrating a microscopic simulation model at signalized intersections based on a safety performance measure (crash potential index) that relates DR required to avoid a crash to the maximum available DR of vehicles. The real trajectories of vehicles extracted from Next Generation Simulation (NGSIM) administered by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) and the microscopic traffic algorithms were obtained from VISSIM to simulate safety performance. The model results were validated, and their transferability was investigated, using an independent sample of vehicle tracking data [9]. Duong et al. [10] proposed a multi-criteria calibration procedure for parameters calibration of microscopic traffic simulation platforms. The multi-criteria procedure considered the underlying traffic attributes (i.e., volume, speed, and density) and provided reasonable estimates of safety performance for the real trajectories dataset from NGSIM (FHWA) [10].

Sobhani et al. [11] used VISSIM to develop a simulation-based modeling approach to assess the safety performance of road locations. The developed framework consists of two main components. The first component was to estimate the number and the severity of conflicts using microsimulation models. The second component was to measure the potential injury severity of each simulated conflict [11].

Fan et al. [12] used VISSIM and SSAM for estimating field-measured traffic conflicts at freeway merge areas. Field conflicts were collected manually and compared with simulated conflicts. A twostep calibration procedure was proposed to calibrate and validate the VISSIM simulation models. It was found that the two-stage calibration procedure reduced the mean absolute percent error (MAPE) for rear-end and lane-change conflicts. The results showed that there was a reasonable consistency between the simulated and the observed conflicts [12]. Huang et al. [13] compared the simulated conflicts generated by the VISSIM and SSAM to manually collected field conflicts at 10 signalized intersections. A two-stage calibration procedure was proposed to improve the consistency between the simulated and the observed conflicts [13]. In the last two studies, the SSAM TTC threshold that is used to identify rear-end conflicts was used as a calibration parameter. Therefore, the calibration process aimed at finding a TTC threshold that would minimize the MAPE between the number of the simulated and field-measured conflicts.

So et al. [14] used an integrated simulation environment to assess the safety impact of the connected vehicles-based driver warning systems. This integrated simulation approach consists of two parts: real-time simulation approach and post-processing approach. In the real-time simulation environment, a driver warning simulator, the GPS simulator, and communication delays simulators were developed and then integrated with VISSIM. The vehicle trajectories produced from this real-time simulation were post-processed by the vehicle dynamics model to generate the vehicle dynamics-based trajectories. Finally, SSAM estimated traffic conflicts using the vehicle dynamics-based trajectories [14].

Stevanovic et al. [15] presented a new method for signal timing optimization through a tradeoff between mobility, safety, and the environment. The method integrated the traffic simulation model (VISSIM), surrogate safety assessment (SSAM), fuel consumption and emission modeling, and signal timing optimization tool [15].

Other studies presented a detailed calibration of simulation models based on the field observed data for freeway segments using PARAMICS [16] and VISSIM [17].

The calibration process includes the annotation of features in the camera image and in an aerial, orthographic image of the intersection. Google Satellite image was used in this study as shown in Figure 1. Four types of annotations were used for camera calibration optimization:

- Corresponding points: distinguishable points are identified and annotated in both the camera image and orthographic image.
- Distances: the known real-world distance between two points in the camera image, both situated on the road surface plane.
- Angles: known angles between parallel lines of lane markings or the right angle lines at the intersections.
- Global up directions: by specifying the locations in the camera image of the tops and bottoms of poles, the calibration optimization algorithm can determine the tilt of the road surface plane (it is assumed that poles are correctly mounted and are perpendicular to the road surface).

The calibration error was represented by the discrepancy between calculated and annotated segment lengths normalized by the length of each segment. The accuracy of the final estimates was very small, and no further error in conflict analysis was attributed to inaccurately estimated camera parameters.
4.1.2. Feature tracking
The automated video analysis relies on computer algorithms to differentiate between features of road users and features that are part of the environment. Features are identified and tracked using an
implementation of the well-known Kanade-Lucas-Tomasi feature tracker algorithm. Tracked features are further refined through the addition of filters. First, features that remain stationary are assumed to belong to the environment and are discarded and not tracked [19]. For this reason, features must be continually generated in order to identify moving features that may subsequently be tracked. Second, feature tracker errors are dealt with by enforcing regularity motion checks to remove features showing unreasonable acceleration or sudden changes in direction. These features are displaying motion properties that are not physically possible of road users and can safely be classified as tracking errors.

### 4.1.3. Feature grouping (objects creation)

In feature grouping, features that move at similar speed and satisfy other spatial proximity and common motion constraints are grouped to create objects where the trajectories of these objects are recorded. The tracking accuracy for this approach has been tested and found to be about 90\%, which is considered reliable [26], and should have little impact on the accuracy of the estimation of conflict indicators.

### 4.1.4. Prototype generation and matching

Prototypes refer to a group of motion patterns that define the set of movements carried out by road users in the video scene. The road user movements are matched to those prototypes, and trajectories that show potential conflicts are identified. The trajectory of an object is matched to individual prototypes from the full set of prototypes using a longest common subsequence algorithm. This provides a set of predicted future positions with associated probabilities of occurrence.

### 4.1.5. Conflicts analysis

Conflicts between vehicles can then be determined by evaluating if any of these future positions coincide spatially and temporally with other vehicles [22]. Every pair of road users that share temporal and spatial proximity has the potential for a conflict. Two vehicles that appear at the same time, both inside the field of view of the camera, are defined as having spatial and temporal proximity, and potential conflicts between them calculated. A critical value of a conflict indicator must be drawn from each interaction. Typically, the most severe value is used to represent the overall severity of a traffic event.

The conflict analysis involves the calculation of conflict indicators such as the TTC conflict. The TTC is defined as 'the time required for two vehicles to collide if they continue at their present speeds and on the same path' [27]. TTC is continually calculated between conflicting vehicles, and thus a set of values is returned for each discrete conflict. One representative value, the minimum TTC, is extracted from this set to indicate the maximum severity of this interaction. The minimum TTC from all conflicting vehicles and the total number of events with minimum TTC less than 3 s were recorded.

Using the minimum TTC of each conflict, the hourly number of field-measured rear-end conflicts was determined for 60 h (30 h for calibration dataset and 30 h for validation dataset) at different 21 TTC thresholds. Thresholds of TTC ranged from 1 to 3 s in 0.1-s increments. As well, conflict locations were determined using the coordinates $(x, y)$ of the critical event position (minimum TTC) [7]. The main reason behind using different TTC thresholds is that the correlation between the simulated and the fieldmeasured conflicts varies with the TTC threshold. Generally, for a higher TTC threshold, there is a higher dependency on exposure (any two vehicles existing at the same time and potentially interacting will be counted as a conflict) [7, 28]. Therefore, the use of high TTC values will likely result in a high correlation that does not really reflect the safety level but more reflecting exposure. One of the main advantages of using computer vision video analysis is the ability to accurately identify conflicts with their TTC values. This cannot be carried out when conflicts are observed manually. Thus, the calibration process in this study is aimed to find the highest possible correlation at different TTC thresholds to express the effect of the calibration process on the estimated number of the simulated rear-end conflicts at different severity levels (TTC threshold is assumed to represent conflict severity level).

### 4.2. PARAMICS platform

The microsimulation model PARAMICS was used in this study to simulate the selected intersection. PARAMICS depends on a psycho-physical car-following model based on the Fritzsche model [29],
which assumes that the driver can have one of five driving modes: Following-I, Following-II, Danger, Closing In, and Driving Freely. These modes are determined using six thresholds: Perceptionthreshold Negative, Perception-threshold Positive, Desired Distance, Risky Distance, Safe Distance, and Braking Distance [29, 30].

In this study, in order to assess the intersections safety using simulation, the selected intersection has to be modeled accurately to match actual field conditions using PARAMICS Modeller. Modeller is the core network building post-simulation tool within the PARAMICS suite of microsimulation tools [31]. First, the geometry of the intersection was extracted from aerial photo and then drawn in technical drawing. Once the geometry technical drawing was defined in the PARAMICS Modeller, all junctions, links, and zones were set up to match the real conditions. For each hour, the detailed traffic count extracted from videos was defined in PARAMICS using demand editor [32] to represent all movements (i.e., through, left, and right) and traffic composition (i.e., percentage of passenger cars, trucks, and buses). Public transit was defined in the PARAMICS model using the real number of public transit buses extracted from videos. Actual traffic signal settings were defined in PARAMICS for each hour using signalized junction with type actuated signal controller (ASC) [32, 33]. In the ASC editor, each phase has minimum green time, maximum green time, yellow time, and all red time. To represent the protected-permissive left turns, a detector was defined at each one of the four left-turn lanes. In each signal cycle, if the detector is occupied, a protected left-turn phase will be called by ASC; otherwise, the left turn will be permissive. As well, to avoid abnormal movements at junctions when vehicles move from one link to another, next lanes were defined at each junction. In addition, some restriction rules were defined in the core network attributes and added to lane's properties to make widening for left turns without abnormal lane changes.

Finally, visual inspection was carried out to check the simulation and to ensure that there are no abnormal movements of the simulated vehicles. Analyzer files were exported from PARAMICS Modeller and processed in PARAMICS Analyser [34] to estimate the hourly average delay results for each approach. Trajectory files (*.trj files) were exported from PARAMICS Modeller and processed in SSAM to estimate the simulated rear-end conflicts at the 21 thresholds of TTC for the same 60 h (30 h for calibration dataset and 30 h for validation dataset) pre-estimated using the automated video analysis. The results of the average delay and the simulated rear-end conflicts will be discussed later in this paper.

### 4.3. PARAMICS calibration procedure

The two-step calibration procedure proposed in [7] was applied to PARAMICS to enhance the correlation between the simulated and the field-measured rear-end conflicts using the calibration dataset. In the first calibration step, the PARAMICS model was calibrated to ensure that the simulation gives reasonable results in terms of average delay times. In the second calibration step, the PARAMICS driving behavior parameters were calibrated to enhance the correlation between the simulated and the fieldmeasured rear-end conflicts.

### 4.3.1. First-step calibration

The main goal of this step is to match the simulated average delay times with the field-measured average delay times. Other measures such as the number of stops, queue length, or a combination of these measures can be used as a target of this calibration step. However, optimizing several measures at the same time can be difficult. Therefore, the average delay per vehicle was selected for optimization as it is the primary measure in evaluating the Level of Service (LOS) for signalized intersections [35].

In order to calibrate the average delay time, the arrival type was calibrated to match the field conditions [7]. The arrival type for each lane group describes the quality of the progression, which is a critical characteristic that must be quantified for the analysis of an urban street or signalized intersection. In order to quantify the arrival type, the platoon ratio needs to be determined. The platoon ratio depends on the proportion of all vehicles arriving during green and the ratio of effective green time for the lane group to the cycle length [7, 35]. Based on the real signal time analysis and field proportion of vehicles arriving during green, which were extracted from the video recordings, the platoon ratio
and the arrival type were determined for each hour as an average. The PARAMICS model was calibrated to produce similar arrival type for each approach for each hour.

To match the field arrival type using PARAMICS Modeller, a dummy signalized junction was added before the four approaches of the analyzed intersection. Each dummy signalized junction has offset time $(\Delta t)$ from the simulation time. By changing the offset time, the percentage of vehicles $(P)$ arriving on green time of the analyzed intersection can be changed. The offset time, which gives a value for $P$ that matches the field condition, was obtained for each approach each hour. As well, a bypass at each approach was added to represent the percentage of vehicles ( $X \%$ ) arriving out of platoon. The percentage $X$ was measured from the field data as average for each hour. The percentage $X$ was defined in PARAMICS by defining two routes: the bypass route that has $X \%$ of the vehicles and the dummy signalized junction route that has $(1-X) \%$ of vehicles. Figure 3 illustrates the process of arrival type calibration.

After the first-step calibration, the average delay and the simulated rear-end conflicts results were estimated for the calibration dataset. Simulated rear-end conflicts were estimated at the 21 thresholds of TTC. The correlation between the field-measured and the simulated conflicts at all TTC thresholds was determined, and the results will be discussed later in this paper.

### 4.3.2. Second-step calibration

The main purpose of this step is to enhance the correlation between field-measured and simulated conflicts by calibrating PARAMICS parameters. Three car-following-model parameters were selected: mean target headway (MTH), mean driver reaction time (MRT), and minimum gap (G). The three parameters affect the desired safety distance between each couple of consecutive vehicles in PARAMICS and can be considered as the safety-related parameters. More description of the selected parameters can be found in [31]. The definition of the selected parameters will be discussed later in this paper in Table I.

Subsequently, a GA [36] technique was applied to estimate the best values of these parameters, which give the highest correlation between simulated and field-measured rear-end conflicts at different TTC thresholds. The range of each PARAMICS parameter was established to be a set of discrete numbers instead of continuous range to simplify and accelerate the optimization process. The range of each parameter was determined based on previous studies [37, 38]. Typically, the initial population is created randomly, which means that all solutions have equal chances to fall into the population. However, this random selection does not guarantee the uniform covering of each parameter space. Rather, Latin hypercube sampling method was used in this study to select the initial population. The first population in GA contained 10 individuals, each of them represents one solution (three values: one value for each PARAMICS parameter). The fitness value, used to evaluate each solution, was the reciprocal of the summation of the correlations between the simulated and the field-measured rear-end conflicts at different TTC thresholds. ### 4.4. Validation of the proposed procedure

The validation dataset (north and south approaches that were not included in the calibration) was used to validate the two-step calibration procedure. The two-step proposed procedure was applied on the PARAMICS Modeller of the validation dataset. In the first step, arrival type in the PARAMICS Modeller was calibrated to match the field conditions, and the hourly average delay results were estimated. In the second step, instead of the default values, the calibrated values of the three calibration parameters, which were obtained from the calibration dataset, were used in the PARAMICS Modeller. After each step, trajectory files were exported and processed in SSAM to estimate the simulated conflicts. The correlation between the simulated and the field-measured rear-end conflicts for the validation dataset was determined at all TTC thresholds, and the results will be discussed later in this paper.

## 5. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONS

### 5.1. Calibration dataset

For the calibration dataset, the simulated hourly average delay time values were estimated for both default PARAMICS and PARAMICS after the first calibration step. Figure 4 shows the average hourly delay and the platoon ratios (arrival types) for the calibration dataset for three cases: field-measured, default PARAMICS, and PARAMICS after first calibration step. The results show that the default simulation model has platoon ratios ranged from 0.46 to 1.06 while the field-measured platoon ratios ranged from 0.53 to 1.46. After the first calibration, the MAPE of the average delay time was reduced from $30.20 \%$ to $7.60 \%$. This shows the importance of the arrival type calibration at signalized intersections when estimating average delay time using microsimulation models.

Simulated rear-end conflicts were estimated at the 21 thresholds of TTC before and after the first calibration step. The correlation between the field-measured and the simulated conflicts at all TTC thresholds was determined for default PARAMICS and PARAMICS after first calibration step. Figure 5 shows that the correlation between simulated and field-measured conflicts has increased at all TTC thresholds after the first calibration. For example, at TTC of 1.5 s, the correlation $(r)$ increased from 0.20 (default) to 0.55 (after first calibration).

To enhance the correlation between simulated and field-measured conflicts, the second calibration step was applied. After 40 generations of the applied GA, the second calibration step ended up with the best values of the three PARAMICS parameters as follows: 0.8 s for MTH, 1.6s for MRT, and 1.50 m for G. Some previous studies mentioned a calibrated value around 0.5 s for the mean reaction time [16, 43]. However, these studies were related to calibration of PARAMICS using a freeway segment. The mean reaction time usually varies between freeways and signalized intersections. Previous studies showed higher values of the mean reaction time at signalized intersections. For example, Wortman et al. [44] showed that the observed mean perception-reaction time was approximately 1.3 s while the 85th percentile times ranged from 1.5 to 2.1 s at signalized intersections [44].

The PARAMICS simulation model results obtained in this paper will be compared with the results obtained from [7], in which the same signalized intersection was analyzed using the VISSIM simulation model.

### 6.3. Conflict spatial distribution

For more investigation of the relationship between simulated and field-measured rear-end conflicts beyond the correlation, the spatial distributions of conflicts were compared. The spatial distribution of the rear-end conflicts was shown using heat maps for field-measured conflicts, PARAMICS-simulated conflicts, and VISSIM-simulated conflicts. Figures 10 and 11 show the heat maps of the rear-end conflicts for the calibration dataset and the validation dataset, respectively. The heat maps show that there are major differences between field-measured and simulated conflict locations for the approaches for both simulation models. For the field-measured conflicts, the heat maps show hot spots concentrated in specific locations. For example, as shown in Figure 10, sections 1 and 3 have high intensities of field conflicts near the stop line of the signal head. On the other hand, the simulated conflicts have hot spots distributed on a larger area, which varies in its location between VISSIM and PARAMICS. The major differences in conflict locations indicate that despite the good correlation obtained from the calibration process, both PARAMICS and VISSIM may have failed to well capture the actual conflict occurrence mechanism. One explanation for the correlation between the simulated and the real conflicts is the relationship between conflicts and exposure (represented in traffic volumes and vehicles interaction).

## 7. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

This study investigated the relationship between field-measured conflicts and simulated conflicts from the microsimulation model (PARAMICS) using the SSAM tool. Automated video-based computer vision techniques were used to extract vehicle trajectories and identify conflicts on all four approaches of an urban intersection in the city of Surrey. Field-measured rear-end conflicts were compared with simulated conflicts from PARAMICS using the SSAM tool. The applicability of the two-step calibration procedure applied to VISSIM in a recent study [7] was investigated and validated using PARAMICS. Finally, the results obtained from PARAMICS in this study were compared with those obtained from VISSIM.

For both simulation models, PARAMICS and VISSIM, the results show that default simulation model gives poor correlation with the field-measured data, and therefore, using simulation models without a proper calibration should be avoided. The proposed two steps of simulation model calibration are very important. The exposure effect is likely included in the first calibration step that controls the vehicle arrival rate and therefore will impact the opportunity for vehicle interactions (exposure) at signalized intersections. On the other hand, the second calibration step would mainly affect vehicle behavior by controlling the values of the safety-related parameters in the simulation model. Also, it is shown that the higher the TTC threshold, the higher the correlation between field-measured and simulated conflicts. This may be explained by the fact that, for a higher TTC threshold, there is a higher dependency on the exposure represented in traffic volume.

Linear regression results show that the coefficient of correlation $\left(R^{2}\right)$ of the linear model between simulated and field-measured conflicts increased significantly after the two steps of calibration for both simulation models. Although PARAMICS and VISSIM give close correlations after the second calibration step, the results at TTC threshold of 1.50 s show that PARAMICS overestimates the number of conflicts while VISSIM underestimates them. In contrast, the results of linear regression at the

TTC threshold of 3.00 s show that both simulation models overestimate the number of conflicts that is likely because of the stronger relation with exposure at high TTC value (3 s).

The conflict spatial distributions, represented by the heat maps, show that there are major differences between field-measured and simulated conflict locations for all approaches for both simulation models. The field conflicts were shown to be concentrated in some specific locations (such as before the stop line or before the start of left-turn lane), while the simulated conflicts were shown to be distributed over a larger area. Again, this indicates that despite the good correlation obtained from the calibration process, both PARAMICS and VISSIM failed to capture the actual conflict occurrence mechanism well.

Therefore, while the results show reasonable correlation between simulated and field-measured rearend conflicts, especially after proper calibration, more work is still needed to confirm that simulated conflicts provide safety measures beyond what can be expected from exposure. Finally, more work is still needed to investigate the relationship between collisions and both field-measured and simulated conflicts [48, 49].

This study has some limitations. The analysis included only one signalized intersection. Therefore, it is recommended that a larger study with several intersections be conducted for more comprehensive research. As well, this study includes only one conflict type (rear end), one conflict indicator (TTC), and one road facility (signalized intersections). For future research, the study should include more conflict types (e.g., right angle and left turn) and use other conflict indicators (e.g., PET and DeltaS). Also, other road facilities (e.g., un-signalized intersections and freeways) should be investigated.
