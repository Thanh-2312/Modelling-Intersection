# Travel Time Comparisons Between Seven Unconventional Arterial Intersection Designs 

Jonathan D. Reid and Joseph E. Hummer

Signalized intersections on high-volume arterials are often congested during peak periods, causing a decrease in through movement efficiency on the arterial. Much of the vehicle delay incurred at conventional arterial intersections is caused by high left-turn demand. Unconventional intersection designs attempt to reduce intersection delay and travel times by rerouting left turns away from the main intersection. Seven unconventional designs-the quadrant roadway intersection, median U-turn, superstreet median, bowtie, jughandle, split intersection, and continuous flow intersection designs-that could apply to a wide range of standard, four-leg intersections are compared. Previous comparisons of intersection delay and travel time between conventional designs and these unconventional designs have been piecemeal and have largely used hypothetical volumes. Simulation experiments were conducted using turning movement data from seven existing intersections of varying sizes to compare the travel time of conventional and unconventional designs fairly. Optimum cycle lengths were used for each design, and a number of factors were held constant to keep the comparisons fair. Off-peak, peak, and peak-plus15-percent volume levels were examined. The results from the simulations showed that at each intersection one or more unconventional designs had lower total travel times than the conventional design. Whereas most of the unconventional designs showed improvement in one or more scenarios, the quadrant roadway intersection and the median U-turn designs consistently produced the lowest travel times. When considering the design of high-volume intersections like those tested, engineers should seriously consider quadrant roadway intersection and median U-turn designs where rights-of-way are available.

Signalized intersections on high-volume arterials are often congested during peak periods, which causes a decrease in through movement efficiency on the arterial. Much of the vehicle delay incurred at conventional arterial intersections is caused by left-turn demand. High left-turn demand at major intersections and high arterial design speeds justify protected left-turn phasing. Protected leftturn phases add more phases to the signal cycle, increase lost time between phases, and reduce the available green time for through movement phases.

Unconventional arterial intersection designs attempt to reduce intersection delay and travel times by diverting left turns from crossing through the main intersection, therefore reducing the number of signal phases. The authors know of 13 different unconventional intersection designs that have been or may be applied to reduce intersection delay and travel times. In this paper, the designs of interest were designs that were both at-grade and could apply to a wide range

[^0]of suburban arterial intersections with four approaches. These criteria reduced the menu of possibilities to seven unconventional designs: the median U-turn, superstreet median, quadrant roadway intersection, bowtie, jughandle, split intersection, and continuous flow intersection designs.

Most of the seven unconventional designs listed have been constructed somewhere in the United States, and two of the designs- the median U-turn and the jughandle designs-are common designs in some states. A review of papers written on these designs showed that previous comparisons of intersection delay and travel time among unconventional designs have been piecemeal, typically comparing a single unconventional alternative against the conventional arterial intersection design. Further, the previous research efforts have all assumed different conditions and in some cases have based comparisons on very weak "straw man" conventional designs. Most of the travel time comparisons in past research have used hypothetical volumes-often with identical intersection approach volumes that are not realistic. Together, these observations leave questions among practitioners as to which unconventional designs would really lead to travel timesavings over a conventional design at a real intersection application.

The purpose of this research was to conduct fair travel time comparisons of conventional and seven unconventional designs using data from actual intersections. The authors wanted side-by-side comparisons of a conventional design and all seven unconventional designs over a range of intersection sizes and volume levels. Comparisons at different volume levels are also an important part of this research because designers are unlikely to trade small peak travel timesavings for huge off-peak travel time penalties. The outcome from these comparisons will provide engineers and designers with more firm guidance on which unconventional alternatives they should consider.

Several limits were imposed on the scope of this research. The most important restriction was that this paper examined only single (isolated) arterial intersections, not corridors. Some unconventional designs gain great efficiency from progression along an arterial corridor by their reduction in signal phases. Another limit of scope was the exclusive focus on travel time and other traffic engineering related results. Safety, driver expectations, cost, and many other factors feed into the decision making behind an intersection design, but this paper focuses on only travel time and related operational measures.

## Median U Turn

The median U-turn design, illustrated in Figure 2, is the most common unconventional intersection in the United States today. The

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/4a8fb569-351c-4bbd-a366-998897d6b095-02.jpg?height=748&width=846&top_left_y=1727&top_left_x=180)
FIGURE 2 Median U-turn intersection design.

median U turn removes all left-turn movements at the main intersection by diverting left-turning vehicles onto one-way crossovers designed into the median. Left-turning vehicles from the arterial pass through the intersection twice (once as a through movement, then as a right movement), and collector lefts first turn right before turning left.

Removing all left turns at the main intersection creates two-phase signal operations at all intersections, eliminating two lost time periods per cycle. Boone and Hummer (3), Dorothy et al. (4), and Reid and Hummer (5) have all analyzed the travel efficiency of the median U-turn intersection and found volume combinations where it reduced intersection travel time and delay compared with the conventional design.

## Superstreet Median

The superstreet median design, illustrated in Figure 3, is similar to the median U-turn design, but it features a break in cross street traffic that allows the signals on opposite directions of the arterial to operate independently. Therefore, the signals in both direction of travel on the arterial can be progressed perfectly, even at differing cycle lengths, at any collector spacing. Reid and Hummer have examined travel time in a superstreet median arterial corridor compared with conventional and median U-turn designs and found improved travel times in some scenarios compared with the conventional design; however, it rarely faired better than the median U-turn design (5).

## Split Intersection

The split intersection, illustrated in Figure 6, separates the collector into one-way pairs with signals at both intersections with the arterial. These signals have three phases that operate similarly to diamond interchange ramp intersections. The split intersection may serve as an intermediate step toward creating an interchange. The separation distance between split roadways should be 60 to 90 m (200 to 300 ft) to prevent spillback. Polus and Cohen (7) and Bared and Kaisar (8) have both conducted a series of simulations comparing split intersections to conventional designs. Their results show that the split intersection has great efficiency potential.

## Continuous Flow Intersection

The continuous flow intersection, illustrated in Figure 7, features a short roadway segment to the left and upstream of the main intersection to handle left-turning traffic. The major innovation of this design is that through and left-turning traffic move simultaneously at the main intersection. This allows, in effect, protected left turns with a two-phase signal.

## Volume Levels Modeled

From the turning movement count data, three volume levels were selected for traffic analysis: a midday (off-peak) period, the afternoon peak period, and a volume 15 percent greater than the afternoon peak period. Off-peak volumes are of interest because some unconventional alternatives are perceived to worsen off-peak efficiency, and engineers should be aware of this potential before designing these intersections. The afternoon peak period was chosen to compare design alternatives near or at capacity. The third volume level presumably looks ahead to intersection volumes that will be typical 3 to 5 years into the future.

Table 2 summarizes the turning movement volumes for the afternoon peak hour for each intersection. Readers should use Table 2 to gain an idea of how an intersection of interest compares with those used in this analysis.

## Construction of the Traffic Models

The researchers used the CORSIM microscopic simulation program to obtain travel time and delay measures for the desired comparisons. CORSIM is currently the best model choice for this type of detailed traffic engineering research for several reasons: the model output includes detailed traffic measures of effectiveness, it can model all designs of interest, it has been validated for unconventional designs (11), it provides detailed animation for visual inspection of results, and it is supported by FHWA.

To maintain consistency between designs during the experiment, several intersection parameters were kept constant, including basic number of lanes, signal timing parameters, design speeds, turn bay lengths, and driver characteristics. CORSIM simulation models were constructed to create equitable comparison of the conventional and unconventional intersection operations. The model volume input was kept constant throughout the alternatives using the interchange feature in the CORSIM model. When interchange travel patterns are specified, CORSIM uses the information to determine the turnmovement decisions (and, therefore, lane-changing decision) of each vehicle within the interchange on entry into the network (12). Also, in an effort to not penalize the conventional intersection that exists in the field today, the design analyzed in the experiment assumed a full build-out of the conventional design, including dual left-turn lanes for all approaches (except for the two-lane collector alternatives) and uniform bay lengths. Table 3 provides design details for two-lane and four-lane collectors. Other uniform design assumptions included the following:

- All left- and right-turn bays were kept at a uniform length- long enough to provide adequate storage for intersections operating near or at capacity.
- Yellow and all red signal timings were 4 and 2 s, respectively, except where these needed to be lengthened to clear vehicles through closely spaced signals.
- Each alternative allowed right turns on red at most locations.
- Left turns on red onto a one-way street were allowed where the designs permitted despite the current prohibition of such movements in some states.
- Truck percentages were assumed to be 3 percent on arterials and 2 percent on collectors.
- Travel speeds assigned to the arterial and collector were 80 km/h and 65 km/h (50 and 40 mph), respectively, in all intersection models.

Where direct left turns were allowed, the authors used leading, protected left-turn signals, which are consistent with general signal timing practice for high-volume corridors. Right-turn overlaps were included based on the estimated lengths of the opposing left-turn phases. Optimized phase splits for each design at each intersection were determined using a spreadsheet based on volumes. The unconventional designs typically included one critical intersection that dictated green times and offsets at other signalized intersections. Four cycle lengths were analyzed for each alternative: 90, 120, 150, and 180 s. Each of the four cycle lengths was run once in CORSIM for each design at each intersection during each time period. The cycle length that produced the best (lowest) system time results was deemed the optimum cycle length, and a second CORSIM run (with a different driver characteristic random number seed) was completed for each design at each intersection during each time period using that optimum cycle length.
The results focused on total system travel time rather than intersection delays or similar measures. Unconventional designs can look very good at the main intersection while imposing large travel time penalties on left-turning vehicles away from the main intersection [readers should beware of Highway Capacity Manual (13) comparisons of unconventional designs for this same reason]. Analyzing systemwide travel time inside a boundary constant between designs kept the comparisons fair.

## RESULTS

Key CORSIM results from each of the seven experiments are presented in Tables 4 though 8. For the most important measures of effectiveness-total system time and percent stops-results are reported for each of the three time periods and for an average of all three time periods. The tables also show the average optimum cycle length used, the average vehicle miles, the average number of trips, and the average moving time to total system time ratio from the CORSIM output.

The researchers analyzed the total time and percent stops for each intersection using analysis of variance. The analysis of variance results showed that the main effect of design and the interaction between design and volume level were statistically significant at the 99.99 percent level for total system time in all seven experiments. In the analysis of percent stops, the main effect of design was statistically significant at the 99.99 percent level in all seven experiments, and the interaction between design and volume level was statistically significant at the 99.99 percent level in five of the seven experiments (in all but Intersections 5 and 6, where it was significant at the 95 percent level). The researchers also examined the effect of the design factor on total system time and percent stops using Duncan's and Tukey's means tests. The results of those means tests are described as follows for each intersection.

Table 4 shows the results of intersections of a four-lane arterial meeting a four-lane cross street. At Intersection 1, the quadrant roadway intersection design had the lowest average total time, which was significantly lower at the 95 percent level than the second-best designs. The average total time values for the median U-turn and the continuous flow intersection designs were not significantly different from each other. In the next tier, the average total times for the conventional and split intersection designs were not significantly different from each other. Note that the split intersection had the lowest total time with off-peak volumes, but the quadrant design was superior in total time for the higher volumes. In a pattern that will repeat itself at the other intersections, the conventional design led to the fewest stops, the quadrant and median U-turn designs led to the largest numbers of vehicle miles driven, and the continuous flow intersection kept traffic moving the best in terms of the highest moving-to-total-time ratio.

As might be expected with a four-lane cross street, the superstreet median design performed poorly. It had the highest total time values and the highest percent stops. It also had the lowest number of trips. Because CORSIM only counts trips when they are completed and does not let vehicles enter the network when the entry link is full of queued vehicles, this is evidence that the superstreet median was near or over capacity at the higher volume levels.

Table 4 also provides results for Intersection 2. Like Intersection 1, the quadrant roadway intersection design again had the lowest average total time, which was significantly lower at the 95 percent level than the second-best design. In this case, though, the second-best intersection (significantly different from all other designs) in terms of average total time was the conventional design. Even at the highest volumes, the conventional design turned in the second-best total time results. In third place, the median U-turn and split intersection designs had average total time values that were not significantly different from each other. Other results followed the pattern mentioned earlier: lowest percent stops for the conventional design, highest vehicle miles for quadrant and median U-turn designs, and highest movingto-total-time ratio for the continuous flow intersection design.
Table 5 shows results for Intersection 3, a four-lane arterial meeting a two-lane cross street. The median U-turn design was the clearly superior design at this intersection. The median U-turn design had significantly lower average total time and average percent stop results (both at the 95 percent level) than the other designs and had the highest moving-to-total-time ratio. The median U-turn design was a solid second-best design for this intersection, rating better than the quadrant design for total time with peak hour volumes, and the superstreet median showed the third-best results.

The quadrant roadway intersection design was the clear leader in total time, producing a significantly lower average total time (at the 95 percent level) and producing the lowest total times for each of the three volume levels. In the second tier, the median U-turn and split intersection designs had mean values of total time that were not significantly different from each other at the 95 percent level. The conventional design produced lower total system times than the other three unconventional designs. For percent stops, the conventional and quadrant designs were significantly (at the 95 percent level) lower than the other designs, although not different from each other. The split intersection design was next for percent stops, followed by the median U-turn design. The familiar patterns in vehicle miles and move-to-total-time ratio reappeared at this intersection, with the median U-turn and quadrant designs being highest in the former and the continuous flow intersection being highest in the latter.

## CONCLUSIONS

The results of the CORSIM simulations of seven existing highvolume arterial intersections indicate that several of the unconventional alternatives have the potential to improve system travel time over conventional designs during peak periods. The analysis also showed that many of the unconventional designs do not compromise system travel times during the off-peak hours of the day relative to a conventional design. Particular trends by design included the following

- The conventional design never produced the lowest average total time. At least one of the unconventional designs always had a lower average total time.
- The conventional design usually produced the lowest percent stops. This finding could correlate to a lower rear-end collision frequency in the long term.
- The quadrant roadway intersection and median U-turn designs usually vied for the lowest average total time. The quadrant roadway performed better at Intersections 1, 2, 6, and 7, and the median U-turn design performed better at Intersections 3 and 4. The two designs had the same result at Intersection 5.
- The quadrant roadway and median U-turn designs produced the most miles driven at each intersection. Certain types of emissions could rise with more miles driven, although emissions may be improved by reduced system travel time.
- Generally, the split intersection competed well with all designs tested at off-peak volume levels and had lower average total times than the conventional design at most intersections.
- The continuous flow intersection always had the highest moveto-total-time ratio of all designs, keeping traffic moving as its name implies.
- The superstreet median and bowtie designs were only competitive with the conventional design at intersections with two-lane cross streets.

Overall, the quadrant roadway intersection and median U-turn designs should receive strong consideration at intersections similar to those studied in this paper, wherever agencies can reasonably procure the extra right-of-way they need. Reductions in peak-period travel times such as those estimated in this paper are too large to dismiss without consideration.

For unconventional designs not performing as well as the quadrant roadway intersection and median U-turn designs, engineers should be mindful of the relative advantages those designs enjoy in special circumstances. The superstreet median design provides for optimum two-way progression with any signal spacing. The bowtie design needs only narrow rights-of-way at intersections. The jughandle design reduces left-turn conflicts on the arterial to zero. The continuous flow intersection probably needs the smallest right-of-way of all the unconventional designs examined here. The split intersection sets the stage for a future interchange. Because all of these designs have their unique niches, the authors suggest that engineers not drop any from their menus of design possibilities.

Unconventional design alternatives that showed improvement over the conventional intersection do not necessitate that an unconventional design is always best or that it should necessarily replace any existing design. The intent of this research was to explore certain geometric and volume conditions that may be better served by an unconventional design alternative not to replace the judgment of the engineer. Certainly, right-of-way, signing, pedestrian movement, and access, which were not part of the scope of this paper, must be considered.

The CORSIM model and analysis results do not reflect driver unfamiliarity with the unconventional design alternatives. Reductions in intersection delay and travel time likely would not reach full potential until some time after the unconventional design is implemented and drivers gain familiarity with turning patterns, particularly if the design is an isolated application.

Many future research questions remain regarding unconventional intersection designs. Many designs require wider or additional rightsof-way compared with a conventional intersection design, and ways to reduce these needs should be explored. Although several of the designs have been implemented in various states and drivers have become accustomed to alternative left-turn patterns, questions remain about driver expectations. Operational questions remain regarding traffic control, the use of dual right turns on red, and the use of left turn on red at the median crossover locations. In the median U-turn and superstreet median designs, high U-turn volumes combined with right-turn volumes from the collector during the same signal phase also should be researched.
