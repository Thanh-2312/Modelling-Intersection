# Influence of bus stop with left－turn lines between two adjacent signalized intersections＊ 

Ming－Bao Pang（庞明宝）${ }^{\dagger}$ ，Lan－Hang Ye（叶兰杭），and Ya－Nan Pei（裴亚男）<br>School of Civil and Transportation，Hebei University of Technology，Tianjin 300401，China

（Received 17 February 2016；revised manuscript received 22 April 2016；published online 25 June 2016）


#### Abstract

Based on the symmetric two－lane Nagel－Schreckenberg（STNS）model，a three－lane cellular automaton model be－ tween two intersections containing a bus stop with left－turning buses is established in which model the occurrences of vehicle accidents are taken into account．The characteristics of traffic flows with different ratios of left－turn lines are dis－ cussed via the simulation experiments．The results indicate that the left－turn lines have more negative effects on capacity， accident rate as well as delay if the stop is located close to the intersections，where the negative effect in a near－side stop is more severe than that in a far－side one．The range of appropriate position for a bus stop without the bottleneck effect becomes more and more narrow with the increase of the ratio of left－turn bus lines．When the inflow is small，a short signal cycle and a reasonable offset are beneficial．When the inflow reaches or exceeds the capacity，a longer signal cycle is helpful．But if the stop position is inappropriate，the increase of cycle fails in reducing the negative effect of left－turning buses and the effectiveness of offset is weakened．


Keywords：cellular automata（CA）model，bus stop，left－turn bus line，vehicle accident

## 1．Introduction

As an important part of public transport networks，bus stops provide the basic conditions for the buses to load and unload passengers．${ }^{[1]}$ They are crucial for urban public trans－ port．However，the set bus stops have some negative influ－ ence on road traffic systems．${ }^{[2,3]}$ The traffic flow on a road－ way is affected when a bus enters in and departs from a stop， for the driving behaviors of other vehicles near the bus have to be changed．Inappropriate position for a bus stop，espe－ cially located close to an intersection，magnifies the negative influence，which frequently causes traffic congestion and acts as a bottleneck for the road．${ }^{[2-8]}$ Moreover the road capacity drops and some traffic accidents may happen．These have been proved by plenty of theoretical studies about the influence of bus stops for roadway traffic and engineering practice，partic－ ularly by using the cellular automata（CA）model in the last decade，which is discrete in both space and time．${ }^{[2-8]}$ For in－ stance，the capacity－drop phenomenon was analyzed，and the combined effect of the signalized intersections and its near－ by bus stops was discussed by using a two－lane CA model by Song and Zhao，${ }^{[2]}$ and Zhao et al．${ }^{[5,6]}$ Qian et al．studied the influences of public transit and the harbor－shaped bus stop on traffic flow by using a one－lane CA traffic model．${ }^{[9]}$ Tian in－ vestigated lane－changing and dwelling behaviors in a two－lane mixed traffic for different proportions of buses over the whole flow．${ }^{[8]}$ According to Kinematic wave theory，${ }^{[10]}$ Gu et al．de－ veloped models and studied vehicle delays and optimization measures when a bus stop was located at a short distance from a signalized intersection．${ }^{[3,4]}$ These studies lay the foundation for further studying the influence of bus stops．However，there are some limitations．

Firstly，the case in which the bus stop has left－turn lines has not been studied，and the influence of left－turning buses has not been discussed either．${ }^{[3-8]}$ In fact，left－turning buses have to change lanes to the left－turn lane after they have de－ parted from the stop and before they reach the forbidden lane－ changing segment at the downstream intersection．Conflicts between left－turning buses and through or right－turning vehi－ cles may occur if the road length for left－turning buses is short and the flux is large with a long queue．This is the main rea－ son why traffic congestion happens and makes the segment a bottleneck，thereby triggering possible traffic accidents．

Secondly，the occurrence of vehicle accident has not been discussed in these models．All drivers are rational，and the safety distance can be met at all times，which does not con－ form to the actual road traffic case obviously．${ }^{[11-16]}$ Especially when a left－turning bus changes lane to the left－turn lane after it has departed from the stop，the through or right－turning ve－ hicles at a relatively high speed might collide with it．${ }^{[13-15]}$ As a result，a rear－end accident may occur．

Thirdly，the coordinated signal timing scheme between two intersections has not been studied．Although some re－ searches discussed that the capacity can be maximized by ad－ justing both the stop position and the cycle，the influence of offset between the two intersections has not been involved．

[^0]According to these concerns, we extend the research object to a road between two signalized intersections containing a bus stop with left-turn lines. A three-lane CA model with the detailed lane-changing rules is presented, where vehicle accidents occur when the necessary conditions are satisfied simultaneously. The influences of bus stop position on capacity, accident rate, and delay are discussed and the performances of cycle time and offset between two intersections are analyzed.

## 2. CA model with vehicle accident

As shown in Fig. 1, a road between two signalized intersections containing a bus stop with left-turn bus lines, is used as the research object. The road is composed of three lanes, named left lane, middle lane, and right lane. The section is divided into five segments denoted as Segments A, B, C, D, and E respectively. Segment A starts from the upstream intersection (stop line of the intersection on the reverse street) where the inflow enters. Segment B is located at the upstream of the stop, where the buses have to change lanes to the right lane in order to enter into the stop. The stop with one lane is located in segment C, where each bus has to spend a certain amount of time on entering in and departing from the stop, and dwelling. Segment D is the part between the stop and the downstream intersection. Sub-segment D1 is the channelized part of the downstream intersection where lane groups are designed, the left lane acts as an exclusive left-turn lane, the middle lane acts as a through lane and the right lane acts as a shared through and right-turn lane. The left-turning buses have to change lanes to the left lane after they have departed from the stop and before they reach D1. Segment E is located at the downstream intersection, which is set for more easy understanding.

In order to describe the traffic flow processing of the studied object, we establish an extended three-lane CA model based on a symmetric two-lane Nagel-Schreckenberg (STNS) model with taking into account the occurrences of vehicle accidents. The road is divided into cells. The length of each cell is 1 m for the purpose of simulating the lane-changing behaviors in a more precise way. Each cell may be either empty or occupied by one vehicle with an integer velocity $v$ between zero and the maximal speed limit $v_{\text {max }}$, where we use $v_{\text {max } 1}$ in place of $v_{\text {max }}$ for a car and $v_{\text {max } 2}$ in place of $v_{\text {max }}$ for a bus. $x(i, t)$ and $v(i, t)$ denote the position and velocity of vehicle $i$ at discrete time step $t$, respectively. The first vehicle is numbered as 1 , and the following vehicles as $2,3, \ldots, i$. Here $d(i, t)=x(i, t)-x(i-1, t)-l$ is the empty cells between vehicle $i$ and its predecessor at time $t$, with $l$ representing the number of cells occupied by one vehicle where $l$ is 6 for a car and 12 for a bus, $d(i, t)_{\text {front }}$ and $d(i, t)_{\text {back }}$ denoting the empty cells between vehicle $i$ and its predecessor and its successor on its destination lane, respectively. Let $d_{\text {safe }}$ denote the safe distance between a vehicle and its preceding one. Considering the complexity of the studied problem, we set $d_{\text {safe }}$ to be $v_{\text {max }}$ in normal vehicle driving motion, and to be 1 m if the speed of the predecessor is zero and the vehicle has to queue for waiting. If the leading vehicle in each lane before the downstream intersection is vehicle $i$ and the signal light of its direction is red, the gap will be calculated as $d(i, t)=x_{\text {light }}-x(i, t)$, where $x_{\text {light }}$ is the position of the stop line at the downstream intersection.

### 2.1. Forward rules

At each discrete time step $t \rightarrow t+1$, the system is updated by the following rules.

Rule 1 Acceleration, $v(i, t+1)=\min \left(v(i, t)+1, v_{\text {max }}\right)$, indicating that the driver of vehicle $i$ expects to maintain the maximal speed.

Rule 2 Deceleration, $v(i, t+1)=\min (v(i, t+1), d(i, t)-$ $d_{\text {safe }}$ ), indicating that the driver decelerates the vehicle so as to maintain a safe distance and avoid crashing with its preceding one.

Rule 3 Randomization, $v(i, t+1)=\max (v(i, t+1)-$ $1,0)$ with randomization probability $P_{1}$, indicating that the vehicle should be decelerated for uncertain factors.

Rule 4 Motion, $x(i, t+1)=x(i, t)+v(i, t+1)$, indicating that the vehicle should continue to move forward with the updated speed.

### 2.2. Lane-changing rules

### 2.2.1. Lane-changing probability

In normal lane-changing rules of a CA mode for a vehicle, the lane-changing probability $P$ is set as a fixed value. But in the study, only partial lane-changing probability with a fixed value is used. ${ }^{[17-19]}$ Here $P_{\mathrm{LC}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{RC}}$ denote the probabilities for a car changing to its left-side lane and changing to its right-side lane if the lane-changing rules are met, respectively. $P_{\mathrm{LB}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{RB}}$ denote the probabilities for a bus changing to its left-side lane and changing to its right-side lane if the lane-changing rules are met, respectively.
(i) $P_{\mathrm{LC}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{RC}}$ in the segments except D1. Here Segment D1 contains $L_{\mathrm{D} 1}$ cells. $P_{\mathrm{LC}}$ for a left-turning car and $P_{\mathrm{RC}}$ for a right-turning car in the section except D1 are defined as two linear-increase functions in the process of approaching the downstream intersection, i.e., $P_{\mathrm{LC}}=P_{\mathrm{RC}}=x(i, t) /\left(x_{\text {light }}-\right.$ $\left.L_{\mathrm{D} 1}\right)$, which shows that $P_{\mathrm{LC}}$ or $P_{\mathrm{RC}}$ increases as the left-turning car or the right-turning car approaches to the stop line. $P_{\mathrm{LC}}$ and
$P_{\mathrm{RC}}$ for other cars in the segments except D1 are set as a fixed value $P_{2}$.
(ii) $P_{\mathrm{LB}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{RB}}$ in the segments except D1. Segment A contains $L_{\mathrm{A}}$ cells, and $P_{\mathrm{RB}}$ in the segment is defined as a linearincrease function, i.e., $P_{\mathrm{RB}}=x(i, t) / L_{\mathrm{A}} . P_{\mathrm{LB}}$ in Segment A is set to be $0 . P_{\mathrm{RB}}$ in Segment B is set to be 1 and $P_{\mathrm{LB}}$ is $0 . P_{\mathrm{LB}}$ for left-turning buses in Segment D except D1 is set to be 1 and $P_{\mathrm{RB}}$ is $0 . P_{\mathrm{LB}}$ for a right-turning bus in Segment D is 0. $P_{\mathrm{LB}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{RB}}$ for a through bus in the Segment D except D1 are both set as a fixed value $P_{2}$.
(iii) Lane-changing probability in Sub-segment D1. All vehicles are prohibited to change lanes after they have reached sub-segment D1, meaning that $P_{\mathrm{LC}}, P_{\mathrm{RC}}, P_{\mathrm{LB}}$, and $P_{\mathrm{RB}}$ are all 0.

### 2.2.2. Lane-changing rules

The different lane-changing rules of a vehicle in different segments are as follows.
i) For Segments A and $\mathrm{E}, d(i, t)<\min \left(v(i, t)+1, v_{\text {max }}\right)$, indicating that vehicle $i$ cannot travel at the expected speed, $d(i, t)_{\text {front }}>d(i, t)$, indicating that the driving condition in the neighboring lane is better than that in its lane, $d(i, t)_{\text {back }}>$ $d_{\text {safe }}$, indicating that when changing lanes, vehicle $i$ cannot collide with its successor on its destination lane for the safety distance is met.

The vehicle will change lanes with its probability, if the above three conditions are met.
ii) Segment B. A car may change lanes by the lanechanging rules described in Segment A with its probability. For a bus in the left lane or middle lane, when two conditions, $d(i, t)_{\text {front }}>v(i, t)$, and $d(i, t)_{\text {back }}>d_{\text {safe }}$, are satisfied simultaneously, it will change to its right-side lane so that it can enter into the stop. If a bus cannot change to the right lane, it will slow down to find the next opportunity to change to the right lane before reaching Segment C. There will be a scene of relatively small traffic jams.
iii) Segment C. The maximal number of buses in the stop with one-lane is two.

For the waiting bus outside the stop, if stop2 $=1, x(i, t+$ $1)=x(i, t)$, meaning that the waiting bus has to stop in the right lane of Segment B, for the latter part of the stop is occupied by the other bus, where stop2 denotes the latter part of the stop and 1 denotes that the part is occupied by the other bus. If stop $2=0, x(i, t+1)=x(i, t)+v(i, t+1)$, meaning that the waiting bus changes lanes and enters into the stop, for the latter part of the stop is empty.

For the entering bus, which occupies the latter part of the stop, if stop1 $=1, x(i, t+1)=x(i, t)$, meaning that the bus has to stop at the position for loading and unloading passengers, for the first part of the stop is occupied by the other bus, where stop1 denotes the first part of the stop. If stop1 = 0, $x(i, t+1)=x(i, t)+v(i, t+1)$, meaning that the entering bus should continue to move forward and stop in the first part for loading and unloading passengers, for the first part of the stop is empty.

For the bus, which occupies the first part of the stop and is ready to depart from the stop, if $d(i, t)_{\text {front }}>v(i, t)$ and $d(i, t)_{\text {back }}>d_{\text {safe }}$, meaning that a bus departs from the stop and enters into the right lane.

The cars may change lanes by the lane-changing rules described in Segment A with its probability.
iv) Segment D. For a left-turning bus, it will continue to change lanes to the left lane with probability $P_{\mathrm{LB}}$ before it reaches Sub-segment D1. Other vehicles may change lanes with their probabilities. All vehicles are prohibited to change lanes after they have reached Sub-segment D1.

### 2.3. Rules of entering, dwelling and departing from the stop

The maximal velocity of bus in the stop is set to be $v_{\text {max } 22}$, and $v_{\text {max } 22}<v_{\text {max } 2}$. The rules for a bus to enter into, move forward and depart from the stop are described in Subsection 2.2. It is impossible to overtake for there is only one lane. When a bus stops in the stop, meaning that its speed is zero, its dwelling time $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ is generated randomly, which follows a normal distribution with a mean value of 30 s according to the Transit Co-operative Research Program (TCRP) Report 19(1996). ${ }^{[5,6]}$

### 2.4. Signal control rules

The coordinated signal timing schemes with four-phase are used, where $T$ is the cycle time and $G$ is offset between the two intersections. For each intersection, the timing scheme includes through and right-turn phase with green time $T_{\mathrm{TR} 1}$ and left-turn phase with green time $T_{\mathrm{L} 1}$ in the east-west direction, through and right-turn phase with green time $T_{\mathrm{TR} 2}$ and left-turn phase with green time $T_{\mathrm{L} 2}$ in the north-south direction.

### 2.5. Criteria rules for a vehicle accident

Considering that the conditions of traffic accidents proposed by Boccara et al., ${ }^{[13]}$ are applied to the continuous traffic flow road, on which a vehicle can move at a high speed and the gap between two vehicles is large, we adjust the first condition $d(i, t) \leq v_{\text {max }}$ to $d(i, t) \leq v(i, t)$ for the reality of urban roads with a low speed and small gap. The other conditions of Boccara et al. remain unchanged. ${ }^{[13]}$ The modified conditions are as follows.

1) $d(i, t) \leq v(i, t)$, indicating that the empty cells between vehicle $i$ and its predecessor are not larger than current velocity and could reach the position of its predecessor by the next time step.
2) $v(i+1, t)>0$, indicating that vehicle $i+1$ will move forward at time $t$.
3) $v(i+1, t+1)=0$, indicating that vehicle $i+1$ will stop abruptly at time $t+1$.

If the three conditions are met, a rear-end accident or a conflict is calculated. Obviously the above three conditions for the occurrences of car accidents hold true under the assumption of neglecting time correlations. ${ }^{[13]}$ Although the future traffic state, i.e., $v(i+1, t+1)$, cannot be known at current time in real traffic, the accident rate in the numerical simulation can be calculated when the simulation is all finished, which means that we have obtained all the sequences of space and time data and know all the states in the simulation period. Here the accident rate is just a numerical parameter in evaluating the influence on traffic safety.

The accident occurrence number $N_{\mathrm{ac}}$ is increased by 1 if these necessary conditions are satisfied. The accident probability $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ is defined as $N_{\mathrm{ac}} / N$, where $N$ is the total number of vehicles.

### 2.6. Delay statistics

A statistical approach is proposed to calculate the delays experienced by vehicles, including average control delay and delay incurred by bus interference near the stop. Specifically, it is assumed that the cars would travel through the road with maximal speed $v_{\text {max }}$ in the absence of a control facility of the intersection. At each time step, vehicles move forward a length of current speed $v$, thus we calculate the difference between speed $v$ and $v_{\text {max }}$ at each time step for each vehicle on the road in the stimulation period $T T$. The delay $D$ is defined as

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{T T} \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(v_{\max }-v(i, t)\right) / v_{\max } .
$$

### 2.7. Boundary conditions

The boundary conditions are open.

### 2.7.1. Inflow boundary condition

The vehicles generated randomly on the stop lines of the upstream intersection pass through the intersection according to the signal control rules, where partial vehicles reach Segment A and become the real inflow of the studied object. In order to simplify the studied problem, the signalized intersections are not included into the studied object. But we use some relatively simple rules to map the relation between the signal control rules of the upstream intersection and the inletting flow. In each green-light period, the total entering probability is distributed to each lane of the subject street segment. $P_{\mathrm{e}}$ denotes the entering probability for each inletting lane of the upstream intersection. (I) Vehicle generation. ### 2.7.2. Outflow boundary condition

At each time step, the leading vehicle in each lane of the downstream intersection is removed, if its position $x_{\text {first }}>L_{\mathrm{R}}$. Here $L_{\mathrm{R}}$ denotes the length of the road. Then its following vehicle becomes the new leader.

## 3. Simulation results and discussion

### 3.1. Simulation condition and signal timing scheme

The road described in Section 2 is simulated, where the total length $L_{\mathrm{R}}$ is 1200 m meaning that there are 1200 cells and the downstream intersection is located at the 1000th cell. Section C contains $L_{\mathrm{C}}=50$ cells, section D1 contains $L_{\mathrm{D} 1}=30$ cells, and section E contains $L_{\mathrm{E}}=200$ cells. $L$ denotes the distance between the stop and the downstream intersection. The ratio of the left-turning car is 0.2. Four bus lines are set. Other parameters are set as follows: $v_{\text {max } 1}=14, v_{\text {max } 2}=11$, $v_{\text {max } 22}=5, P_{1}=P_{2}=0.3, P_{\mathrm{LC}}=P_{\mathrm{RC}}=x(i, t) /\left(1000-L_{\mathrm{D} 1}\right)$, $t_{1}=t_{2}=5 \mathrm{~s}, t_{3}=3 \mathrm{~s}, t_{4}=4 \mathrm{~s}, t_{5}=6 \mathrm{~s} . P_{\mathrm{LT}}=P_{\mathrm{RT}}=0.2$, and $P_{\mathrm{LU}}=0.01$. Bus arrival follows a Poisson distribution and the average arrival rate is 1/60. Each time step corresponds to 1 s. The first $10^{4}$ time steps are eliminated to avoid transient behaviors. The flow is averaged by $2 \times 10^{4}$ time steps. The final result is an average of 10 simulation results in order to reduce stochastic influence.

The coordinated signal timing schemes with four phases are used. The control parameters of the two intersections are set to be the same, for there is no difference in basic condition between the intersections. At the same time, we adopt different timing strategies by the traffic states in order to obtain the optimal control parameters. The fixed timing control is used for saturated flow, and actuated signal control is used for unsaturated flow. ${ }^{[20]}$ Then the concrete control method and parameters are optimized for each simulation experiment. For example, three methods including the most famous and typical TRRL (Webster) model and its two improved methods, namely the Australian Road Research Bureau (ARRB) model and the stop-line method, are used to calculate the parameters of each signal timing scheme when the traffic state is saturated flow. ${ }^{[18-20]}$ By comparing the simulation results of signal timing schemes, the method and its parameter values are determined. Table 1 shows the calculated parameter values and their simulated results of a case, where inflow volume is $1150 \mathrm{veh} / \mathrm{h}, L=200 \mathrm{~m}$, the ratio of left-turning bus lines is 50\%, and offset is 60s. Obviously the control effect using the ARRB model is best. The optimal control parameters by the ARRB model are applied to the case.

### 3.2. Influence of left-turning bus line

Figure 2 shows the comparison of the flux, $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ and $D$ of simulation results between case 1 without left-turn bus line (shown in Fig. 2(a)) and case 2 with a 50\% ratio of left-turn bus lines (shown in Fig. 2(b)), where the values of $L$ are 50 m, 75 m, 100 m, and 150 m, respectively. The achieved findings are as follows.
I) The traffic volume increases with the inflow increasing until it reaches a saturated value. There is a critical point of the inflow above which the traffic on the studied road transforms from free flow to saturated flow. The saturated flow describes the capacity of the road. At the same time, the accident probability $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ decreases slightly before the inflow reaches the saturated flow and then increases markedly after the saturated flow. There is no difference in $D$ before the saturated flow and then increases markedly after the saturated flow. This is because there are fewer vehicles on the road when the state is free flow. $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ and $D$ are very low and there is no great difference. When a jamming state occurs, the disorder phenomenon becomes more and more serious with the increase of inflow. Then $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ and $D$ increases markedly.
II) The influences of $L$ are different. The smaller the value of $L$, the greater the influence is, if the bus stop is located close to the downstream intersection. The results are again proved
that inappropriate bus stops have a negative effect on traffic flow. ${ }^{[5,6]}$
III) The negative effect with 2 left-turn bus lines is more serious than that without a left-turn line when the inflow exceeds the saturated flow, $L$ is a small value, i.e., 100 m , and the other conditions are the same. ### 3.4. Influence of signal control

Figure 7 shows the relationships between the flux and inflow, $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ and inflow, $D$ and inflow for different values of signal cycle $T$, where there are 2 left-turn bus lines and the stop is located at 100 m in case a, 200 m in case b from the downstream intersection. The achieved findings are as follows.

F1 The flux increases with the inflow increasing until it reaches a saturated value in Figs. 7(a1) and 7(b1). The capacity grows with the increase of $T$. It means a longer cycle corresponding to a higher capacity in a certain range. The capacities for different values of $T$ in Fig. 7(a1) are all smaller than their counterparts in Fig. 7(b1) due to the more serious negative effect of small $L$.

F2 Before the inflow reaches the saturation flow, $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ (Figs. 7(a2) and 7(b2)) and $D$ (Figs. 7(a3) and 7(b3)) are very small, where $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ keeps a slow decline and $D$ keeps a slow increase with the increase of inflow if the signal cycle is relatively short, i.e., $T=80,100$, and 120 s . But $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ is large and keeps a fast decline with the inflow increasing if $T$ is relatively long, i.e., $T=160$ and 200 s. In general, $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ in the case with a longer cycle time is larger than that in the case with a shorter cycle time when in free flow state. However, when the inflow reaches the capacity, $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ and $D$ in the case with a shorter cycle time increase more dramatically with the increase of inflow, while in the case with a longer cycle time, $P_{\mathrm{ac}}$ and $D$ rise much more slowly. It means that a longer cycle time corresponds to a lower accident rate and delay when in a saturated state.

When the inflow reaches or exceeds the capacity, a longer signal cycle is helpful in enhancing capacity, reducing accident rate and delay. But if the stop position is inappropriate, i.e., $L=100 \mathrm{~m}$, the increase of cycle fails to reduce the negative effect of left-turning buses.

## 4. Conclusions and perspectives

The influences of bus stop with left-turn lines between two adjacent signalized intersections on capacity, accident rate and delay are studied by using a three-lane CA model. The results of the simulations suggest that the left-turning buses in a near-side stop have more negative effects. The range of appropriate position for bus stop with no bottleneck effect becomes smaller and smaller with the left-turn bus lines increasing. When the inflow reaches or exceeds the capacity, a longer signal cycle is helpful. But if the stop position is inappropriate, the increase of cycle fails to reduce the negative effect of a left-turning bus and the effectiveness of offset is weakened. These findings may provide some reference values for setting bus stop and traffic management.

Some relatively simple rules are used to map the relation between the signal control rules of the upstream intersection and the inletting flow. The inflows from different directions are set to be the same. Vehicle accident does not really occur in the simulation and these dangerous situations are only calculated and considered to be the signals of accidents occurring. Future work will concentrate on these problems.
