#### Abstract

In this paper, we address the optimal autonomous vehicle (AV) coordination problem at road intersections, which is of great importance in modern intelligent transportation systems (ITS). We first formulate it as a general collision-free traffic scheduling framework. Aiming at the dynamic characteristics of vehicles' arrival, a corresponding dynamic coordination strategy is thus proposed to achieve better quality of service (QoS), i.e., the traffic throughput and delay. The road stability is also guaranteed using the Rate Stability Theorem and Lyapunov Theorem. Provided numerical results validate our analysis and show the performance improvements achieved by the proposed framework.


## I. Introduction

With the rapid development of urbanization nowadays, traffic jams leads to numerous problems to the modern transportation system, including environmental pollution, traffic congestions and accidents, etc [1]-[3].

Since several roads merge at road intersections, they play important roles in transportation systems, even though the occupation of intersections are proportional small. According to presented reports, nearly 20\% of fatalities recently are intersection related in EU and Unite States, which makes intelligent intersection traffic management pronounced and challenging [4], [5]. However, in combination with the development of wireless communications, augmented situational awareness as well as intelligent control and decision-making algorithms, opportunistic vehicle coordination becomes promising, especially for autonomous vehicles, which have attracted more and more attention from both academic and industrial areas.

Recently, the intersection coordination problem for fully autonomous vehicles remains open, and has attracted growing interests. Early investigations in [6]-[8] suppose that, there exists a decision center within the intersection area, by which a time-space collision avoidance strategy can be developed. The authors in [9] combine a first-come-first-served policy with the concept of optimal coordination problem to optimize the design of control signals. In [10], the intersection coordination problem is formulated to reduce the communications overhead, and try to exploit low complexity distributed versions of the problem. A model-based heuristics algorithm is also presented in [11], where the original coordination problem is decomposed into two sub-problems, to reduce the complexity of the solution.

Most existing investigations are now focused on a static scenario, where the total number of involved vehicles is known, and does not change until all vehicles are cleared [12]. The main task of the coordination problem is also designed to minimize the driving cost (e.g., fuel consumption), by adjusting the driving speed and acceleration. Since the original problem tends to be NP-hard, low complexity approximations are tried to achieve real time solutions [11].

However, the dynamic feature of the traffic shows that, the queues on the roads waiting to cross the intersection changes with time. Therefore, in addition to the safety guarantee, the most important task for coordination is to make more vehicles pass the intersection within the limited time period. In other words, our main scope of this piece of work is to maximize the throughput of road intersections.

In this paper, we first formulate a dynamic coordination framework based on the queuing theory. Then the original complicated problem is decomposed into two sub-problems, and can be easily handled. The stability of all queues within the intersection is analyzed using the Rate Stability Theorem. Then a low complexity dynamic coordination algorithm is presented based on the Lyapunov Theorem. Numerical results show that, the presented coordination is able to achieve better quality of service (QoS) compared to existing sub-optimal strategies.

## II. System Model

We consider a typical road intersection which is consisted of four roads, as shown in Fig. 1. Queued vehicles with different destinations, enter the intersection, and cross it under instructions. Our goal is to find the optimal traffic coordination strategy that achieves better QoS, namely traffic throughput and delay. Meanwhile, no traffic accident occurs in each road.

The coordination system is described by a time-discrete model and divided into three main sections, including the following:

1) Arrival Area (AA). AA is where vehicles arrive the coordination system dynamically. In this part, the continual arrival can be described by the widely adopted Poisson Process [13]. The expected arrival rate $\lambda_{k}$ reflects the busyness of the $k$-th road.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/e4012029-977f-4e12-9f2d-e5ad872a0c92-2.jpg?height=866&width=793&top_left_y=183&top_left_x=208)
Fig. 1. Illustration of the coordination system.

2) Buffer Area (BA). In BA, each arrived vehicle queues up and waits for the indication.
3) Conflict Area (CA). CA represents the intersection area where a potential collision may occur. In this paper, we see the condition that any two vehicles occupy the CA at the same time as a collision [14].

In BA and CA, we use queue length $i_{k}$ to indicate the number of vehicles waiting for coordination at road $k$. Notations $\mathcal{K}=\{1, \ldots, 4\}$ and $\mathcal{I}=\left\{1, \ldots, i_{k}\right\}$ are used to represent the sets of roads and vehicles. The state parameters of the $i$-th vehicle on road $k$ are described by $\mathbf{x}_{i, k}(t)=\left[p_{i, k}(t), v_{i, k}(t)\right]^{\mathrm{T}}$, where $p_{i, k}(t)$ and $v_{i, k}(t)$ are position and velocity, respective$\mathrm{ly}^{1}$. The motion dynamics of the $i$-th vehicle at time step $t$ is given by

$$
\mathbf{x}_{i, k}(t+1)=\mathbf{A} \mathbf{x}_{i, k}(t)+\mathbf{B} u_{i, k}(t), i \in \mathcal{I}, k \in \mathcal{K}
$$

where $t \in\{1,2 \ldots, T\}$ denotes the discrete time interval, $u_{i}^{k}(t)$ denotes the control inputs. Considering the practical constraints of any individual vehicle, we do have

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 & \leq v_{i, k}(t) \leq v_{i, k \text { max }} \\
u_{i, k \text { min }} & \leq u_{i, k}(t) \leq u_{i, k \text { max }}
\end{aligned}
$$

where (2) implies that backing up is not allowed for each vehicle.

As shown in Fig. 1, the intersection area is modeled as a closed and compact subset of positions along the path of each vehicle. The entering and leaving positions of each vehicle are defined as $L_{i, k}$ and $H_{i, k}$, respectively. To avoid accidents caused by the positioning errors $\delta$, a safety distance $d_{\mathrm{sa}}$ is

[^0]given as
$$
d_{\mathrm{sa}} \geq \delta
$$
The actual collision-free area is thus $\left[L_{i, k}-\delta, H_{i, k}+\delta\right]$, defined by $\left[L_{i, k}^{*}, H_{i, k}^{*}\right]$. The corresponding arrival time $t\left(L_{i, k}^{*}\right)$ and $t\left(H_{i, k}^{*}\right)$ can be achieved according to (1).

In this paper, we assume that a collision occurs when any two vehicles' occupation time in CA is overlapped. The collision free constraint is expressed as

$$
\left[t\left(L_{i, m}^{*}\right) t\left(H_{i, m}^{*}\right)\right] \cap\left[t\left(L_{j, n}^{*}\right) t\left(H_{j, n}^{*}\right)\right]=\emptyset
$$

where $m, n \in \mathcal{K}, i \in\left\{1, \ldots, i_{m}\right\}$ and $j \in\left\{1, \ldots, i_{n}\right\}$ respectively.

Depending on different performance criteria, the cost function $J_{\text {cost }}$ can be defined as:

- $J_{\text {fast }}$ : An objective function which relates to the maximum velocity to maximize the traffic throughput.
- $J_{\text {efficient }}$. An objective function which relates to the efficient driving velocity to achieve high coordination efficiency.

In this paper, we mainly focus on the traffic throughput, the cost function is thus described as

$$
J_{\text {fast }}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i, k}(t), u_{i, k}(t)\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(v_{i, k \max }-v_{i, k}(t)\right)^{2}
$$

If the intersection system is static, i.e., irrespective the influence of AA, the general coordination framework can be described as

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathscr{P}_{1}: \min _{u_{i, k}(t)} & \sum_{k=1}^{4} \sum_{i=1}^{i_{k}} J_{\text {fast }}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i, k}(t), u_{i, k}(t)\right) \\
\text { s.t. } & (1)-(4)
\end{aligned}
$$

Due to the constraint (4), only one vehicle can occupy the intersection at any time. The key solution to (4) is to determine the passing order of all vehicles, which is essentially NP-hard. Low complexity distributed approximate solutions are given in [11], [14].

## III. Optimal Dynamic Coordination Framework

## A. Problem Formulation

Problem $\mathscr{P}_{1}$ formulates the general framework for static traffic coordination. However, the queue length of each road is time-varying due to the dynamic nature of AA, i.e., $i_{k}(t)$. Thus, we need to ensure that all queues in the coordination system remain stable under coordination, i.e.,

$$
\lim _{T \rightarrow \infty} \sup \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} E\left[i_{k}(t)\right]<\infty, \quad k \in \mathcal{K}
$$

The following stochastic optimization framework is given for the dynamic coordination problem, based on $\mathscr{P}_{1}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathscr{P}_{2}: \min _{u_{i, k}(t)} & \sum_{k=1}^{4} \sum_{i=1}^{i_{k}(t)} J_{\text {fast }}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i, k}(t), u_{i, k}(t)\right) \\
\text { s.t. } & (1)-(4),(7)
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that in $\mathscr{P}_{2}$, the number of vehicles on road $k$ becomes a function of $t$. Constraint (4) and the extra stability constraint (7) make the problem challenging. Proper relaxations are provided in the next part, by which $\mathscr{P}_{2}$ can be solved efficiently.

## B. Problem Decomposition

In this section, we first try to give the maximal throughput of the coordination system, which acts as important performance benchmarks. In order to reduce the complexity of the constraint (4), the passing order $O$ is then determined by a drift-plus-penalty expression related to the current queue backlog. Finally, a multiple-roads coordination framework is given based on $O$, by which all queues in the coordination system can remain stable.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/e4012029-977f-4e12-9f2d-e5ad872a0c92-3.jpg?height=335&width=771&top_left_y=1167&top_left_x=221)
Fig. 2. Illustration of the single-road scenario.

1) Upper limit analysis: As shown in Fig. 2, the dynamic length of queue in BA is essentially determined by the arrival process in AA and the departure process in CA. Therefore, the dynamic length can be written as $i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right)$. The task of single road coordination is to maximize the available $\lambda_{k}$, by which the road's potential can be fully exploited.

Since vehicles are served as a FIFO strategy, constraint (4) on road $k$ can be easily rewritten as an affine one, i.e.,

$$
t\left(H_{i-1, k}^{*}\right) \leq t\left(L_{i, k}^{*}\right), \forall i \in \mathcal{I}
$$

where $t\left(H_{i-1, k}^{*}\right)$ and $t\left(L_{i, k}^{*}\right)$ denote the out and in times of vehicle $i-1$ and vehicle $i$ on road $k$, respectively.

Therefore, the single road dynamic coordination problem can be concluded as a standard Quadratic Programming (QP) problem at time $t$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathscr{P}_{3}: \min _{u_{i, k}(t)} & \sum_{i=1}^{i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right)} J_{\text {fast }}\left(\mathbf{x}_{i, k}(t), u_{i, k}(t)\right) \\
\text { s.t. } & (1)-(3),(9)
\end{aligned}
$$

By solving $\mathscr{P}_{3}$, the coordination time for each vehicle can be achieved, i.e.,

$$
T_{i, k}=t\left(H_{i, k}^{*}\right)
$$

Note that $T_{i, k}$ depends on the form of (5), and results in different average coordination rate

$$
a_{k}^{\mathrm{av}}=\frac{1}{i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right)} \sum_{i=1}^{i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right)} \frac{1}{T_{i, k}}
$$

Note that, $a_{k}^{\text {av }}$ is an important parameter which reflects the coordination ability of each road. And in next part, $a_{k}^{\text {av }}$ is used to solve the dynamic coordination problem. When $\lambda_{k}$ and $a_{k}^{\text {av }}$ are given, the dynamic state equation of the $k^{\text {th }}$ queuing system is given by:

$$
i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t+1\right)=\max \left\{i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right)-a_{k}^{\mathrm{av}}, 0\right\}+\lambda_{k}
$$

According to the Rate Stability Theorem [15], the traffic on road $k$ stays rate stable if and only if

$$
\lambda_{k} \leq a_{k}^{\mathrm{av}}
$$

Remark 1: In the single road scenario, since there are no other competitive candidates, the only road occupies the whole coordination resources. Therefore, $a_{k}^{\text {av }}$ can thus be defined as the intersection capacity, which performs the upper limit in coordination throughput analysis.

Remark 2: In order to prevent rear-end collision, the safety distance between any two adjacent vehicles in BA is set as $d_{\mathrm{sa}}$. In this paper, when a departure process is in progress, remaining vehicles will fill the vacancy by a coordination strategy given in [14], as shown in Fig. 2.
2) Dynamic multiple roads coordination: In this part, we consider a real intersection where multiple roads are involved. To determine the passing order $O$, we refer to an alternative approach based on minimizing the drift of a Lyapunov function. Finally, a drift-plus-penalty based solution is presented to achieve both efficiency and stability. Intuitively, it can be explained as the coordination resources sharing among different roads, based on the intersection capacity defined in the previous section.

Let $\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)=\left[i_{1}\left(\lambda_{1}, t\right), i_{2}\left(\lambda_{2}, t\right), i_{3}\left(\lambda_{3}, t\right), i_{4}\left(\lambda_{4}, t\right)\right]^{\mathrm{T}}$ be the vector of current four queue backlogs of waiting vehicles, the Lyapunov function $L(\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t))$ is defined by

$$
L(\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)) \triangleq \sum_{k=1}^{4} i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right)^{2}
$$

which is a scalar measure of road congestion at each sampling time period. For multiple-roads scenario, if the total arrival rate $\lambda$ and the total departure rate $a^{\mathrm{av}}$ satisfy (14) ${ }^{2}$, the following solutions will stabilize the traffic at intersections [15].

Proposition 1: For a rate stable coordination system, minimizing the bound on $\Delta(L(\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)))$ every slot $t$ would

[^1]stabilize the system, which $\Delta(L(\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)))$ is expressed as
$$
\Delta(L(\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t))) \triangleq \mathbb{E}\{L(\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t+1))-L(\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)) \mid \mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)\}
$$
Substitute (13) and (15) into (16), the bound can be derived
$$
\Delta(L(\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t))) \leq B-\sum_{k=1}^{4} i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right) a_{k}^{\mathrm{av}} \mid \mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)
$$
where $B$ is a constant, $a^{\text {av }}$ is obtained from (12), the minimum upper bound of $\Delta(L(\mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)))$ can thus be obtained by maximizing
$$
\sum_{k=1}^{4} i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right) a_{k}^{\mathrm{av}} \mid \mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)
$$
Note that, to maximize (18), the coordination system will select the road with a long queue and high average coordination rate. In this paper, to achieve better (QoS), we try to minimize a bound on the following drift-plus-penalty expression:
$$
G J_{\text {cost }}-\sum_{k=1}^{4} i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right) a_{k}^{\text {av }} \mid \mathbf{i}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}, t)
$$
where $J_{\text {cost }}$ is defined in section II. To minimize the latency time, we set $J_{\text {cost }}=J_{\text {fast. }} G \geq 0$ represents an importance weight on a balance between the latency and the stability of the coordination system. Note that if the latency time takes a great proportion in (19), the convergence rate of (7) will decrease correspondingly. According to the coordination cost given by (6), average coordination rate given by (12) and the congestion state $i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right)$ given by (13), the passing order $O$ is obtained by greedily minimizing (19).

When $O$ is determined, we can calculate the control signal for each car by $\mathscr{P}_{3}$. The passing order $O$ does not change until next decision is made. Therefore, the decision interval $T_{\mathrm{d}}$ will affect the coordination performance directly. It is clear that, the decision interval $T_{\mathrm{d}}$ should be less than the coherence time of the traffic to achieve the optimal solution. Or the quality of service (QoS) including throughput and delay will be degraded, which can be illustrated as heavy traffic congestion. In our work, the decision interval is set according to the average interval of vehicle arrival, according to the definition of vehicle arrival process.

## $C$. The dynamic coordination algorithm

According to the previous analysis, we consider an algorithm based on the current queue length and the maximum latency of each road and change it after each $T_{\mathrm{d}}$.

## IV. NUMERICAL RESULTS

## A. Simulation Settings

In this part, four roads are considered in a typical traffic intersection, same as Fig. 1. Each road is modeled as a finite length queueing system. For each vehicle, the speed limits $v_{i, k \text { min }}[\mathrm{m} / \mathrm{s}]$ and $v_{i, k \text { max }}[\mathrm{m} / \mathrm{s}]$, actuation limits $u_{i, k \text { min }}\left[\mathrm{m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}\right]$ and $u_{i, k \text { max }}\left[\mathrm{m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}\right]$ are given by Table I. Sampling time of each vehicle is set by $T_{\mathrm{s}}=0.05[\mathrm{~s}]$.

```
Algorithm 1 Decentralized coordination at the intersection
    initialize the vehicles' parameters subject to (2) and (3)
    repeat
        for each \(k \in[1,4]\) do
            for each \(i \in\left[1, i_{k}\left(\lambda_{k}, t\right)\right]\) do
                if new vehicles arrive then
                    update the vehicles' parameters
                end if
                \(\left.\mathcal{V}_{( } i, k\right)\) : receive \(t\left(H_{j, k}^{*}\right), \forall i, j \in \mathcal{I}, j<i\)
                \(\left.\mathcal{V}_{( } i, k\right)\) : solve the distributed coordination problem
                \(\mathscr{P}_{1}\)
                \(\mathcal{V}_{(i, k):}\) broadcast \(t\left(H_{i, k}^{*}\right)\)
            end for
            \(\mathcal{R}_{k}\) : calculate the total coordination time of \(k\)-th
            road and solve the drift-plus-penalty expression (19)
            according to the queue length of each road
        end for
        for each \(k \in[1,4]\) do
            compare the value of (19)
            if expression (19) of \(r\)-th road is smallest then
                coordinate vehicles on \(r\)-th road, vehicles on other
                roads are waiting for the next decision interval \(T_{\mathrm{d}}\)
            end if
        end for
        update the queue information by (13)
until the coordination process is over
```

## C. Intersection Capacity

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/e4012029-977f-4e12-9f2d-e5ad872a0c92-5.jpg?height=541&width=683&top_left_y=1246&top_left_x=251)

In Fig. 4, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the coordination time is demonstrated to achieve the range of the coordinate rate. From the simulation results, we find that the average coordination time (ACT) varies between 0.85-1.24 seconds. It can be seen that

- If the average coordination rate $\lambda \leq \frac{1}{1.24}$, the intersection will stay stable all the time, which corresponds to a low traffic scenario.
- Otherwise, if $\lambda \geq \frac{1}{0.85}$, the number of vehicles waiting for traffic will rise over time, i.e., a high traffic scenario.

## E. Throughput Comparison

As mentioned before, the intelligent coordination method is essentially to properly allocate coordination resources among different roads. The main task is to push the real throughput towards the capacity as close as possible. A sub-optimal uniform coordination strategy is presented for performance comparison. In the uniform coordination, all roads share same amount of opportunities to enter the intersection.

Two different scenarios in Fig. 5 are considered. Road 2 acts as the main road, with a large amount (50\%) of the total traffic, while the other 3 small roads share the rest 50\% traffic. Meanwhile, two different conditions including busy and idle hours are considered for evaluation. In Fig. 6(b) and Fig. 7(b), the buffer queue in road 2 increases heavily, while other 3 roads are wasting resources under the uniform coordination strategy. The proposed method makes the vehicles in the buffer queues quite balanced, and the whole system remains stable.

Total throughput and maximum delay are also compared in Fig. 8. We can see that, the proposed strategy outperforms the uniform one obviously in two scenarios, which validates our analysis.

## V. Conclusions

In this paper, we mainly deal with the dynamic autonomous vehicle coordination problem at road intersections. Due to the dynamic feature of the vehicles' arrival, our main contribution lies to formulate a general coordination framework, which tries to make the system stable using stochastic optimization. Low complexity algorithms are also presented to solve the essentially NP-hard problem. Numeric results show that, (i) the road intersection capacity can be thus achieved by solving our proposed framework. (ii) The maximum arrival rate of the vehicles can be identified according to the framework, or the coordination system will not keep stable. (iii) Compared to the sub-optimal coordination strategy, the proposed method achieves obvious performance advantage whether the traffic is high or low. The presented frameworks, algorithms and intuitive numeric results will provide meaningful insights for theoretical researches as well as practical implementations.
