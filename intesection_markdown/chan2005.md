# Evaluation of Cooperative Roadside and Vehicle-Based Data Collection for Assessing Intersection Conflicts 

Ching-Yao Chan, IEEE Member, Bénédicte Bougler<br>California PATH Program, University of California at Berkeley<br>1357 S. $46^{\text {th }}$ Street, Richmond, CA 94804, USA


#### Abstract

Intersection collisions represent a significant portion of highway accidents. Recent research and development activities have suggested potential solutions to address this critical safety issue by supplying timely alerts to drivers of imminent collisions. These intersection decision support systems utilized sensor, communication and computer technologies to help drivers recognize risky conditions, thus actions can be taken to avoid collisions. A cooperative vehicle-infrastructure concept, built upon such premises, seek to combine the information available from vehicles and roadside in a synergistic framework so that a flexible yet sensible system can be deployed in the near future. The work presented in this paper represents an experimental effort conducted at a real-world intersection, by the use of an instrumented vehicle and roadside traffic monitoring, to explore technical challenges encountered in a vehicle-infrastructure integration approach.


Index Terms - Vehicle Infrastructure Integration (VII), Intersection Decision Support (IDS), data collection, intersection crashes, conflict assessment

## I. Introduction

Traffic accumulates and crosses paths at intersections, where accident risks and concentration of collisions are noticeably high. $(1,2)$ Overall crossing path crashes comprise 25 percent of the crashes in the United States. The most commonly seen collisions at or near intersection are straight crossing-path, rear-ending, and left-turn across paths. $(2,3)$ The contributing factors to these collisions include driver inattention, signal violation, and incorrect driver judgment. These primary factors of accidents are more noticeable for intersection crashes.

Within the Intelligent Vehicle Initiative (IVI) program of the USDOT, an Infrastructure Consortium was established to work on the development of Intersection Decision Support (IDS) systems. (4-6) This consortium consists of the state departments of transportation of California, Minnesota and Virginia, teamed with the leading public research universities in their respective states. Additional organizations are now joining the IDS partners to carry out a broader scope of work under the Cooperative Intersection Collision Avoidance Systems (CICAS).

In the envisioned IDS solutions, sensors are deployed near and at intersections to gather the information about approaching traffic. Computers and embedded data processing assess risks presented for a subject vehicle (SV), based on the relative positions and speeds of other vehicles. At appropriate time instants, an advisory signal is displayed or transmitted to alert the SV driver of the imminent hazard. Even though the aforementioned system can be mostly aecomplished by the implementation of sub-systems on the infrastructure, a more flexible and sensible solution relies upon the combination of infrastructure-based and vehicle-based information. For example, instead of using a driver-infrastructure interface (DII), such as a waming sign to communicate to the SV driver, a similar or a different signal can be utilized inside a vehicle for more direct interaction. Moreover, any of the functionality required for an IDS solution, including sensing, processing, warning, and control, can be conceptually distributed between infrastructure- and vehicle-based sub-systems. The operating concepts and associated technical challenges under this framework are the topics of studies under the CICAS program.

The developments of countermeasures to provide timely alert to drivers require situation-dependent assessment of driver behaviors in particular scenarios, such as straight crossing or left-turn across-path. Nevertheless, the building blocks of IDS and CICAS solutions, including sensing and surveillance, wireless communication, and state map construction (5), are cross-cutting elements common to all intersection conflict conditions. In this paper, we describe an experimental study. that was conducted to analyze the feasibility and the challenges that are involved in generating a timely warning for SV drivers. We will use the Left-Turn Across-Path/Opposite Direction (LTAP) situation in our analysis, while monitoring relevant issues that are applicable to other conflict types.

The LTAP-OD conflict occurs when a subject vehicle (SV), while making a left turn, encounters a threat presented by an approaching principal other vehicle (POV). The POV refers to the opposing vehicle that is most likely to be in conflict with SV due to its closeness in distance or time. SV drivers typically have difficulties in judging the arrival lag or gap of POV. The proposed IDS and CICAS countermeasure depend on sensors to detect the presence and speed of the vehicles approaching the intersection so that the lengths of time lags or gaps in the POV traffic can be quantified. The advisory signals to alert the SV drivers of the potential conflict are issued when the traffic conditions are predicted to cause imminent hazards and/or close encounters.

We have conducted a series of studies to establish a systematic methodology to allow the analysis of driver behaviors in the aforementioned LTAP-OD and LTAP-LD scenarios, which is a critical case for intersection decision support systems. In earlier publications (7-9), we have reported on the results of
field observations to monitor traffic patterns and to conduct threat assessment of oncoming traffic faced by the subject vehicles. Furthermore, we have utilized the field observation data to estimate the driver time gap acceptance behaviors. (10) In this paper, we use the same roadside traffic monitoring methods and in addition utilize an instrumented vehicle to collect synchronized roadside and vehicle-based data. Through the analysis of these data, we attempt to evaluate the technical challenges in assessing intersection conflicts.

## II. Motivation for experimental evaluation .

One major challenge in devising warning criteria, and thus implementing an effective IDS or CICAS solution, is the estimation of current vehicle trajectories and the prediction of potential conflicts. Constructing a complete sensing system to capture all relevant traffic information is no trivial matter, regardless of whether it is a pure infrastructure-based approach or it is to be achieved through the fusion of information from both the infrastructure and the vehicles.

Conventional traffic monitoring devices can offer only partial information. For example, embedded ground loops typically generate presence signals only while video systems detect vehicle presence and speed at the virtual-loop locations only. Even more advanced target detection and tracking sensors, such as radar or vision systems, have limited field of views or ranges or resolutions. Hardly any single sensor can satisfy the requirements of an IDS or CICAS system. It is likely that a suite of sensors need to be combined to provide an adequate set of inputs for the sensing and surveillance function. Under the VII (vehicle-infrastructure integration) architecture, the CICAS system will further require that the vehicle and infrastructure information is consistently and reliably exchanged by sharing the measurements from both sides.

The other aspect of providing an effective timing in CICAS systems is the prediction of conflict probability and the subsequent issuance of alert signals. Complications in the providing a warning arise in two ways. One is that the prediction based on best engineering data judgment is not necessarily correct, simply because drivers may take actions in response to personal decisions or unexpected circumstances on the road. The other is that even if threat is correctly assessed, the whole population of drivers has a wide range of acceptance tolerances and will perceive and react differently to the offered advisory signal.

In consideration of these complex issues, in previous work we have conducted a number of field observations at real-world intersections with monitoring of traffic interactions between subject vehicles and other vehicles. Through the field data collections, some insight can be gained in interpreting the traffic patterns in typical maneuvers. Furthermore, based on the actual driver actions, an estimation of driver acceptance behaviors is possible. Extending from the previous work, we proceeded to set up a field observation where we used similar roadside equipment, as reported in previous publications (7-8). In addition, we used an instrumented vehicle in conjunction with the roadside setup at the selected intersections. More detailed descriptions of the field observation are given below.

The objectives of the conducted field experiments are two-fold: 1) To acquire synchronized movements of subject vehicles and other vehicles as the basis for evaluation
In our previous experiments, it was found that even if multiple sensors were set up at different orientations, the availability of simultaneous data of subject vehicles and other vehicles are often limited, partly due to the limited functionality of the sensor used and partly due to the obstructed or limited views of sensors. Furthermore, in order to monitor of potential conflicts the collected data of multiple vehicles must be synchronized to be meaningful at-all.
2) To investigate the limitations of a potential operating concept of vehicle-infrastructure integration framework There are a number of operating concepts that can be implemented for the CICAS systems. One differentiating factor is the distribution of information coming from infrastructure and the vehicles. One extreme version of which is to assume that there is very little sensor investment on the infrastructure except for the existing traffic devices and the infrastructure computing and processing unit. In this case, vehicles information reside vehicles themselves exclusively. The other extreme version is to assume that the sensing and processing function is completely handled by the infrastructure and the vehicles are only to receive commands or warning signals. A varying degree of allocation of functions will dictate how the IDS systems will operate. Common to all systems under the framework are a number of technical issues and challenges that can be evaluated by inspecting the field observation data. ## III. DESCRIPTION OF FIELD OBSERVATION

Given the objectives stated in the previous section, we devised an experimental plan and procedure with the attempt to acquire data for the evaluation or validation of cooperative operating concepts.

## A. Field Observation Site

The data collection site is an urban intersection in the city of Berkeley located at the crossing of Shattuck and Hearst. This is a site with a fair amount of traffic, with additional mix of pedestrians and bicycles. Figure 2 shows an aerial photograph of the area near the intersection.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/7bd3c8aa-342b-4a4f-8c50-b37e32673187-3.jpg?height=930&width=782&top_left_y=597&top_left_x=260)
Fig. 2 Field Observation Site
The traffic direction of interest to the field observation is in line with the vertical axis of the picture, which is roughly parallel to the north-south orientation. The intersection is controlled by signals. The left turns are permissive in the green phase but the signal cycle does not include a protected left-turn phase.

As illustrated in the figure, the experimental setup was configured to observe the left-turn motions of subject vehicles with a trajectory depicted by the yellow turning curve. The subject vehicle is initially traveling north then turning toward west. As shown in the photograph, there is a left turn pocket for the subject vehicles.

The triangle in the figure roughly represents the coverage area of a radar device used for monitoring the movements of other vehicles in the opposite direction. The radar and associated data acquisition system were stationed inside a van parked at the corner of the intersection. Even though the equipment is not completely inconspicuous to the passing traffic, the intersection is busy enough with all distractions that traffic is minimally affected by the van.

## B. Field Observation Equipment

The monitoring radar and the associated equipment are similar to those used in earlier studies with the addition of a global positioning system. Through the common time stamps of Universal Time (UTC), the collected data can be synchronized with data from other sources.

In addition to the roadside equipment, we also utilized a fully instrumented sedan. The instrumented vehicle is a PATH experimental vehicle that has been used extensively in a number of studies. (12) The vehicle is equipped with radar at its four corners, several cameras facing different directions, a global positioning system, and sensors for detecting the use of steering. brake and throttle.

The GPS unit used on the instrumented vehicle is a CSI Wireless DGPS MAX, which is a high-accuracy GPS receiver that incorporates internal sensors capable of receiving corrections from Space Based Augmentation Systems (SBAS), the worldwide OmniSTAR service, and DGPS beacon stations. When using any of these services, the DGPS MAX provides sub-meter accuracy at position update rates of up to five times per second (5 Hz). The unit provides accuracy and update rates better than most commercial units available on automobiles for navigation purposes.

With the depicted experimental setup, we then instructed a number of drivers to drive through the intersection and to repeat the left turn maneuvers. The GPS location data of the instrumented vehicle is continuously recorded, while roadside radar monitoring is also continuously collected. Through the utilization of the combined data synchronized by the UTC time stamps, the movement of the subject vehicle is available for side-by-side observation of other approaching vehicles.

The data from the field observation represent a potential operating scenario, where partial information (such as the movements of other vehicles detected by radar) are acquired by the infrastructure and partial information is provided by on-board vehicle sensors (such as the GPS coordinates of the subject vehicles). Within a cooperative architecture the vehicles' data is sent to the infrastructure for processing along with other information. The roadside computer then assesses the potential conflicts encountered by SV in this LTAP-OD scenario. The availability of such data allows us not only to investigate the performance requirements of components in a CICAS, but it can also serve as an experimental platform to validate the warning criteria in the target application.

## V. Challenges and issues in safety solutions

The combined utilization of vehicle-based SV trajectories and roadside POV data is not a trivial task. A fundamental premise for an effective CICAS system is that a timely warning can be provided to SV drivers at an appropriate time instant. The appropriateness of the timing not only depends on the selection of proper warning criteria but also relies wholly on solid prediction of SV and POV approaches towards the point of conflict. Some of the basic points were carefully outlined (11), but we will illustrate with descriptive experimental data here.

Figures 9 and 10 respectively show the time elapsed versus the local X and Y coordinates after the SV has moved beyond a distance of 60 meters in the local coordinate system as shown in Figures 5-8. The two graphs are used to show the variations of SV trajectories after it has passed that position. Several observations can be made here:

- A majority of subject vehicles came to a stop, with a stopping position in a wide range between 60 and 80 meters, as revealed by Figure 8.
- Many subject vehicles would creep out from the stopped positions and the creeping motions might proceed in several steps before the actual turn occurred, which isevident from the progression in Y values in Figure 9. Opposing traffic, pedestrians, and signal transition were potential factors that affect the decisions of drivers in completing maneuvers. (13)
- The SV stopping or initiating positions before the turn were scattered over 1-2 lane widths before the turn, as observed in Figure 10.
- The waiting time of a SV from a stopped position to the completion of the left turn took up to 100 seconds, which probably involves the waiting for a complete new signal cycle, even though most SV turns took only 3-6 seconds.

It is worth noting that the aforementioned scenarios are stated with the measurement errors of the GPS sensors included and reflected in data. Since the GPS-unit used for this experiment has relatively high precision and high-update rates, other commercial-off-the-shelf product will perform relatively poorly. These examples demonstrated that position data alone are likely not adequate for the prediction of intersection conflicts in the suggested cooperative framework. To meaningfully assess the threat of POV relative to an approaching SV, other accompanying data such as speed, acceleration, or even steering will be required among the data set that is communicated from SV to the infrastructure.

Alternatively, the infrastructure will need to be equipped with more sophisticated sensors that can generate more revealing status of the subject vehicles.

In general, the potential problems encountered in implementing a vehicle-infrastructure integration approach for sensing and processing include at least the following primary issues:

1. The frequency of input updates and data processing Based on current understanding of driver behaviors observed in field data, it is likely that an alert signal should be provided accurately within a fraction of second. This means that sensor inputs must be available in the order of 0.1 second intervals or thereabout. Consistent with this requirement, communication links between vehicles and the infrastructure station must be executed in the same or smaller time frames. The radar and vehicle data given in this paper are updated every 75 milliseconds. The exact requirement of timing granularity is a topic for continuing studies.
2. The accuracy and consistency of inputs received from infrastructure-based and vehicle-based sensors

The goal of a cooperative system is to use supplementary inputs received from infrastructure sensors and vehicles and to fuse the combined data into an unambiguous set of information for data processing. In reality, however, data packets are bound to be lost, sensors are offset by their error tolerances, and false identification and misses happen. Therefore, in the process of data fusion, robust processing techniques must be implemented to ensure the provision of reliable data sets.

3. Data type and contents communicated between infrastructure and vehicles

Wireless communication is an essential component in a cooperative vehicle infrastructure operating concept. Depending on the operating concept and the allocation of functionality and sub-systems, the message format and contents will vary. Besides the core data contents of engineering data, data encapsulation for security and reliability purposes should also be included in the transmitted message.

## VI. Conclusion

The paper describes recent efforts taken by the authors and their colleagues to explore the viability of data usage in a cooperative vehicle-infrastructure IDS framework. Through synchronized data collection from the roadside and an instrumented vehicle, the issues in designing warning criteria for a collision avoidance system are examined. The data from real-world traffic allow us to gain great insight into the challenges that need to be overcome for the implementation of cooperative IDS systems.

The experimental data acquired in the field observation also constitute an ideal test library for testing potential warning design. Different algorithms of sensor fusion schemes and threat assessment can be evaluated to produce the alert signals for warning. The timing of warning signals can also be compared against the driver behaviors in the observed maneuvers. The in-depth experimentation of these processes and the definition of data requirements under various operating concepts remain the topics of future studies.
