#### Abstract

This paper proposes a cooperative scheduling mechanism for autonomous vehicles passing through an intersection, called TP-AIM. The main objective of this research is to ensure safe driving while minimizing delay in an intersection without traffic lights. Firstly, an intersection management system, used as an info-collecting-organizing center, assigns reasonable priorities for all present vehicles and hence plans their trajectories. Secondly, a window searching algorithm is performed to find an entering window, which can produce a collision-free trajectory with minimal delay, besides backup windows. Finally, vehicles can arrange their trajectory individually, by applying dynamic programming to compute velocity profile, in order to pass through intersection. MATLAB/Simulink and SUMO based simulations are established among three types of traffic mechanisms with different traffic flows. The results show that the proposed TP-AIM mechanism significantly reduces the average evacuation time and increases throughput by over 20\%. Moreover, the paper investigates intersection delay, which can be reduced to less than 10\% compared to classical light management systems. Both safety and efficiency can be guaranteed in our proposed mechanism.


Keywords: Autonomous Intersection Management, Intelligent Transportation Systems, Trajectory Planning, V2X Communications, Dynamic Programming

## 1. Introduction

Due to traffic problems in urban cities, congestions always make people spend more time on road and cause more pollution. In particular, intersections are considered to be highly changing scenes and high-risk places in which accidents may occur. In [1, 2], research data shows the main reasons for traffic accidents among European countries, saying that accidents of intersections accounted for 20\%, which is a relatively high percentage for road traffic system. Therefore, more intelligent control systems for intersections should be explored to improve safety and efficiency.

To be more efficient, using V2X communication could enable intersection to be a collective intelligence center and increase its capacity. A survey of different design principles for traffic control using V2X communication is given in [2, 3]. Vehicle information as velocity, acceleration and vehicle type can be shared, and traffic information as vehicle density, accidents and light signal timing can be acquired and disseminated. A vehicle can be aware of the situation even beyond the line of sight, rather than detecting the environment all by itself. Therefore, an individual autonomous vehicle can be extended into an informationsharing network with movable vehicles as agents.

[^0]In earlier research of traffic coordination methods, the main difference is signalized or non-signalized intersection. With traffic light, it could ensure intersection safety and improve traffic flow, but growing ever-increasing traffic problem is a bottleneck to this signalized model, many significant researchers have paid efforts in adaptive signal control algorithms, where inductive loops are used to detect vehicle's position and request additional green time, including adding predictive models into adaptive signal control systems [4, 5] to forecast vehicle arrivals and estimate queue lengths at intersections. Neural network based algorithm is implemented in [6], a velocity planning algorithm is designed in [7] based on traffic signal information obtained by V2I communication, the idea is to minimize the acceleration/deceleration rates while ensuring full passage through the intersection without a full stop. In a non-signalized intersection, the existing solutions are sorted into two domains, the first one is cooperative resource reservation mechanism for optimizing the passing order (conflict zone resolution), the second one is trajectory planning approaches (velocity profile planning). Note that the roundabouts are typical traffic scene without signals and have the same control algorithms at some points.

For the first part, improvements in conflict zone resolution mechanism, consist of two parts: centralized mechanisms and decentralized mechanisms. As transportation
becomes more complex, a single vehicle couldn't perform efficiently without information exchange. The manager is used as a virtual traffic light. As for the control part in deciding a feasible schedule and assigning resource, there are two ideas: negotiation-based control[8, 9, 10] and planning-based control[11, 12, 13, 14]. The former consumes fewer computation resources because only the vehicles near the intersection are taken into account, whereas the latter sometimes can achieve higher performance. In [9], a first-come-first-serve policy is used, and every vehicle must send a reservation message to the intersection manager, then the latter checks availability of space. Based on a set of rules, if one car's reservation is not in conflict with the reservations of others, the car is allowed to get through the intersection. Otherwise, the car has to generate and send new request messages while decelerating until it gets the permission from the manager. In [15], using bufferassignment based coordinated control method for vehicle's trajectory. In [13], an incoming vehicle requests the state information from the manager, then discrete mathematics is used to plan a safe trajectory to pass the intersection; the related message protocol is also designed. CACC-VI algorithm in [14] is designed to improve the throughput within the current green time by taking advantage of the redundant road capacity and reorganizing the platoons.

Others such as centralized model with predictive control [16, 17] offer integrated scheduling and trajectory planning for multiple vehicles. In [18], a new centralized reservationoriented scheduling algorithm is proposed in state-driven scheduling way. In these methods, efficiency and safety are both valuable objectives and are closely correlated for a good balance between these criteria [19].

A decentralized mechanism is suitable for small intersections, most researches are known for non-signalized ones. It remains uncertain when vehicle flow extended to a high level, it would come to have dead-lock failure or not. However, some dead-lock free cases had been discussed in [20], a distributed conflict resolution mechanism via vehicular network was proposed to solve multi-vehicle navigation problem [21]. It abandoned "First-Come-First-Serve" policy in decision-making process to avoid deadlock, when one vehicle arrives earlier in conflict zone.

To make some improvements in control strategy for vehicle trajectory planning problem, there are two ways, one is platoon-based control strategy[22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27], in which time plan of traffic lights is set referring to the information of platoons coming from different directions so that they can get through the intersection without being interrupted. If V2X device is integrated into the platoon, the information can be sent directly to the intersection manager instead of identifying the platoon by inductive loops or other sensors [26]. In [22], an arterial traffic control algorithm for intersections is proposed, which is capable of recognizing the vehicle platoons and of determining future optimal signal plans based on the current traffic controller status, on-line platoon data and priority requests from special vehicles, such as transit buses. Several methods can be used to plan velocity profile for vehicles approaching the intersection [28], including tree search [29], mixed-integer linear programming [30] and elastic bands [31]. In [32], a geometric concept in the coordination diagram is proposed for trajectory planning and also priority assignment. If the vehicles' motion is planed simultaneously, the computational cost increases exponentially with the number of vehicles. Thus prioritized planning is developed [33] to decompose the problem into lower-dimension problems. And the problem can also be decomposed in time scale using partial motion planning (PMP) if the complete motion to the goal cannot be found within the available time [34].

In this paper, after acquiring information from manager, we use a window searching algorithm to find an entering window with no collision and minimal delay. Then the vehicle begins to plan its trajectory using a segmented dynamic programming algorithm based on the decided entering window and the future motions of the preceding vehicle.

Another way is cooperative driving, in which the intersection controller can receive the vehicles' requests to get through the intersection, and give each vehicle the advice of an optimal course in consideration of their dynamic capacity of action ensuring no crash occurs, at the same time minimizing the intersection delay and fuel consumption [8, 9, 10, 15, 12, 14]. Besides, one vehicle can join in or depart from an already formed platoon; and several vehicles can also choose to form a new one[14]. By doing this, space tiles and time slots would be allocated in an efficient way according to the reservation. Other works on cooperative resource reservation consist of conducting simulation and prototyping. In [35], one real vehicle, a number of virtual vehicles and a simulation platform are set up as a realistic scenario without signals. In [36], it investigates a simulation platform with mini vehicles equipped with 802.11g Wi-Fi for communication.

In this paper, a hybrid strategy of cooperative resource reservation and planning-based algorithms is proposed. The vehicles pass through the cross as a platoon passes at a cruising mode with a constant velocity, and the occupation of the conflict zones is reservation-based. The cooperative resource reservation part gives rules to each vehicle approaching an intersection, in order to find the appropriate time and velocity of entering the cross. Each vehicle plans its velocity according to the results of reservation and the velocity profile of the vehicle ahead. In this stage, a trajectory planning-based part, segmented Dynamic Programming is used to search an optimal velocity profile before vehicles cross conflict zones. From a theoretical viewpoint, it has a combination with cooperative resource reservation method: priority-setting in a distributed way and centralized tile-assignment residing at infrastructure center, that is to say, a distributed conflict resolution mechanism can extend traffic intersection to be adjustable in real time and can determine order of requesting space tile by various types and current states of vehicle themselves. From an applicative viewpoint, in order to
accommodate a certain level of vehicle heterogeneity, five types are considered in the simulation part. Besides, it is adjustable to multiple vehicles from multiple directions.

The contributions are as follows: a model of an intersection with V2V communication is established, cooperative resource reservation and an intersection management mechanism (TP-AIM) is designed for trajectory planning. Instead of traffic lights, a manager is installed as an information hub, it can collect all vehicles info and send conflict zone occupancy time (CZOT). Simulations are established between three types of traffic mechanisms: TP- AIM, fixed traffic lights, and adaptive traffic lights. By evaluating simulation results in evacuation time, delay and throughput capacity, we could see that the proposed TP-AIM mechanism significantly outperformed others.

Firstly, when entering the V2I range, intersection manager can assign a priority, which is defined by each one's driving info (including types, headings and distances to the intersection), to every encountered vehicle. Then vehicles can send their requests to the manager, and get a response back with respect to their priorities. After receiving the response, the vehicle itself uses a window searching algorithm to find an entering time and velocity to enter the intersection with no collision and minimal delay. Then based on the latter and the future motions of the preceding vehicle, the vehicle begins to plan velocity profile using a segmented dynamic programming algorithm. Backup entering windows are ready to restart trajectory planning, in case of any incident or failure with the intersection manager, or if the vehicle cannot find a feasible velocity profile within its dynamical limits.

Secondly, TP-AIM is tested and verified using an open source traffic simulator SUMO [37] under different traffic densities. Finally, the results prove that TP-AIM greatly outperforms traditional traffic lights and adaptive traffic lights, improving efficiency and ensuring safety at the same time. Note that the proposed criteria describe the planning methods for finding an optimal trajectory, deployment-related requirements (e.g. communication quality, packet loss, applied sensor setup) are not the focus of this paper.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In section 2, the scenario of our research is described, and the control objectives are established. In section 3, our algorithm system, including priority setting, window searching algorithm and trajectory planning algorithm, is introduced in greater details. In section 4, simulation results are presented to prove the validation of our algorithm, and comparisons are made with other adaptive traffic lights and fixed lights cases. Finally, some concluding remarks and perspectives are given in Section 5.

### 2.1. Intersection

In this paper, a symmetrical four-legged intersection, which has three incoming lanes and three outgoing lanes, is studied and shown in Figure 1. For incoming direction, lane on the left is for left turning, the middle one is for straight direction and lane on the right is for both straight direction and right turning. This type of road represents a standard high-speed road for real transportation system.

The area where collision of vehicles coming from different lanes may happen is defined as the "conflict zones". In this intersection model, we have 36 conflict zones in total. The width of a conflict zone equals the lane width, which is denoted as $w$. The vehicles are diverse not only in routes, but also in types, lengths, acceleration and deceleration abilities. It is assumed that there is no lane shifting within the V2I range.

### 2.2. Assumptions and Objectives

For traditional traffic light, only lanes without conflict can be given green lights simultaneously: the vehicles on a lane A must totally stop before the red light to give way to another lane B to avoid collisions. This is an effective protocol to solve the time and space conflict at the intersection. However, it is not efficient and not fair enough: a vehicle on lane A may have to wait even if it arrives earlier than some vehicles on lane B. Besides, compared to cruising, more fuel is consumed during this stop-idling-start process.

Under connected vehicles environment and autonomous intersection management, there are many different types of vehicles and directions they would take, dynamic programming is an appropriate and more adaptive method.

It is assumed that typical vehicles are all replaced by autonomous vehicles, and the traffic light is replaced by an intersection manager. The manager is capable of communicating with the vehicles within its range $R$. And the vehicles are all equipped with V2X devices so that they can exchange necessary information with each other and with the manager. The network connection is activated all the time and with negligible transmission delay, packet loss, interference, etc. It is assumed that there is no lane shifting within the V2I range. In this paper, a new mechanism for autonomous intersection management is developed with the following design objectives:

- heterogeneity of vehicles must be considered;
- the decision should be made locally at each vehicle in order to reduce the computational cost at the intersection manager;
- entering windows for each vehicle indicating the possible moments and velocities to enter the intersection needs to be found in order to avoid cross collisions;
- a vehicle's trajectory before entering the intersection must be planned to avoid rear-end collision and to match with the entering window;
- fail-safe mode should be designed to deal with situations where the velocity profile planning cannot find a feasible solution for the given entering window;
- the average delay of vehicles should be reduced.

## 3. Algorithm Design

### 3.1. Overview

An overview of TP-AIM mechanism is given in Figure 2. When approaching an intersection, before entering the V2I range, a vehicle circulates according to its implemented self-driving algorithm; after getting inside the range, the vehicle demands the intersection manager for the related conflict zone occupancy time (CZOT), which is defined in the following subsection. The manager processes these requests in a decided order: the vehicles which have proposed the demand are divided into service groups for which the priority is given by the vehicles' types, headings, and distances to the intersection. A vehicle can only get the CZOT information after its related vehicles, which shares conflict zones with it, in the previous group have finished the trajectory planning process.

Given the related CZOT, the vehicle should utilize the window searching algorithm (in section 3.3) to find an optimal entering moment and velocity which lead to a collisionfree trajectory inside the intersection with minimal delay, as well as backup solutions which are also collision free but have larger delays. It is supposed that vehicles circulate at a constant speed in the intersection.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/c44724e6-a32a-470f-aa80-a79cea92636b-06.jpg?height=905&width=870&top_left_y=270&top_left_x=1030)
Figure 2: Overview of TP-AIM

After that, the vehicle needs to plan its own trajectory based on its current position, velocity and its acceleration/deceleration ability to enter the intersection at the decided entering moment with the correct entering velocity. On one hand, if the vehicle currently has a preceding vehicle on the same lane, the former should request the latter for future motions which have been planned; then a Segmented Dynamic Programming algorithm (in section 3.4.1) is used to plan actions in order to achieve intermediate goals and to avoid running into the preceding vehicle. On the other hand, if the vehicle is the first one on its lane, it should use a three-section acceleration template (in section 3.4.2) to plan its velocity profile. If the trajectory planning succeeds, the vehicle must send a message to the manager and the CZOT it has requested is formally determined. If the trajectory planning could not find feasible inputs within the vehicle's dynamic limits, in other words, the vehicle cannot exploit the current entering window, it should enter later. Thus the requested CZOT should be canceled and a backup solution of the window searching is used to restart the trajectory planning process.

### 3.2. Definitions

### 3.2.1. Conflict Zone Occupation Time

The cross collision is defined as collision inside the intersection coming from vehicles on different lanes. For vehicle $V_{i}$, the set of all the conflict zones which it will pass through is defined as $S C Z_{i}$; the distance from the intersection's entrance to the conflict zone $C Z_{k}$ in $S C Z_{i}$ is denoted as $d_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}$; its velocity inside the intersection is
denoted as $v_{i}$ and its length is denoted as $l_{i}$. If we note the moment at which $V_{i}$ enters the intersection as $t_{\text {enter }}$, then the moment when $V_{i}$ enters and leaves $C Z_{k}$, which are separately denoted as $t_{1, V_{i}}$ and $t_{2, V_{i}}$, can be defined as follows

$$
\begin{aligned}
& t_{1, V_{i}}=t_{\text {enter }}+\frac{d_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}}{v_{i}} \\
& t_{2, V_{i}}=t_{\text {enter }}+\frac{d_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}+\delta_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}\left(l_{i}, w\right)}{v_{i}}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\delta_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}\left(l_{i}, w\right)$ is the distance to be covered by $V_{i}$ to get through $C Z_{k}$, which is related to $V_{i}$ 's heading and the position of $C Z_{k}$ in the intersection.

In reality, the noise of sensor and error of actuators cause bias in position and velocity measurement, and the road traffic does not demand extreme accuracy; while in simulation, the discretization of time causes errors in position and velocity as well. These inaccuracies cannot be ignored for vehicles moving at high speed. Therefore a time buffer $t_{b}$ is added on the original entering and leaving moment:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& t_{1, V_{i}}^{\prime}=t_{1, V_{i}}-t_{b} \\
& t_{2, V_{i}}^{\prime}=t_{2, V_{i}}+t_{b} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore the conflict zone occupancy time (CZOT) for $V_{i}$ at $C Z_{k}$ can be defined as

$$
O T_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}=\left[t_{1, V_{i}}^{\prime}, t_{2, V_{i}}^{\prime}\right] .
$$

In the same way, the conflict zone occupancy time for another vehicle $V_{j}$ at $C Z_{k}$ can be defined. The overlapped occupancy time (OOT) for $V_{i}$ and $V_{j}$ at $C Z_{k}$ can be calculated as following:

$$
\begin{aligned}
O O T_{C Z_{k}, V_{i}, V_{j}} & =f_{O O T}\left(O T_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}, O T_{V_{j}, C Z_{k}}\right) \\
& =\max \left(0, \min \left(t_{2, V_{i}}^{\prime}, t_{2, V_{j}}^{\prime}\right)-\max \left(t_{1, V_{i}}^{\prime}, t_{1, V_{j}}^{\prime}\right)\right) .
\end{aligned}
$$

If $O O T$ is bigger than $0, V_{i}$ and $V_{j}$ will collide at $C Z_{k}$, which is noted as $V_{i} \stackrel{C Z_{k}}{\nLeftarrow} V_{\mathcal{J}}$. The set of vehicles which have conflict with $V_{i}$ at $C Z_{k}$ in cross collision type is denoted as $S_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}$ :

$$
S_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}=\left\{V_{j} \mid V_{j} \stackrel{C Z_{k}}{\nLeftarrow} V_{i}\right\} .
$$

It should be indicated that the CZOT stored at the manager is anonymous and only related to conflict zone index. The occupancy time of conflict zone $C Z_{k}$ is defined as:

$$
C Z O T\left(C Z_{k}\right)=\left\{O T_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}} \mid \forall V_{i}\right\} .
$$

### 3.2.2. Set partition

The vehicles on the incoming street within V2I range can be divided into three different sets:

- Free Set $S_{F}$ : The vehicles which have demanded the CZOT, but have not got the response from the manager.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/c44724e6-a32a-470f-aa80-a79cea92636b-07.jpg?height=306&width=891&top_left_y=260&top_left_x=1019)
Figure 3: Phases for 4 serving groups in $S_{F}^{\prime}$

- Planning Set $S_{P}$ : The vehicles which have obtained the response from the manager, and they are trying to find a feasible and collision-free trajectory to get through the intersection.
- Cruising Set $S_{C}$ : The vehicles which have found their trajectories to get through the intersection, and their CZOT is stored at the intersection.

On the same lane, if we sort the vehicles on an incoming street by the distance to the intersection, $S_{C}$ has the smallest distance, then it comes to $S_{P}$ and finally $S_{F}$. The number of vehicles in $S_{C}$ and $S_{F}$ is not limited. Meanwhile, there is at most one vehicle for each lane in $S_{P}$. Because the CZOT of a vehicle can be decided if and only if it has found a trajectory, then it becomes a member of $S_{C}$, and its nearest following vehicle can get the response from the manager and become a member of $S_{P}$.

Note that, when one vehicle is entering V2I range, setting partition to it means that manager will respond in turn based on this given partition, then, among those vehicles in Free Set $S_{F}$, the manager will assign CZOT with respect to priority-setting, which is discussed in following.

### 3.2.3. Service Priority

If two or more vehicles of Free Set $\left(S_{F}\right)$ are demanding CZOT from the manager at the same time, vehicles should wait in turns to get the response. Because the manager could only respond to only one vehicle underlying the current CZOT information. Thus, defining service priority is considered as giving an order in requesting CZOT for those in $S_{F}$. Otherwise, serving two vehicles at one time would give an incomplete and incorrect decision.

For example, vehicle $V_{i}$ and $V_{j}$ send their demands simultaneously, and the two vehicles have conflict zone in common; the manager cannot answer them at the same time using the current incomplete CZOT information, because the two vehicles are related. Therefore, the two vehicles must be responded one by one, the first one only need to consider the current CZOT while the second vehicle has to take the previous into account as well. Thus a fair and efficient service priority needs to be defined.

In Figure 3, we have following steps about how to define service priority based on vehicle $V_{i}$ 's total score $n_{\text {total }, V_{i}}$.

Before defining, we illustrate an additional set $S_{F}^{\prime}$ should be discriminated from Free Set $S_{F}$. As it is assumed that there is no lane-shifting and overtaking operation within
the V2I range, which means a vehicle must get through the intersection before its following ones on the same lane. Thus, for calculating each one's total score, a first-comefirst-served principle is used. In other words, it is sufficient to only take the first vehicle in the free set $S_{F}$ on each lane into consideration at a time, and this subset of $S_{F}$ is denoted as $S_{F}^{\prime}$. There are at most 12 vehicles in $S_{F}^{\prime}$.

As for defining service priority, firstly, the vehicles in $S_{F}^{\prime}$ is divided into 4 groups inspired by four phases of traditional traffic lights. Underlying such division, two objectives could be satisfied: one is that each group has the biggest set of collision-free headings, and vehicles can circulate simultaneously; the other is that vehicles of the same group are independent and they can get their requested CZOT at the same time. Secondly, according to their distances to the intersection, headings, and types, the vehicles in $S_{F}^{\prime}$ are given scores noted as $n_{\text {total }, V_{i}}$, which is the sum of $n_{d}, n_{h}$ and $n_{t}$. The principles are listed as follows:

1. Vehicles which have smaller distances to the intersection get a higher score in $n_{d}$;
2. Vehicles turning left have a higher score in $n_{h}$ than vehicles going straight and turning right because turning left needs to occupy more conflict zones;
3. Emergency vehicles get the highest score in $n_{t}$, then heavy-duty vehicles like trucks and buses get a higher score than others like sedans and hatchbacks.

Thirdly, the groups' serving priority is sorted according to the maximal score in the group. Theoretically speaking, we define multiple vehicles coming from multiple lanes into different groups, the group having the highest priority is denoted as $G_{1}$, then it comes to $G_{2}, G_{3}$ and $G_{4}$. The manager should send the requested CZOT information to vehicles in $G_{1}$ at first. Then vehicles of $G_{1}$ begin to search for the entering windows and plan the trajectories. After having found a feasible collision-free trajectory, the vehicle sends a confirmation message to the manager. The manager does not have to wait for all the definite answer from $G_{1}$ to begin to serve the members of $G_{2}$. If all the related CZOT of a vehicle in $G_{2}$ is decided, then it can be served before all the vehicles in $G_{1}$ have found a solution. After all the vehicles in the current $S_{F}^{\prime}$ have found their trajectories, $S_{F}^{\prime}$ is updated to their nearest following vehicles in $S_{F}$, then the manager begins to repeat the three steps to find the priority to serve the new $S_{F}^{\prime}$.

### 3.3. Window Search

The nature of intersection management is to solve the space-time conflict so that the vehicles can share the limited space resource inside the intersection. When a vehicle is replied by the manager, it has the occupancy time of all the conflict zones that it will pass through. The conflict zones that the vehicles with different headings will occupy is shown in Figure 4. The set of conflict zones to be occupied when vehicle $V_{i}$ goes through the intersection is noted as $S C Z_{i}$.

If we unfold the CZOT in the time dimension, the occupancy of a conflict zone within a duration can be seen as obstacles. Finding an entering window can be interpreted as finding a trajectory to bypass these obstacles. For example, for a vehicle going straight, $C Z O T\left(C Z_{k}\right)$ is unfolded as a line of grids in Figure 5. The distance axis begins from the entrance of the intersection $p_{e}$, and each segment represents the width $w$ of a conflict zone. The time axis begins from the lower boundary $t_{l b}$ of the searching time interval which is defined later and each segment on this axis represents the protocol's sampling time $T_{s}$. The black grids mean that the related conflict zone is occupied by other vehicles during the indicated time, and the occupancy is already stored at the manager, so it can-
not be changed. Conversely, the blank grids indicate that the conflict zone is available so that it can be occupied by the host vehicle.

Obviously, the vehicles in $S_{C}$ are countable, so their CZOT stored at the manager is not infinite, therefore at least the host vehicle can get inside the intersection after all the vehicles in $S_{C}$ having done occupying the zones in $S C Z_{i}$. In this case, if the vehicle enters the intersection in the speed limit, it would be minimally delayed.

However, the vehicle could insert earlier by taking advantage of the available grids. That's why the window searching algorithm is designed. As assumed before, the vehicles circulate in a constant speed in the intersection, so the vehicle should maintain the entering velocity until it totally gets through. Thus according to the entering time and velocity, the vehicles' occupancy time at different conflict zones can be predicted. The objective is to search for earlier entering windows, including entering time and velocity, which produces a collision-free trajectory in the intersection, in other words, the grids that the vehicle plans to occupy should not overlap with the black grids.

For a pair of entering time and velocity noted as ( $t_{\text {enter }}$, $\left.v_{\text {enter }}\right)$, the occupancy time $O T_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}$ of $C Z_{k} \in S C Z_{i}$ can be deduced using Equ.(1) and Equ.(2). Then the overlapped occupancy time with $C Z O T\left(C Z_{k}\right)$ can be calculated in the same way as Equ.(4):

$$
O O T_{C Z_{k}, V_{i}}=\sum_{O T_{j} \in C Z O T\left(C Z_{k}\right)} f_{O O T}\left(O T_{V_{i}, C Z_{k}}, O T_{j}\right) .
$$

where $O T_{j}$ is a successive occupancy time in $C Z O T\left(C Z_{k}\right)$. Then the total OOT for all $C Z_{k} \in S C Z_{i}$ is defined as:

$$
O O T_{\text {total }, V_{i}}=\sum_{C Z_{k} \in S C Z_{i}} O O T_{C Z_{k}, V_{i}} .
$$

If $O O T_{\text {total }, V_{i}}$ equals to 0, then $V_{i}$ will not collide other vehicles when it enters at $t_{\text {enter }}$ with the velocity $v_{\text {enter }}$. Otherwise, the pair $\left(t_{\text {enter }}, v_{\text {enter }}\right)$ is not a safe entering window for $V_{i}$.

Another objective is to minimize the delay of vehicles, and it can be expressed as a minimized exiting time $t_{\text {exit }}$ for getting out of the intersection, which is also the function for $t_{\text {enter }}$ and $v_{\text {enter }}$. Combining these two objectives, a cost function can be defined:

$$
J_{\text {cost }, V_{i}}\left(t_{\text {enter }} \cdot v_{\text {enter }}\right)=k_{1} \cdot O O T_{\text {total }, V_{i}}+k_{2} \cdot\left(t_{\text {exit }}-t_{0}\right),
$$

where $t_{0}$ is the current moment, and $k_{1}, k_{2}$ are the weights of the two parts. As safety is the first priority, $k_{1}$ should be much bigger than $k_{2}$.

The searching area needs to be bounded to get close to reality and to produce solutions which have a higher success rate in trajectory planning. The lower boundary $t_{l b}$ of $t_{\text {enter }}$ is defined as:

$$
t_{l b}=\max \left(t_{\text {fullspeed }}, t_{\text {enter }, \text { preced }}\right),
$$

where $t_{\text {fullspeed }}$ is the moment when the vehicle enters the intersection if it accelerates to the road's speed limit, and $t_{\text {enter,preced }}$ is the entering time of the nearest preceding vehicle. $t_{l b}$ is set like so because the vehicle cannot enter the intersection earlier than using the speed limit or before its preceding vehicle. The upper boundary $t_{u b}$ of $t_{\text {enter }}$ is defined as the moment when all the conflict zones in $S C Z_{i}$ have not occupied afterward:

$$
t_{u b}=\max _{C Z_{k} \in S C Z_{i}} \max \left(C Z O T\left(C Z_{k}\right)\right) .
$$

Then a lower boundary $v_{l b}$ should be given to $v_{\text {enter }}$ in order to guarantee the traffic's fluency inside the intersection. And the upper boundary $v_{u b}$ of $v_{\text {enter }}$ is defined as the road's speed limit $v_{\text {limit }}$.

Thus the window searching problem is transformed to a two-dimension optimization problem, of which the objective is to find the value of an entering window $\left(t_{\text {enter }}, v_{\text {enter }}\right)$ within constraints that produces the minimal $J_{\text {cost }, V_{i}}$. Several optimization algorithms can be used to solve this problem. In this paper, we utilize the Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) Algorithm[38] because of its simplicity in exploration and exploitation.

During the searching process, all the found tuples of $\left(t_{\text {enter }}, v_{\text {enter }}\right)$ having zero overlapped occupancy time are recorded in the vehicle's cache as backup solutions, and the optimal solution would be attempted at first for trajectory planning. If the planning fails to find a feasible trajectory, then a backup solution is used to restart the planning. If window searching algorithm cannot find any collisionfree solution, it means that there is no chance to enter the intersection earlier and the vehicle should insert using the speed limit after all vehicles in $S_{C}$ having got out of its way. An example of the optimal solution and backup solutions can be referred to Figure 5.

### 3.4. Trajectory Planning

After finding entering windows, the vehicle needs to plan its trajectory before entering the intersection according to these windows. Two different algorithms are designed for two situations the vehicles can encounter: with/without a preceding vehicle in the same lane. For the first situation, vehicle's velocity profile is planned by a segmented dynamic programming algorithm, velocity profile can be seen as its' trajectory ; while for the second situation, a three-section acceleration template is used. Normally, these two situations happen successively: the preceding vehicle enters the intersection during the planning scope, so the two algorithms need to be deployed one after the other. It is assumed that the whole planning process can be finished in a computation period $\delta_{c}$. During $\delta_{c}$, the vehicle circulates according to the self-driving algorithm and the position and velocity at $t_{0}+\delta_{c}$ are assumed to be predictable.

### 3.4.1. With preceding vehicle

If there is a preceding vehicle $V_{j}$ before $V_{i}$, then the first goal of $V_{i}$ is to avoid rear-end collision with $V_{j}$, and catching up with the decided entering window becomes the second. In this situation, the vehicle should plan its trajectory from the moment $t_{0}+\delta_{c}$ to $t_{\text {enter,preced }}$, when the preceding vehicle enters the intersection.

The maximum acceleration and deceleration of vehicle $V_{i}$ are separately denoted as $a_{\text {max }}$ and $b_{\text {max }}$. It is assumed that the $V_{i}$ can choose an acceleration to execute, called an action, from several values between the two limits. The action set $A_{i}$ of $V_{i}$ is defined as:

$$
A_{i}=\left\{a_{i, 1}, a_{i, 2}, \ldots, a_{i, n}\right\} \text {, where } a_{i, j} \in\left[-b_{\max }, a_{\max }\right] \text {. }
$$

For different vehicles, the dimension $\left\|A_{i}\right\|$ of action set can also be different. Since the preceding vehicle $V_{j}$ 's trajectory has been successfully planned, the host vehicle $V_{i}$ can request necessary information via V2V to predict $V_{j}$ 's position and velocity in the future.

The vehicle's velocity profile can be planned using dynamic programming (DP) [13], because this problem can be simplified by breaking it down into a sequence of decision steps over time, and a sub-problem can be solved recursively by using the results of its previous steps. And the results of overlapping subproblems are memorized and looked up subsequently. As shown in Figure 6, each node represents a state ( $p, v$ ) including a position and a velocity. The planning begins with an initial state ( $p_{0}, v_{0}$ ), and terminates with a target state $\left(p_{\text {target }}, v_{\text {target }}\right)$. Each node has $\left\|A_{i}\right\|$ actions to produce new states at every step, but not all the child-nods are qualified: the state that a childnode represents may be out of the velocity's limit or may cause collision with the preceding vehicle, so it should be deleted and cannot produce new child-nodes. The nodes produced at the final step are called the final states. The objective is to find the final state which has the smallest error to the target state. The nodes belong to different layers, if a node is the $j$ th in the same layer and behind it there are $N$ steps to be planned afterwards, then this node is noted as $n_{N, j}$, and the set of all its qualified childnodes is noted as $C N\left(n_{N, j}\right)$. If two nodes at the same layer have the same state, their child-nodes will be exactly the same as well. Thus they can be regarded as overlapping subproblems.

If DP is deployed directly using $T_{s}$ as the step length, it could be considerably expensive in computation. For example, if we need to plan the velocity profile for the future $20 s, T_{s}=0.2 s$ and $\left\|A_{i}\right\|=5$, by using DP, there are 100 steps to be planned, and the number of nodes at the last step is $O\left(5^{100}\right)$. Therefore, it is supposed that an action is maintained during the planning step $\Delta T$, which is an integer multiple of $T_{s}$. The total number of steps to be planned is defined as:

$$
N_{\text {step }}=\operatorname{ceil}\left(\frac{t_{\text {enter,preced }}-t_{0}-\delta_{c}}{\Delta T}\right),
$$

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/c44724e6-a32a-470f-aa80-a79cea92636b-10.jpg?height=646&width=866&top_left_y=272&top_left_x=1019)
Figure 6: Planning velocity profile using Dynamic programming

where $\operatorname{ceil}(x)$ is the function which returns the smallest integer larger than or equal to $x$. The target state of DP is defined as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& p_{\text {target }}=p_{\text {enter }}-v_{\text {enter }} \cdot\left(t_{\text {enter }}-\Delta T \cdot N_{\text {step }}-t_{0}-\delta c\right) \\
& v_{\text {target }}=v_{\text {enter }}
\end{aligned}
$$

And it represents an ideal case: when the preceding vehicle enters the intersection, the host vehicle can reach $v_{\text {enter }}$ and it does not have to make an adjustment before $t_{\text {enter }}$.

If $\Delta T$ is too big, the error between the final state and the target could be large; whereas if $\Delta T$ is too small, $N_{\text {step }}$ is still big which leads to high computational cost. Therefore, a segmented dynamic programming is designed to solve this dilemma: the original DP process is divided into several successive and smaller DP process with limited steps to compute and intermediate goals to achieve. For the same example defined previously, if $\Delta T=2 s$, then $N_{\text {step }}=10$; if the original DP is divided into 2 successive DP of 5 steps, then the number of nodes in final states are $O\left(5^{5}\right)$. Thus the computational time can be greatly reduced compared to the one in the previous example.

The number of steps in the $i$ th segmented DP is defined as $N_{i}$, and the maximum number of steps in a segmentation is denoted as $N_{\text {max }}$,

$$
\sum N_{i}=N_{\text {step }}, N_{i} \leqslant N_{\max } .
$$

The moments in $i$ th segmented DP are noted as $t_{i, N_{i}}, t_{i, N_{i}-1} \ldots t_{i, 1}, t_{i, 0}$. Then the velocity and position of the child-nodes are examined by

$$
\begin{aligned}
0 & \leqslant v\left(t_{i, j+1}\right) \leqslant v_{\text {limit }}, t_{i, j}-t_{i, j+1}=\Delta T \\
p(t) & <p_{\text {preced }}(t)-l_{\text {preced }}, \forall t \in\left[t_{i, j+1}, t_{i, j}\right] .
\end{aligned}
$$

where $p_{\text {preced }}$ is the position of the preceding vehicle, and $l_{\text {preced }}$ is its length. The child-nodes which are outside
these limits should be deleted and they cannot generate new child-nodes in the next step.

The intermediate target of the whole DP is the target of $i$ th segmented DP, which is defined as followings

$$
\begin{gathered}
p_{\text {temp }}=p_{0}+\frac{\sum_{j \leq i} N_{j}}{N_{\text {step }}} \cdot\left(p_{\text {target }}-p_{0}\right) \\
p_{\text {target }, i}=\min \left(p_{\text {preced }}\left(t_{i, 0}\right)-l_{\text {preced }}, p_{\text {temp }}\right) \\
v_{\text {temp }}=v_{0}+\frac{\sum_{j \leq i} N_{j}}{N_{\text {step }}} \cdot\left(v_{\text {target }}-v_{0}\right) \\
v_{\text {target }, i}=\min \left(v_{\text {preced }}\left(t_{i, 0}\right), v_{\text {temp }}\right)
\end{gathered}
$$

This definition is an integrated consideration of the final target and the motion of the preceding vehicle.

If $n_{N_{i}, j}$ is a node which represents a final state of the $i$ th segmented DP, thus $N_{i}=0$, then its cost $C_{0, j}$ is defined as

$$
C_{0, j}=\omega_{1} \cdot \operatorname{abs}\left(p_{0, j}-p_{\text {target }, i}\right)+\omega_{2} \cdot \operatorname{abs}\left(v_{0, j}-v_{\text {target }, i}\right),
$$

where $p_{\text {target }, i}$ and $v_{\text {target, } i}$ are the target position and velocity of the $i$ th segmented DP, and $\omega_{1}$ and $\omega_{2}$ are the weights of position and velocity error. This cost is called the terminal cost.

Otherwise, for a node with $N_{i}>0$, its cost is defined as

$$
C_{N_{i}, j}=\min \left(\left\{C_{N_{i}-1, k}+\operatorname{abs}\left(a_{N_{i}, k}\right) \mid n_{N_{i}-1, k} \in C N\left(n_{N_{i}, j}\right)\right\}\right)
$$

$$
a_{N_{i}, m}=\arg \min \left(\left\{C_{N_{i}-1, k}+\operatorname{abs}\left(a_{N_{i}, k}\right)\right\}\right),
$$

where $n_{N_{i}-1, k}$ is a child-node of $n_{N_{i}, j}$, and $a_{N_{i}, k}$ is the action used to produce it. The term $\operatorname{abs}\left(a_{N_{i}, k}\right)$ is defined as the control cost.

By calculating the cost of the node at the initial state, an action profile with minimal cumulated control cost can be found which can lead to a node having a small error to the target state. Then the action profiles of segmented DP are connected successively to generate vehicle's velocity profile from $t_{0}+\delta_{c}$ to $t_{\text {enter,preced }}$.

### 3.4.2. Without preceding vehicle

Normally, this situation happens when the preceding vehicle has entered the intersection. Because of the existence of the preceding vehicle and the limited action set, a vehicle may not reach the target state $\left(p_{\text {target }}, v_{\text {target }}\right)$, so adjustment should be done to catch up with the entering window $\left(t_{\text {enter }}, v_{\text {enter }}\right)$. In this case, the vehicle only needs to consider the entering window because the preceding vehicle's occupancy time is already considered in the window searching process, so there will not be any rear-end collision.

A three-section acceleration template is designed to plan the vehicle's velocity profile from $t_{\text {enter }, \text { preced }}$ to $t_{\text {enter }}$. It is assumed that the velocity profile for this duration has three sections: acceleration, cruise, and deceleration, or deceleration, cruise and acceleration. A uniform acceleration is considered to simplify the planning process. The first and the third section have the same value of acceleration, and the duration of the three sections are separately $T_{1}, T_{2}$ and $T_{3}$. The acceleration value should be minimized in order to reduce the fuel consumption. Then the velocity profile planning problem can be translated into solving the following optimization program:

$$
\text { minimize }|a|
$$

with constraints:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& t_{\text {enter,preced }}+T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}=t_{\text {enter }} \\
& v_{\text {final }}+a \cdot\left(T_{1}-T_{3}\right)=v_{\text {enter }} \\
& d\left(v_{\text {final }}, a, T_{1}\right)+d\left(v_{\text {final }}+a \cdot T_{1}, 0, T_{2}\right) \\
&+d\left(v_{\text {final }}+a \cdot T_{1},-a, T_{3}\right)=p_{\text {enter }}-p_{\text {final }}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\left(p_{\text {final }}, v_{\text {final }}\right)$ is the optimal final state given by the segmented DP, and $d(v, a, t)$ is the distance covered by the vehicle with initial speed $v$ and acceleration $a$, during time $t$. The constraints Equ.(23), Equ.(24) and Equ.(25) are separately the constraints of time, velocity and position. Equ.(26) and Equ.(27) are the speed limit and acceleration limit.

If a value of $a$ can be found within the constraints, then the velocity profile from $t_{0}+\delta_{c}$ to $t_{\text {enter }}$ is successfully planned, and the vehicle can catch up with the current entering window. The vehicle should send a confirmation message to the manager, and its related CZOT is stored. Otherwise, the vehicle is not capable to take advantage of the entering window, therefore it should enter the intersection later. A backup solution in its cache is used to restart the velocity profile planning process.

### 4.2. Results

Comparisons are made between the same intersection controlled separately by fixed-period traffic lights, adaptive traffic lights[26] and our TP-AIM mechanism in different scenarios. In the following, we use "Fixed Lights", "Adaptive Lights" and "TP-AIM" to represent these three methods. For Fixed Lights, the length of green light for straight direction and right turning is $25 s$, for left turning is $20 s$, and the yellow light between two phases is $2 s$. Therefore, the phase period of Fixed Lights is $98 s$. The generated traffic flows for these three methods are exactly the same. The performance of different methods are evaluated by the following criteria:

1. Evacuation time: the least time during which all the generated vehicles in the first $100 s$ get through the intersection.
2. Throughput: the ratio between the number of vehi- cles getting through the intersection and the number of vehicles entering the V2I range on the incoming streets during a whole signal period of Fixed Lights. It should be clarified that there is no V2I communication in Fixed Lights, so its "V2I range" is virtual and it is just used for statistic.
3. Delay: the increase in travel time due to the presence of intersection. Travel time is defined as the period between the moment when vehicle gets through the intersection and the moment when vehicle enters the V2I range. Then delay can be calculated as the difference between the real travel time under intersection control and the estimated travel time if the vehicle travels at the speed limit for the entire journey.

In Figure 7, it can be found that for the same scenario, TP-AIM mechanism can always evacuate all the vehicles during the least time, and much larger decrease in evacuation time can be seen in TP-AIM than in Adaptive Lights, especially under light and heavy traffic conditions; as the traffic gets heavier, evacuation time using the three methods all increases; however, the increase for TP-AIM (147.6s) is smaller than that for Fixed Lights (211.8s) and Adaptive Lights (273.8s).

Simulation results show that Adaptive Lights have absolute advantage in evacuation time and throughput compared to Fixed Lights, while our TP-AIM mechanism considerably outperforms the other mechanisms in all the
three criteria. For traffic lights, flows having intersection point in their trajectories can not get into the intersection simultaneously, so that vehicles have to be held before the intersection. Meanwhile this limit is broken through in TP-AIM, the intersection is divided into smaller conflict zones, and vehicles can be inside the intersection at the same time if they don't take any conflict zone simultaneously. Thus the intersection space is used much more efficiently. Besides, vehicles don't have to stop only if necessary, therefore travel delay can be reduced.

In terms of computation, as shown in Table 3, for all the simulation scenarios, more than 90\% of vehicles can find a feasible velocity profile for the first given entering window, and the others need to use backup solutions in their cache to plan the velocity profile again. The results show that a vehicle tries at most three times to find a feasible velocity profile. By using the setup mentioned previously, it takes at most $2.6 s$ for a vehicle to find entering windows and to plan a feasible velocity profile. On average, the computation time is less than $2 s$, of which $78 \%$ is spent on window searching (WS), and the rest on trajectory planning (TP). In near future, when vehicles have efficient onboard computation ability, TP-AIM can be implemented for real-time usage.

## 5. Conclusion

In this paper, a trajectory planning based autonomous intersection management mechanism (TP-AIM) is developed to control an intersection under inter-connected ve-
hicles environment in a safer and more efficient way.
The intersection is controlled by a manager which is used to collect and distributes conflict zone usage occupancy information. When entering the V2I range, vehicle demands the occupancy time of its related conflict zone from the manager; then the manager responses these demands in priority by dividing the vehicles into service groups. After having got the response, a window searching algorithm is used in order to find an entering window which can produce a collision-free trajectory with minimal delay, as well as some other entering windows as backups. Then the vehicle begins to plan its velocity profile using a segmented dynamic programming algorithm based on the decided entering window and the future motions of the preceding vehicle. If the current trajectory planning fails, a backup entering window is applied to restart the planning.

Simulation results prove that the proposed TP-AIM mechanism significantly reduces the average evacuation time and increases throughput by over 60s and over 20\% respectively. Additionally, the paper investigates intersection delay, the maximum delay in TP-AIM can be reduced to less than 10\% of Adaptive and Fixed Lights. Furthermore, TP-AIM can bring qualitative change to the current intersection management systems. For future research, real V2X implementation [40] and multiple intersections can be taken into account via the coordination of several managers.
