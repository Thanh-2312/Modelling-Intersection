# Quality-of-Experience-Oriented Autonomous Intersection Control in Vehicular Networks 

Penglin Dai, Kai Liu, Member, IEEE, Qingfeng Zhuge, Edwin H.-M. Sha, Senior Member, IEEE, Victor Chung Sing Lee, Member, IEEE, and Sang Hyuk Son, Fellow, IEEE


#### Abstract

Recent advances in autonomous vehicles and vehicular communications are envisioned to enable novel approaches to managing and controlling traffic intersections. In particular, with intersection controller units (ICUs), passing vehicles can be instructed to cross the intersection safely without traffic signals. Previous efforts on autonomous intersection control mainly focused on guaranteeing the safe passage of vehicles and improving intersection throughput, without considering the quality of the travel experience from the passengers' perspective. In this paper, we aim to design an enhanced autonomous intersection control mechanism, which not only ensures vehicle safety and enhances traffic efficiency but also cares about the travel experience of passengers. In particular, we design the metric of smoothness to quantitatively capture the quality of experience. In addition, we consider the travel time of individual vehicles when passing the intersection in scheduling to avoid a long delay of some vehicles, which not only helps with improving intersection throughput but also enhances the system's fairness. With the above considerations, we formulate the intersection control model and transform it into a convex optimization problem. On this basis, we propose a new algorithm to achieve an optimal solution with low overhead. Finally, we build the simulation model and implement the algorithm for performance evaluation. Comprehensive simulation results demonstrate the superiority of the proposed algorithm.


Index Terms-Autonomous intersection control, quality of experience, vehicular network, optimization.

## II. Related Work

In the last decade, great effort has been put on optimizing the performance of traffic intersections on the basis of traffic signal plan [22]-[27]. The research on adaptive traffic signals exploits various techniques, such as neural networks [22], Q-learning [23] and auction [25] to enhance the capability of intersections. However, these techniques cannot fully exploit the current advantage of vehicular networks.

With recent advances in communication technologies and the emerging of autonomous vehicles, the cooperative driving without traffic lights shows great potentials in providing more efficient and much safer way in intersection control [11]-[20]. Safety is one of the most critical concerns in designing AIC system. Dresener et al. [11] proposed a AIC framework and designed simple protocols for safe intersection management. Hafner et al. [13] addressed the two-agent safety control problem for piecewise continuous system with disturbance and imperfect state information. Further, Hafner et al. [14] employed the theoretical control method to design a collisionfree system and implemented it on two instrumented vehicles. Kowshik et al. [15] analyzed the vehicle behaviors and designed a hybrid architecture for guaranteeing the safety in the worst-case behaviors. Milanés et al. [4] designed an automatic fuzzy controller to guarantee the vehicles' safety when crossing an intersection by estimating the vehicles' statuses via V2V communications and controlling the vehicles' behaviors. Colombo et al. [28] designed least restrictive supervisors for collision avoidance at traffic intersections. By exploiting dynamics of agents along their path, they translated the problem into an equivalent scheduling problem and obtained both exact and approximate solutions.

In addition to safety, the throughput is another important issue to be considered in AIC systems. Li et al. [12] proposed a cooperative driving method to generate the safety driving pattern using spanning tree. A trajectory planning algorithm is proposed by utilizing the set of safety patterns. Although both safety and traffic efficiency are considered, the formulated problem is a combinatorial optimization problem with high computation overhead. To reduce the computation complexity, heuristic optimization methods are applied to solve the control problem. Wu et al. [16] proposed two methods for different traffic flow scales where a dynamic programming (DP) method is used to derive the optimal solution in light traffic workload and an ant colony system (ACS) is used to acquire the nearoptimal solution in heavy traffic workload. Makarem et al. [19] introduced a linear quadratic controller and soft constraints for reducing the computation overhead and enabling real-time schedule, but the method cannot promise the optimal solution. Kamal et al. [20] designed a coordination scheme to smooth the traffic flow at intersection. However, the purpose of this work is to improve the global traffic flow rather than considering travelling experience for individual vehicles. Jin et al. [29] proposed a modified multi-agent system (MMAS) with optimal scheduling of vehicles' departure time. The modified system can provide system-wide benefit in traffic efficiency.

As far as we know, the closest AIC system to our work is proposed in [17], where a Cooperative Vehicle Intersection Control (CVIC) algorithm is designed to minimize the potential overlaps of vehicles' trajectories at intersection, which can ensure vehicle safety and improve traffic efficiency. Representative relevant studies on road traffic control are summarized in Table I.

Overall, distinguishing from previous work, we not only focus on driving safety and traffic efficiency, but also consider the quality of experience from passengers' perspective in designing the coordination method. Furthermore, we consider the travel time of individual vehicles at intersection for the system fairness. Last but not least, the proposed solution is able to simplify the non-linear collision-free constraints and achieve optimal solution with low computational overhead.

## IV. Methodology

## A. Traffic Control Model

The smoothness of vehicular kinematic states is one of the most critical factors to be investigated for improving the quality of experience. To quantitatively measure the smoothness, we formulate the optimization problem by considering vehicle movement in threefold: the vehicle jitter, the acceleration and the expected velocity.

1) Objectives: As frequently switching between acceleration and deceleration may result in bad travel experience such as carsickness, we define the vehicle jitter as an instance of switching between the acceleration and deceleration. Therefore, one objective is to reduce the occurrence of vehicle jitters. Denote $L$ as the set of lanes, and $U_{k}$ represents the set of vehicles in the lane $l_{k} . a_{i k}$ is the acceleration/deceleration rate of $i$ th vehicle in lane $l_{k}$. We define the magnitude of global vehicle jitter at the intersection as follows:

$$
J_{1}=\prod_{k=1}^{\|L\| \mid U_{k} \|} \prod_{i=1} e^{-\beta \operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{i k}(t)\right) a_{i k}(t+1)}
$$

where $\beta$ is the control parameter and $\operatorname{sgn}(x)$ represents the sign function, $(\operatorname{sgn}(x)=1$ if $x>0 ; \operatorname{sgn}(x)=-1$ if $x<0 ; \operatorname{sgn}(x)$ return 0 if $x=0$ ). Since the equation $e^{-\beta \operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{i k}(t)\right) a_{i k}(t+1)}$ of an individual vehicle is an exponential function, and $\beta$ controls the increase magnitude of $e^{-\beta \operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{i k}(t)\right) a_{i k}(t+1)}$. With lager value of $\beta$, the value of $e^{-\beta \operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{i k}(t)\right) a_{i k}(t+1)}$ increase more quickly. In this paper, the default value of $\beta$ is set to 0.1.

We give an example to further illustrate Eq. (1). Considering the state of one vehicle, the condition can be classified into two cases: 1) If $\operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{i k}(t)\right) a_{i k}(t+1)$ is no less than zero, then $e^{-\operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{i k}(t)\right) a_{i k}(t+1)}$ is less than or equal to one, indicating that the acceleration/deceleration state of vehicle $u_{i k}$ remains the same. So, there is no vehicle jitter. 2) If $\operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{i k}(t)\right) a_{i k}(t+1)$ is less than zero, then $e^{-\operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{i k}(t)\right) a_{i k}(t+1)}$ is greater than one, indicating that the vehicle switches between acceleration and deceleration states, which represents an instance of vehicle jitter. Therefore, a lower value of $J_{1}$ indicates a better scheduling performance in terms of reducing the instance of vehicle jitter.

Second, a large value of acceleration/deceleration exacerbates the sharpness of velocity profile, and it may enforce strong thrust on passengers. To capture such a characteristic, the magnitude of acceleration is defined as the sum of acceleration/ deceleration rate of all the vehicles:

$$
J_{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\|L\|} \sum_{i=1}^{\left\|U_{k}\right\|}\left(v_{i k}(t)-v_{i k}(t+1)\right)^{2} .
$$

Equation (2) reflects the magnitude of global variation of velocities. The AIC system is expected to maintain a stable velocity of vehicles by reducing $J_{2}$.

Third, we investigate the expected velocity of vehicles under given traffic workloads. Keeping an expected velocity of vehicles will not only help to enhance the smoothness, but also improve the traffic throughput. Accordingly, we define the variance of expected velocity as follows:

$$
J_{3}=\sum_{k=1}^{\|L\|} \sum_{i=1}^{\left\|U_{k}\right\|}\left(V_{k}-v_{i k}(t+1)\right)^{2}
$$

where $V_{k}$ is the expected velocity in lane $l_{k}$. A low value of $J_{3}$ indicates that the velocities of vehicles approximate to the corresponding expected value, which not only balance the travel time of different vehicles, but also contribute to improve the overall throughput. According to the equation $v=q / k$, where $q$ is the traffic flow and $k$ is the vehicle density, the expected velocity can be computed by $E[v]=E[q / k]$. We consider a commonly adopted Greenshield's model [34] for analysis. Denote $v^{f}$ as the free-flow speed, and suppose $k^{j}$ is the jam

$$
J=w_{1} J_{1}+w_{2} J_{2}+w_{3} J_{3}

As analyzed, there is a trade-off among the three objectives. For instance, giving a higher weight to $J_{1}$ may give better performance on alleviating vehicle jitters, but it may sacrifice traffic throughput. Therefore, in practice, the weight factors can be adjusted and determined by transportation operators based on particular needs.

2) Mobility Constraints: In the following, we formulate the constraints by considering the characteristics of vehicular mobility. First, the motion of vehicle has to obey the kinematic law. Denote $s_{i k}(t)$ as the distance between vehicle $u_{i k}$ and the intersection entrance at $t$. Given the scheduling time slot $\tau$, the following constraints have to be satisfied:

$$
\begin{aligned}
s_{i k}(t+1) & =s_{i k}(t)-v_{i k}(t) \tau-\frac{1}{2} a_{i k}(t+1) \tau^{2} \\
v_{i k}(t+1) & =v_{i k}(t)+a_{i k}(t+1) \tau
\end{aligned}
$$

Without loss of generality, we assume that the maximum acceleration/deceleration of all the vehicles is the same. $a_{\text {min }}$ and $a_{\text {max }}$ are the maximum acceleration and deceleration. Similarly, the minimum and maximum velocity of all the vehicles is the same. $v_{\text {min }}$ and $v_{\text {max }}$ are the minimum and maximum velocity, respectively. Therefore, the following constraints should be satisfied:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& a_{\min } \leq a_{i k}(t+1) \leq a_{\max } \\
& v_{\min } \leq v_{i k}(t+1) \leq v_{\max } .
\end{aligned}
$$

3) Collision Avoidance Constraint and Linearization: With respect to collision avoidance, firstly, we consider the safety distance between any two adjacent vehicles in the same lane. Denote $h$ as the safety headway between $u_{i k}$ and $u_{i+1, k}$, and then it has to satisfy:

$$
s_{i+1, k}(t+1)-s_{i k}(t+1) \geq h \cdot v_{i, k}(t) .
$$

Secondly, we consider collision avoidance in the intersection area. In previous MPC-based framework, the collision avoidance constraint is normally formulated as a non-linear constraint, which gives high computational cost. In this model, we linearize the collision avoidance constraints by scheduling the entering priority of the vehicles at the intersection.

The order of vehicles entering the intersection is determined when the vehicle entering the decision zone. The length of the decision zone is set as $\left|v_{\text {max }}^{2} /\left(2 \cdot a_{\text {min }}\right)\right|$. To be elaborated later, utilizing the decision zone will enable the convex optimization and help to reduce the complexity of the proposed solution.

d_{k}{ }^{(m)}= \begin{cases}\frac{\pi w}{16}, & \text { turning } \\ \frac{w}{4}, & \text { straight. }\end{cases}
$$

Based on Eq. (14), for vehicle $u_{i k}$, denote $d s_{i k}^{(m)}$ as the distance to the entrance of the collision area $c_{m}$, and denote $d e_{i k}^{(m)}$ as the distance to the exit of the collision area $c_{m}$, then $d s_{i k}^{(m)}$ and $d e_{i k}^{(m)}$ can be obtained as follows:

$$

\end{array}\right.
\end{aligned}

$$
\begin{aligned}
& t s_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1)=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}
\frac{d s_{i k}^{(m)}(t)}{v_{i k}(t+1)}, & l_{k} \in C L_{m} \\
\infty, & l_{k} \notin C L_{m}
\end{array}\right. \\
& t e_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1)=\left\{\begin{array}{cc}
\frac{d e_{i k}^{(m)}(t)}{v_{i k}(t+1)}, & l_{k} \in C L_{m} \\
\infty, & l_{k} \notin C L_{m}
\end{array}\right.
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\infty$ indicates that vehicles in $l_{k}$ will not occupy $c_{m}$ when crossing. With Eqs. (17) and (18), the dwelling interval of $u_{i k}$ in $c_{m}$ at time $t+1$, denoted by $\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1)$, is represented as follows:

$$
\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1)=\left[t s_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1), t e_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1)\right] .
$$

To ensure the safe crossing, it has to satisfy that for any collision area, there is no overlap of the dwelling interval for all the vehicles. To facilitate the analysis, we define the order on the dwelling interval at $c_{m}$ as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1) & \leq \sigma_{j n}^{(m)}(t+1), \quad \forall l_{k}, l_{n} \in C L_{m} \\
& \Leftrightarrow t e_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1) \leq t s_{j n}^{(m)}(t+1)
\end{aligned}
$$

Such an order promises that the vehicles $u_{i k}$ and $u_{j n}$ will not collide at $c_{m}$. Accordingly, we define the following rules to determine the order of dwelling intervals for any pair of vehicles in $c_{m}$ :

a) For two vehicles $u_{i k}$ and $u_{i+1, k}$, where $l_{k} \in C L_{m}$, then it has to satisfy that $\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1) \leq \sigma_{i+1, k}^{(m)}(t+1)$.
b) For two vehicles $u_{i k}$ and $u_{j n}$, where $l_{k} \neq l_{n}$ and $l_{k}, l_{n} \in$ $C L_{m}$, if $\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t) \leq \sigma_{j n}^{(m)}(t)$, then it has to satisfy that $\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1) \leq \sigma_{j n}^{(m)}(t+1)$.

Rule a) prevents the rear-end crash for the vehicles in the same lane when crossing the intersection. Rule b) means that for two vehicles possibly collide at a collision area, they should follow the rule that the early arrival vehicle gets through the collision area first.

In addition to the safety, we further consider the travel time of individual vehicles from the view point of system fairness. Specifically, for two vehicles whose dwelling intervals are overlap, it should follow:

- c) For $u_{i k}$ and $u_{j n}$, where $l_{k} \neq l_{n}$ and $l_{k}, l_{n} \in C L_{m}$, if $T T_{i k} \geq T T_{j n}$ and $\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t) \cap \sigma_{j n}^{(m)}(t) \neq \emptyset$, then it has to satisfy that $\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1) \leq \sigma_{j n}^{(m)}(t+1)$.

Rule c) gives the vehicle with longer travel time a higher priority to cross the intersection, which is designed to prevent that some vehicles may have much longer travel time than others when crossing the intersection. With the above analysis on sorting the dwelling intervals of vehicles in each $c_{m}$, the collision-free constraints at the intersection area can be formulated as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1) \leq & \sigma_{i+1, k}^{(m)}(t+1), \quad \forall l_{k} \in C L_{m} \\
\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1) \leq & \sigma_{j n}^{(m)}(t+1), \text { if } \sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t) \leq \sigma_{j n}^{(m)}(t) \\
& \forall l_{k}, l_{n} \in C L_{m}, l_{k} \neq l_{n} \\
\sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t+1) \leq & \sigma_{j n}^{(m)}(t+1), \text { if } \sigma_{i k}^{(m)}(t) \cap \sigma_{j n}^{(m)}(t) \neq \emptyset \\
T T_{i k} \geq & T T_{j n}, \quad \forall l_{k}, l_{n} \in C L_{m}, l_{k} \neq l_{n}
\end{aligned}
$$

## C. Recovery Mode

Typical optimization-based control models have overwhelming complexity, which render them cannot find feasible solutions from time to time [17]. Although our proposed algorithm is based on a convex optimization model and it can derive optimal solutions most of the time, it is still possible that there will be no feasible solution when the traffic workload is getting heavier. To handle such situations, we follow the recovery mode

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \min J=w_{1} e^{\sum_{k=1}^{\|L\|\left\|U_{k}\right\|} \sum_{i=1}-\beta \operatorname{sgn}\left(a_{i k}(t)\right) a_{i k}(t+1)}+w_{2} \sum_{k=1}^{\|L\|} \sum_{i=1}^{\left\|U_{k}\right\|}\left(a_{i k}(t+1)\right)^{2}+w_{3} \sum_{k=1}^{\|L\|} \sum_{i=1}^{\left\|U_{k}\right\|}\left(V_{k}-v_{i k}(t)-a_{i k}(t+1) \cdot \tau\right)^{2} \\
& \max \left\{a_{\min }, \frac{u_{\min }-v_{i k}(t)}{\tau}\right\} \leq a_{i k}(t+1) \leq \min \left\{a_{\max }, \frac{u_{\max }-v_{i k}(t)}{\tau}\right\} \\
& \frac{1}{2} a_{i+1, k}(t+1) \tau^{2}-\frac{1}{2} a_{i, k}(t+1) \tau^{2} \leq h \cdot v_{i, k}(t)+v_{i k}(t) \tau-v_{i+1, k}(t) \tau-s_{i k}(t)+s_{i+1, k}(t) \\
& a_{i+1, k}(t+1) \tau \cdot d s_{i k}^{(m)}(t)-a_{i k}(t+1) \tau \cdot d s_{i+1, k}^{(m)}(t) \leq v_{i k}(t) \cdot d s_{i+1, k}^{(m)}(t)-v_{i+1, k}(t) \cdot d s_{i k}^{(m)}(t) \\
& a_{\ln }(t+1) \tau \cdot d s_{i k}^{(m)}(t)-a_{i k}(t+1) \tau \cdot d s_{\ln }^{(m)}(t) \leq v_{i k}(t) \cdot d s_{\ln }^{(m)}(t)-v_{\ln }(t) \cdot d s_{i k}^{(m)}(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

proposed in [17] to make the system complete. The key ideas of the recovery mode are recapitulated as follows: 1) the ICU searches the vehicles whose dwelling time are overlapped at the intersection and determines the priority of each lane according to the vehicle density. 2) The vehicles in the lanes with higher priority will maintain their original velocities to get through the intersection. 3) The vehicles in the lanes with lower priority will be classified into two groups. The first group is the vehicles which have no collision risk, denoted as GO group. Other vehicles which may cause collision are classified into the STOP group. The vehicles in GO group can keep their velocities, and the vehicles in STOP group should decelerate their velocity before entering the intersection. After those vehicles in the high priority lanes which are in conflict with the STOP group vehicles have passed the intersection, the ICU returns to the optimization-enabled mode.

## B. Metrics

To quantitatively measure the algorithm performance, we design the following four metrics: quadratic mean of acceleration (QMA), percentage of jitter, standard deviation of travel time (SDTT) and throughput.

- Quadratic mean of acceleration (QMA): it is defined as the quadratic mean of accelerations of all vehicles at a specific time, which is computed by:
$$
\operatorname{QMA}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N(t)}\left(a_{i}(t)\right)^{2}}}{N(t)}
$$
where $N(t)$ represents the total number of vehicles at time $t$. QMA $(t)$ records the average absolute value of accelerations of all the vehicles in the current time slot. The low value of QMA $(t)$ reflects that the small variation of all the vehicles is small, which indicates a smoothness of driving from the global system point of view.
- Percentage of jitter: Denote the number of vehicles that have vehicle jitter at time $t$ as $N_{s}(t)$, and then the percentage of jitter is computed by $N_{s}(t) / N(t)$, where $N(t)$ is the total number of vehicles at time $t$. A low percentage of jitter indicates better quality of passenger experience.
- Standard deviation of travel time (SDTT): Denoted the travel time of the vehicle $u_{i}$ as $\mathrm{CTT}_{i}$, and then the mean total travel time MTT of all vehicles is computed by $\sum_{i}^{N(t)}\left(\mathrm{CTT}_{i} / N(t)\right)$. Accordingly, the standard deviation of travel time SDDT is computed by
$$
\mathrm{SDTT}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N(t)} \sum_{i=1}^{N(t)}\left(\mathrm{CTT}_{i}(t)-\mathrm{MTT}\right)^{2}}
$$
This metric evaluates the quality of experience in terms of system fairness. The lower value of SDTT indicates better system performance on balancing travel time of individual vehicle.
- Throughput: it is defined as the number of vehicles crossing the intersection per hour. A high throughput indicates the algorithm is good at enhancing traffic efficiency.

## D. Effect of Packet Loss

To further demonstrate the robustness of QEOIC, we evaluate the effect of packet loss under different packet loss percentages. Note that the compared algorithms (i.e., Traffic Light, MMAS and CVIC) do not consider the packet loss problem, and thus they are not comparable. For QEOIC, in case the packet sent by vehicle is lost, the vehicle will continue with its previous instructed acceleration. Accordingly, QEOIC can predict the position of this vehicle according to its previous kinematics states.

Fig. 8 shows the QMA of QEOIC algorithm under different percentages of packet loss. With an increasing of packet loss percentages, the QMA of QEOIC increases gradually. When the packet loss rates are 1\%, 5\% and 10\%, the QMA become 0.30, 0.33 and 0.39 , respectively. ## VI. Conclusion and Future Work

AIC system has been demonstrated as a promising way to enable future autonomous intersection control. In this paper, in addition to designing AIC system from transportation management point of view to ensure vehicle safety and improve traffic efficiency, we further consider to enhance the system performance by evaluating the quality of experience from passengers' perspective. Specifically, we proposed a QEOIC algorithm to schedule the vehicles to cross the intersection efficiently and smoothly. We quantitatively analyze the characteristics of vehicle movement and transform these factors into a convex objective function. Furthermore, we define the decision zone and divide intersection into multiple collision areas. Then we design a schedule rule to determine the priority of the vehicles in each collision area, which linearize the collision constraints. Finally, we transform the traffic control model into a convex optimization problem, which can be solved efficiently in realtime. An extensive simulation results demonstrate that the QEOIC can strike a better balance among travel smoothness, system fairness and throughput under a wide range of varying traffic workloads.

To further enhance the system robustness, we will incorporate wireless communication issues such as transmission delay into consideration. Further, we will take realistic vehicle model into consideration and adapt the current optimization-based solution into a realistic AIC system. Moreover, cooperative driving control strategies among multiple intersections are expected to be further explored.
