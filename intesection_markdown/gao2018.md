#### Abstract

Existing two-phase signal optimization models neglect the unyielding behavior of illegal permissive left-turners at intersections in many developing countries. At permitted left-turn phase warranted intersections, leftturn traffic usually needs to yield to the opposing through during permitted phasing; however, in certain circumstances such an explicit permissive leftturn yielding rule may be violated when aggressive left-turners compete with the opposing through vehicles to take the conflict point and pass through a two-phase signal-controlled intersection without yielding. Such driving behaviors, very commonly observed at urban intersections in many developing countries, have been demonstrated to have significant impact on the intersection operational performance, which has not been taken into consideration in the existing traffic signal design paradigms. This paper presents a cell-based signal optimization model for intersections without the explicit left-turn yielding rule during permitted phases. The model features its effectiveness to capture traffic dynamics and interactions between conflicting movements during permitted phases and to design appropriate signal timing plans to improve the operational performance of such intersections. Extensive experimental analyses and field validation in comparison with results from TRANSYT-7F demonstrate the promising property of the proposed model.

## 1. Introduction

Design of effective signal timings to improve the operational performance of intersections has long been one of the critical tasks for traffic engineers. Over the past several decades, a large body of literature has been devoted to this issue (Webster 1958; Allsop 1971, 1972; Han 1995; Improta and Cantarella 1984; Gallivan and Heydecker 1988; Heydecker 1992; Heydecker 1996; Wong 1996, 1997; Silcock 1997; Wong and Wong 2003; Wong and Wong 2002; Wong, Wong, and Tong 2006) and one of the major challenges to design effective traffic signal operation is to deal with left turn movements, which are generally acknowledged to be the highest-risk movements at intersections. An estimated 27\% of all intersection-related crashes in the United States are associated with left turns, with over twothirds occurring at signalized intersections (O'Connor 2004). Vehicles making this type of movement

encounter potential conflicts from several sources including opposing through traffic, through traffic in the same direction, and crossing vehicular and pedestrian traffic.

Depending on the intersection channelization and the left-turn and opposing through traffic volumes, left-turn traffic at a signalized intersection is usually treated by protected or permissive phasing or their combination. Permissive (also known as permitted) phasing allows two opposing approaches to time concurrently, with left turns allowed after yielding to conflicting traffic or pedestrians (referred as the explicit left-turn yielding rule in this study). Permissive left-turn phasing is generally not practical for high-volume intersections, but may function to operate intersections with low or medium traffic demand adequately and safely if the explicit left-turn yielding rule is strictly followed. Many previous studies have discussed and developed effective signal timing strategies to improve the performance of intersections with permissive left-turn phasing (Bonneson, Messer, and Fambro 1988; Lin 1992; Giannopoulos and Mustafa 1996; Findler, Surender, and Catrava 1997; Janson and Buchholz 1998; Levinson and Prassas 2001; Liu et al. 2002; Kim 2006; Chai and Wong 2014).

Despite the effectiveness of the explicit left-turn yielding rule during permissive phasing, in certain circumstances it may be violated where aggressive left-turners compete with the opposing through vehicles to occupy the conflict point to pass through the intersection without yielding. Whoever first takes the conflict point will force its conflicting movement to yield. In the case when the left-turn movement first occupies the conflict point, the opposing through needs to yield and wait for the gap to pass, and vice versa. Such driving behavior, very commonly observed at urban intersections in many developing countries (see Figure 1 for an example), has been demonstrated to show significant impact on the traffic operational performance. But this behavior has not been taken into consideration in existing traffic signal design paradigms.

This study, proposed to address the above concerns, will develop a traffic signal optimization model for two-phase signalized intersections without explicit left-turn yielding rules, which can find its best application in many developing countries. Rest of the paper is organized as follows. Existing related studies are reviewed in Section 2. The notations used in the model formulation are listed in Section 3. In Section 4, a two-dimensional cell transmission framework with design of various types of virtual cells is proposed to represent the interactions of movements from different approaches within the intersection, and the concept of overlapping cells is introduced to model the unique phenomena of competition between the left turn and the opposing through movements at the conflict points.

Grounded on the new traffic dynamics formulations, a cell-based signal optimization model will be formulated and solved by a GA-based heuristic in Section 5. Extensive experimental analyses in comparison with results from TRANSYT-7F (version 8) will be performed to validate the proposed model in Section 6. Some conclusions are given in Section 7.

## 2. Literature review

In review of literature, most of studies on traffic signal design and optimization fall into two basic categories: mathematical programming models and simulation-based models. In the mathematical programming models, a set of mixed integer linear programming (MILP) formulations have been proposed in the literature (Gartner, Little, and Gabbay 1975a, 1975b; Little, Kelson, and Gartner 1981; Cohen and Liu 1986; Gartner et al. 1991; Chaudhary and Messer 1993) aiming to maximize the bandwidth or to minimize the intersection delay (Wong 2003). Lin, Tung, and Ku (2010) have proposed a mixed integer nonlinear programming model to maximize the bandwidths for arterial signals. Coll et al. (2013) have used the linear programming approach to solve the optimization problem of a system of signals. A very recent study by Li (2014) has developed a two-phase method to generate reliable bandwidth plans under progression time uncertainty. Despite the contribution of those studies, most of such models have not addressed the impact of unbalanced turning movements. At intersections without explicit left-turn yielding rules during permitted phases, unexpected delay of through movements at the conflict point within an intersection may easily disrupt the progression bandwidth. In addition, neglect of traffic dynamics interactions in those models may limit their applicability in real world.

To consider traffic flow interactions, some researchers have proposed the simulation-based models, in which traffic flow models are developed to represent the complex interactions between traffic operations and traffic signal parameters so that signal timings can be optimized based on the performance indices generated. Various versions of TRANSYT (Robertson 1969), TRANSYT-7F (Wallace et al. 1988), and extended models (Wong 1996) are perhaps the most widely used offline signal timing optimization packages within this category. The packages utilize the platoon dispersion model to predict traffic arrivals at downstream intersections. For online optimization, such models have been extended to comprise the simulation kernel of the real-time adaptive traffic control systems that react to dynamic traffic conditions. Examples of such systems include SCOOT (Robertson and Bretherton 1991), OPAC (Gartner 1983), and RHODES (Mirchandani and Head 2001). Other simulation-based approaches include store-and-forward models (D'Ans and Gazis 1976; Papageorgiou 1995; Aboudolas, Papageorgiou, and Kosmatopoulos 2009), queue-and-dissipation models (Kashani and Saridis 1983; Wu and Chang 1999; Van den Berg et al. 2003; Liu and Chang 2011), stochastic models (Yu and Recker 2006) and discrete-time kinematic models (Lo 2001).

To achieve better computational efficiency and modeling accuracy, the cell transmission model (CTM), first proposed by Daganzo $(1994,1995)$ for depicting traffic dynamics on freeways and freeway networks, have been extended in many previous studies to replicate traffic operations on urban intersections. For examples, Chang (1998) has collected extensive travel delay data on signalized urban streets in Hong Kong and validated that the CTM's travel delay estimates reasonably matched the actual field measurements under a variety of traffic conditions. Lo (1999, 2001; Lo and Chow 2004) had used the CTM to capture the total travel delay to evaluate the dynamic arterial traffic signal control strategies for a one-way arterial street in Hong Kong. Lin (2001) has applied the CTM to evaluate the performance of intersection signal timing plans for a one-way arterial. In order to model the traffic dynamics at a four-leg intersection, Flötteröd and Nagel (2005) have proposed some practical extensions to Daganzo's CTM by extending the number of both merging cells and diverging cells from 2 to more than 2; however traffic dynamics within the intersection were simplified. Laval and Daganzo (2006) have proposed a lane-specific CTM that incorporates lane-changing behavior. In this model, cells are arranged in a two-dimensional grid format. The model is used to explain the flow reduction at freeway lane drops and the relationship between the speed and capacity of moving bottlenecks. Its
applicability on signalized urban streets, however, was not verified. TRANSYT by TRL (Binning, Burtenshaw, and Crabtree 2008), since its version 13, has introduced the CTM as an alternative to the existing platoon dispersion model, which offers relatively more realistic modeling approach for traffic dynamics on links and at intersections. Szeto et al. (2009) have used the CTM to predict the traffic volume on a signalized urban road network. Karoonsoontawong and Waller (2010) presented a robust optimization formulation that simultaneously solves the traffic signal optimization and dynamic traffic assignment problems. The traffic propagation on road network are modelled with the CTM, but the traffic dynamics within signalized intersections are not mentioned. Rohde and Friedrich (2010) have applied the enhanced CTM proposed by Flötteröd to deal with signalized multi-lane junctions; but the operation of traffic within the intersection was still simplified. Ukkusuri, Ramadurai, and Patil (2010) have embedded the CTM into a robust dynamic system optimal model. The CTM was used to estimate the total travel time, total control delay and number of stops on a road network. Su, Kurzhanskiy, and Horowitz (2013) has used the CTM to simulate traffic operation on a segment of a signalized arterial. This study treated the intersections on an arterial as nodes and did not discuss how to model the traffic operations within intersection. He et al. (2013) have used the CTM to simulate a signalized urban network with only intersections controlled by a four-phase traffic signal. Configuration of lanes and channelization of the approach were neglected. Tiriolo, Adacher, and Cipriani (2014) have divided an urban link into two sections: merged section and channelized section and modeled the traffic dynamics on each section based on the CTM to capture spillback effect and queues dissipation and to represent flow interactions of queues among neighboring lanes; but traffic interaction within the intersection was not addressed.

Realizing the fact that traffic dynamics and movement interactions within the signalized intersection have significant impacts on the operation of traffic signal, researchers have started to extend the CTM with more microscopic considerations. For examples, Li et al. (2010) has modeled the diverging section of urban link with sub-cells to simulate the effect of lane blockage on link traffic dynamics; but the blockage due to conflicts between left turns and the opposing through traffic within the intersection was not discussed. Long et al. (2011) have applied the CTM to simulate the formation and dissipation of traffic jams at the microscopic level. The downstream of a link is channelized to represent the movements to different destinations. Flötteröd and Rohde (2011) developed an incremental node model for general road intersections. But interactions between conflicting flows are ignored. Qi et al. (2013) have formulated the relationship between the throughput of intersection and the lane changing behavior on links. The developed lane changing logic was embedded with the CTM to explore the interactions of different traffic streams. Like other studies, this research does not deal with the modelling of traffic operation within intersection.

In summary, despite the effectiveness of the aforementioned studies in modeling the interrelations between traffic dynamics and intersection control variables, none of those studies have examined the impact of aggressive left-turn maneuvers on traffic signal design and the overall intersection performance. More specifically, traffic dynamics and movement interactions within the signalized intersection and their impacts on the traffic signal operation have not been sufficiently addressed in those studies, which have limited their applications in many developing countries.

### 4.1. Approach diverge

In order to model the flow on the shared lanes diverging to different exits, we introduce the virtual cells $c_{i, \text { left }}^{A V} c_{i, \text { through }}^{A V}$ and $c_{i, \text { right }}^{A V}$ located after the stop line for approach $A_{i}$. Because the shared leftturn and through lane is involved, we introduce the overlapping cells $c_{i, l e f t}^{A O}$ and $c_{i, t h r o u g h}^{A O}$ located at the conflict point for approach $A_{i}$. For the sake of easy modelling, we also introduce the overlapping cell $c_{i, \text { right }}^{A O}$ located on the movement right for approach $A_{i}$. With the aid of the virtual cells and overlapping cells, the approach diverge is modelled as shown in Figure 2. The virtual cells $c_{i, l e f t}^{A V}, c_{i, t h r o u g h}^{A V}$, and $c_{i, \text { right }}^{A V}$ send the vehicles received from the cells $c_{i j, A_{i}^{A}}^{A}$ into the overlapping cells $c_{i, \text { left }}^{A O}, c_{i, \text { through }}^{A O}$, and $c_{i, \text { right }}^{A O}$ simultaneously.

For lane $L_{i j}^{A}$ on approach $A_{i}$, we use the vector $\left(p_{i j, l \in f t}^{A}[t], p_{i j, t h r o u g h}^{A}[t], p_{i j, r i g h t}^{A}[t]\right)$ to quantify the lane function. And we use the vector $\left(U_{i}^{A}[\right.$ left,$t], U_{i}^{A}[$ through,$t], U_{i}^{A}[$ right,$\left.t]\right)$ to indicate the right of way for the left, through, and right movements on approach $A_{i}$ at step $t$.

The variable $U_{i}^{A}[k, t](k \in K)$ is given by:

$$
U_{i}^{A}[k, t]= \begin{cases}1 & \text { if signal displays green at step } t \\ 0 & \text { if signal displays red at step } t\end{cases}
$$

In Equation (1), the amber light is divided into effective green and effective red.
The maximum storage capacity and flow capacity for the virtual cells and overlapping cells are calculated based not on the turn ratio for the approach, but on the quantified lane functions because of the unbalanced flow among lanes under some situations, given by:

$$
\begin{gathered}
N\left[c_{i k}^{A V}, t\right]=\sum_{j} p_{i j k}^{A}[t] \cdot N\left[c_{i j, h, t}^{A}, t\right] \\
Q\left[c_{i k}^{A V}, t\right]=\sum_{j} p_{i j k}^{A}[t] \cdot Q\left[c_{i j, h, A}^{A}, t\right] \\
N\left[c_{i k}^{A O}, t\right]=N\left[c_{i k}^{A V}, t\right] \\
Q\left[c_{i k}^{A O}, t\right]=Q\left[c_{i k}^{A V}, t\right]
\end{gathered}
$$

Number of vehicles in the virtual cells at step $t$ is related to the state of signal, and is updated with the following equation.

$$
n\left[c_{i k}^{A V}, t\right]=\sum_{j} p_{i j k}^{A}[t] \cdot U_{i}^{A}[k, t] \cdot n\left[c_{i j, h_{i}^{A}}^{A}, t\right]
$$

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/58c02ad8-ddd8-4d87-b5bd-a01f7ba23c10-08.jpg?height=456&width=1247&top_left_y=179&top_left_x=229)
Figure 3. Modelling the exit merge with virtual cells and overlapping cells.

And number of vehicles that the virtual cells actually send at step $t$ is calculate with:

$$
s\left[c_{i k}^{A V}, t\right]=\min \left(S\left[c_{i k}^{A V}, t\right], R\left[c_{i k}^{A O}, t\right]\right)
$$

The number of vehicles that the overlapping cells actually receive at step $t$ is given by:

$$
r\left[c_{i k}^{A O}, t\right]=s\left[c_{i k}^{A V}, t\right]
$$

### 4.2. Exit merge

We introduce the virtual cells $c_{i k}^{A T}$ and $c_{i}^{E}$ to model the merge of flows from different approaches at the exit $E_{i}$ as shown in Figure 3. The virtual cell $c_{i}^{E}$ sends the vehicles received from different approaches into the cells $c_{i j}^{E}$ simultaneously.

The maximum storage capacity and flow capacity of the virtual cell $c_{i}^{E}$ are calculated based on the lanes on the exit, given by:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& N\left[c_{i}^{E}, t\right]=\sum_{j} N\left[c_{i j}^{E}, t\right] \\
& Q\left[c_{i}^{E}, t\right]=\sum_{j} Q\left[c_{i j}^{E}, t\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

Number of vehicles in the virtual cell at step $t$ is updated with the following equation.

$$
n\left[c_{i}^{E}, t\right]=\sum_{j} n\left[c_{i j}^{E}, t\right]
$$

And the number of vehicles that the overlapping cells $c_{i k}^{E F}(k \in K)$ actually send at step $t$ is calculated with:

$$
S\left[c_{i k}^{E F}, t\right]= \begin{cases}S\left[c_{i k}^{E F}, t\right] & R\left[c_{i}^{E}, t\right] \geq \sum_{k} S\left[c_{i k}^{E F}, t\right] \\ \operatorname{mid}\left(S\left[c_{i k}^{E F}, t\right], R\left[c_{i}^{E}, t\right]-\left(\sum_{k} S\left[c_{i k}^{E F}, t\right]-S\left[c_{i k}^{E F}, t\right]\right), p_{i k}^{E}[t] \cdot R\left[c_{i}^{E}, t\right]\right) & \text { otherwise }\end{cases}
$$

The operator mid in Equation (12) is to find the middle value among three numbers. The reason that the mid operator is used in Equation (12) is explained by Daganzo (1995). As indicated by Equation (12), all vehicles will flow from the overlapping cell $c_{i k}^{E F}$ to the virtual cell $c_{i}^{E}$ if the virtual cell $c_{i}^{E}$ has enough space to accommodate all vehicles, otherwise, only part of vehicles can be allowed to flow into the overlapping cell.

The number of vehicles that the virtual cell receives at step $t$ is given by:

$$
r\left[c_{i}^{E}, t\right]=\sum_{k} s\left[c_{i k}^{E F}, t\right]
$$

And the number of vehicles that each cell $c_{i j}^{E}$ in $L_{i j}^{E}$ is calculated with the following equation.

$$
r\left[c_{i j}^{E}, t\right]=\frac{R\left[c_{i j}^{E}, t\right]}{R\left[c_{i}^{E}, t\right]} \cdot r\left[c_{i}^{E}, t\right]
$$

### 4.3. Within-intersection conflicts

The conflict between the permitted left-turn movement and the opposing through can be modelled with the overlapping cells, which are located prior to the conflict point within the intersection as shown in Figure 4. In Figure $4_{1}, A_{i}$ is the opposing approach for $A_{i}$, and $\bar{k}$ is the opposing conflicting movement for movement $k$, which satisfies $\bar{k}=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}\text { left } & k=\text { through } \\ \text { through } & k=\text { left }\end{array}\right.$.

The cells $c_{i k}^{A O}$ and $c_{\bar{i} \bar{k}}^{A O}$ prior to the conflict point are not allowed to send vehicles simultaneously because vehicles pass through the conflict point according to the rule of gap acceptance. According to the number of acceptable gaps occurred in the priority movement, the number of vehicles that cut through the priority flow can be estimated. In a time step, the number of vehicles that cut through the conflicting flow can be estimated by the following equation according to the Highway Capacity Manual 2010.

$$
s\left[c_{i \bar{k}}^{A O}, t\right]=s\left[c_{i k}^{A O}, t\right] \cdot \frac{e^{-s\left[c_{i k}^{A O}, t\right] \cdot \frac{\delta[\bar{k}]}{\tau}}}{1-e^{-s\left[c_{i k}^{A O}, t\right] \cdot \frac{\delta[[\bar{k}]}{\tau}}}
$$

In Equation (15), we assume that the movement $k$ is the priority traffic. At a two-phase signal-controlled intersection where illegal left turners exist, if the vehicles on the two movements arriving at the conflicting point at the same time, the conflict point will be occupied by a more aggressive head vehicle from one of the conflicting movements (a prevalent case in many developing countries). Therefore, sometimes the permissive left-turning movement can also be the priority movement. A statistical probability variable is introduced to determine which cell to discharge flow first. Let $p^{O}[t]$ denotes a random variable and $p_{i k}^{O}$ the statistical probability variable for the flow in the cell $c_{i k}^{A O}$ to go first. If $p^{O}[t] \leq p_{i k^{\prime}}^{O}$ it indicates the situation that the movement $k$ will occupy the conflict point at the step $t$, and the movement $\bar{k}$ will cut through the conflict point according to the gap acceptance rule and vice versa.

### 4.4. Estimation of intersection operational performance

The Figures 2-4 illustrate the traffic propagation from approach to exit at a two-phase signalcontrolled intersection. With the lane based two-dimensional cell framework, queue's formation and dissipation in different lanes can be captured separately and the queue length can be estimated according to the cell state. In this study, the state of a cell $c_{i j x}^{A}$ in lane $L_{i j}^{A}$ is defined as 'blocked' if the cell is not empty and no flow moves out of it; otherwise the state of the cell will be set as 'open', given by:

$$
U\left[c_{i j x^{\prime}}^{A} t\right]= \begin{cases}\text { blocked } & n\left[c_{i j x^{\prime}}^{A} t\right]>0 \text { and } s\left[c_{i j x^{\prime}}^{A} t\right]=0 \\ \text { open } & \text { otherwise }\end{cases}
$$

Generally, red signal and the queue in front of it blocks the cell $c_{i j x}^{A}$. Based on the state sequence of cells in lane $L_{i j}^{A}$ at step $t$, changes of the queue length in lane $L_{i j}^{A}$ are dynamically updated. A queue (represented by oblique lines) dissipation process in lane $L_{i j}^{A}$ is shown in Figure 5. The tail of the queue is located at cell $c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A T}}^{A}$, and the head of the queue is located at cell $c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A H}}^{A}$. For the vehicle queue in lane $L_{i j}^{A}$, the following equation holds.

$$
U\left[c_{i j x}^{A}, t\right]= \begin{cases}\text { blocked } & x=h_{i j}^{A T}, X_{q t}+1, \cdots, h_{i j}^{A H}-1 \\ \text { open } & x=1, \cdots, h_{i j}^{A T}-1, h_{i j}^{A H}, \cdots, h_{i}^{A}\end{cases}
$$

Number of vehicles contained in the head and tail cells of the queue may be less than their storage capacity whereas those in between the head and tail cells fully jammed, given by:

$$
\begin{gathered}
n\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A T}}^{A}, t\right] \leq N\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A T}}^{A}, t\right] \\
n\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A H}}^{A}, t\right] \leq N\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A H}}^{A}, t\right] \\
n\left[c_{i j x}^{A}, t\right]=N\left[c_{i j x}^{A}, t\right] \quad\left(x=h_{i j}^{A T}+1, \cdots, h_{i j}^{A H}-1\right)
\end{gathered}
$$

Once the states of the cells are determined, traffic performance indices such as vehicle queue length, stop delay, and throughput in lane $L_{i j}^{A}$ can be derived. The queue length at step $t, q_{i j}^{A}[t]$, is estimated with the following equation:

$$
q_{i j}^{A}[t]=\sum_{x=h_{i j}^{A T}}^{h_{i j}^{A H}} n\left[c_{i j x}^{A}, t\right]
$$

The increment of delay incurred by the queued vehicles during step $t, d_{i j}^{A l}[t]$, is estimated with the

$$
d_{i j}^{A I}[t]=q_{i j}^{A}[t] \cdot \tau-0.5 \tau \cdot\left(r\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A T}}^{A}, t\right]-s\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A H}}^{A}, t\right]\right)

We assume that vehicles join and leave the queue uniformly during each time step. Thus, the stop delay incurred by the vehicles joining the queue during step $t$ is equal to $0.5 \tau \cdot r\left[c_{i j, A_{i j}^{A T}}^{A} t\right]$, and that incurred by the vehicles leaving the queue during step $t$ is equal to $0.5 \tau \cdot s\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A H}}^{A}, t\right]$. Thus, $d_{i j}^{A l}[t]=$ $\left(q_{i j}^{A}[t-1]-s\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A H}}^{A}, t\right]\right) \cdot \tau+0.5 \tau \cdot\left(r\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A T}}^{A}, t\right]+s\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A H}}^{A}, t\right]\right)$. Because only after vehicles pass through conflict points, they will completely clear the intersection, the throughput is evaluated at conflict points. The increment of throughput for approach $A_{i}$ at step $t$, $V_{i}^{A l}[t]$, is estimated with the following equation:

V_{i}^{A l}[t]=\sum_{k \in K} s\left[c_{i k}^{A O}, t\right]

## 5. The signal optimization model and its solving algorithm

With the new traffic dynamics formulations, a cell-based model can be formulated to optimize signal timings for a two-phase signal-controlled intersection without explicit left-turn yielding rules during permitted phases.

Given the time period $T$ of analysis, Equation (24) represents the primary objective of the control model for minimizing the total stop delay incurred by all the queued vehicles on approaches of the intersection.

$$
\max \left\{1.0 / \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{i=1}^{m_{A}} \sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}^{A}}\left[q_{i j}^{A}[t] \cdot \tau-0.5 \tau \cdot\left(r\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A T}}^{A}, t\right]-s\left[c_{i j, h_{i j}^{A H}}^{A}, t\right]\right)\right]\right\}
$$

Subject to:

$$
\begin{gathered}
C \geq C_{\min } \\
C \leq C_{\max } \\
g_{l} \geq g_{l}^{\min }, \quad l=1,2 \\
g_{l} \leq g_{l}^{\max }, \quad l=1,2 \\
\sum_{l=1}^{2} g_{l}+\sum_{l=1}^{2} a_{l}+\sum_{l=1}^{2} a r_{l}=C
\end{gathered}
$$

Constraints (25) and (26) restrict the cycle length between its minimum and maximum; Constraints (27) and (28) require that the green time for each phase should satisfy the minimal and maximum allowable
green time restrictions; Moreover, the sum of green times and inter-green times for all phases at the intersection should be equal to the cycle length which is required by Constraint (29).

The proposed cell-based optimization model consists of complex formulations, including cellbased traffic dynamics formulations. It is thus difficult to find the global optimal solution through traditional non-linear programming approaches. The following genetic heuristic is designed to yield the optimal solution efficiently:

Step 1: Randomly generate an initial population;
Step 2: Calculate the reciprocal of total stop delay for each individual according to expression (24);
Step 3: Sort the population in descending order according to the reciprocal of total stop delay; if the maximum generation reaches, then stop and decode the first individual as the optimal solution;
Step 4: Execute the selection routine according to the roulette strategy;
Step 5: Execute the crossover routine to the new population; and
Step 6: Execute the mutation routine to the new population, go to Step 2.
To generate the control parameters that satisfy the traffic signal optimization constraints, the following coding scheme is developed. Two real fractions, $\varepsilon_{1}$ and $\varepsilon_{2}\left(0 \leq \varepsilon_{1}, \varepsilon_{2} \leq 1\right)$, are used to code the green times for the 2 phases and the cycle length of the signal according to the following equations:

$$
\begin{gathered}
C_{\min }^{\prime}=\sum_{l=1}^{2} g_{l}^{\min }+\sum_{l=1}^{2} a_{l}+\sum_{l=1}^{2} a r_{l} \\
C=C_{\min }+\left(C_{\max }-C_{\min }\right) \cdot \varepsilon_{1} \\
g_{1}=g_{1}^{\min }+\left(C-C_{\min }^{\prime}\right) \cdot \varepsilon_{2} \\
g_{2}=g_{2}^{\min }+\left(C-C_{\min }^{\prime}\right) \cdot\left(1-\varepsilon_{2}\right)
\end{gathered}
$$

Equation (30) indicates that $C_{\text {min }}^{\prime}$ is not equal to $C_{\text {min }}$. It is the summation of minimum allowable green times and inter-green times for all phases. Equation (31) constrains the randomly generated cycle length to be within the maximum and minimum allowable cycle length. Using the cycle length generated above, Equations (32) and (33) allocate green time for each phase.

The basic logic of the heuristic solving algorithm is composed of two parts, which is illustrated by Figure 6. The standard genetic algorithm generates alternative signal timing parameters, and the cell-based simulation estimates the performance of alternative signal timing parameters with the proposed traffic flow model in Section 4. Each individual in population is decoded to a set of signal timings based on the aforementioned scheme. The objective value during the given analysis period $T$ which is calculated by expression (24) is taken as the fitness of the individual. Based on the fitness values of individuals in the population, the selection, crossover and mutation routines are executed.

According to the equations (30) through (33), two variables are necessary for randomly generating the signal timing plans. Therefore, an individual is composed of two random variables, $\varepsilon_{1}$ and $\varepsilon_{2}$, which are of uniform distribution between 0 and 1.0. The individual coding scheme is shown by Figure 7(a).

The crossover operation in Step 5 is to swap one of the two random variables of two randomly selected individuals. Take two individuals which are composed of $\varepsilon_{11}$ and $\varepsilon_{12}$, and $\varepsilon_{21}$ and $\varepsilon_{22}$, respectively, as example. The crossover operation is demonstrated by Figure 7(b,c). In the study, the crossover operations 1 and 2 are executed with equal opportunity. So, the detailed crossover routine is given as follow:

### 6.4.2. Sensitivity analyses

For each value of left turn yielding probability, we used our proposed model to obtain the optimum signal timing plan and its performance measures of stop delay and throughput at intersection level. Figure 11 illustrates the impact of left-turn yielding probability on the total throughput and total stop delay from our proposed model. One can observe that 100\% or 0\% left-turn yielding results in higher intersection delay and lower throughput than partial yielding. This is probably due to the fact that 100\% or 0\% leftturn yielding may incur blockage either on the shared left-through lane or on the opposing through lane. Under Scenario II, the left-turn traffic volume is less than the through volume, so the total stop delay in case of $p_{i, \text { through }}^{O}=0$ is much higher than that in case of $p_{i, \text { through }}^{O}=1$. However, as for the total throughput, the opposite trend can be observed. It seems that there exists an 'optimum' leftturn yielding probability (0.6-0.7 for Scenario II) for the intersection to achieve its best operational performance.

## 7. Conclusions

This paper contributes to developing a cell-based traffic signal optimization model for two-phase controlled intersections without the explicit left-turn yielding rule during permitted phasing, where aggressive left-turn maneuvers compete with the opposing through traffic to take the conflict point and pass the intersection. Such driving behaviors, very commonly observed at urban intersections in many developing countries, have been demonstrated to show significant impact on the intersection operational performance but have not been taken into consideration in the existing traffic signal design paradigms. To capture traffic dynamics and interactions between conflicting movements during permitted phases, a two-dimensional cell transmission framework is proposed, in which virtual cells are designed to model the approach diverge and exit merge and overlapping cells are introduced to model the movement conflicts within the intersection. The performance indices of traffic operation can be obtained based on the cell states, grounded on which a mathematical optimization model is developed and solved to design appropriate signal timing plans to improve the operational performance of such intersections.

Evaluation of the proposed model at a real-world intersection through both simulation and field analysis confirm the effectiveness of the proposed model over TRANSYT-7F for design of signal timings at two-phase controlled intersections without explicit left-turn yielding rules under a broad range of traffic demand and distribution patterns. It further indicates that aggressive left-turn maneuvers,
though not preventable at intersections in developing countries, will not always deteriorate the intersection performance if they are properly guided and considered in the traffic signal design. The proposed model can find its best application at intersections experiencing similar driver behaviors in many developing countries.

Future research along the line could be to apply the proposed model to a general road network and employ the rolling horizon scheme to achieve the acceptable computing efficiency for real-time operation.
