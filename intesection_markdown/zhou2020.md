#### Abstract

The concept of Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs) enables instant traffic information to be shared among vehicle networks. With this newly proposed concept, a vehicle's driving behaviour will no longer be solely based on the driver's limited and incomplete observation. By taking advantages of the shared information, driving behaviours of CAVs can be improved greatly to a more responsible, accurate and efficient level. This study proposed a reinforcement-learning-based car following model for CAVs in order to obtain an appropriate driving behaviour to improve travel efficiency, fuel consumption and safety at signalized intersections in real-time. The result shows that by specifying an effective reward function, a controller can be learned and works well under different traffic demands as well as traffic light cycles with different durations. This study reveals a great potential of emerging reinforcement learning technologies in transport research and applications.


Index Terms-Neural network, reinforcement learning, car-following, intersection, traffic light, machine learning, deep deterministic policy gradient, traffic oscillation.

## I. Introduction

URBANIZATION urges cities to increase their population and the total number of vehicles. Transportation system designed and constructed ages ago becomes overloaded, which causes traffic congestion and becomes a problem that affects every traveller. Researchers are putting their efforts in searching methods to increase current transportation capacity. It is believed that Connected and Automated Vehicle (CAV) can play a key role in traffic optimization by utilizing communication technologies. Ideas such as Vehicle to Vehicle communication (V2V) [1] and Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) [2]-[4] have been proposed to be a foundation of CAVs. Autonomous driving, which takes control from human drivers, is one of the characteristics of CAVs that requires a very feasible control mechanism to achieve. Previously, some control methods for CAV were developed from one of the

[^0]existing classical car-following models, and empirical examinations were conducted to validate them [5], [6]. However, the mechanism in driving is fundamentally different between human drivers and CAVs because the resources they use vary significantly, which can shift driving behaviour of a CAV to an entirely different direction. Therefore, the autonomous vehicle controller is not necessarily to be constrained by classical model-based approaches, the model of which has only been validated on human drivers. Therefore, new methodologies for developing dedicated controller for CAVs are required.

Regarding trajectory optimization at intersections, Ma et al. [7] proposed a CAV's controller that predesigns a smooth trajectory to encourage CAVs to arrive at an intersection only during green phase to avoid fully stop during red phase. However, this method computes the smoothed trajectories by given a known final status at an intersection and runs an optimization procedure repeatedly. It is no doubt that this method is suitable for making an optimized plan in a static environment. However, this method requires a final status for optimization, it may not be suitable for a case where no final status is provided.

Meanwhile, the state-of-art machine learning technologies have gained enormous attentions in many research areas. Reinforcement Learning (RL), a branch of this family, has achieved a couple of successes in playing video game [8] and board game [9], [10] against human players. With respect to carfollowing theory, attempts were made to develop a learningbased model for vehicles to learn an appropriate controller from scratch [11]-[13]. During the learning phase, new driving behaviour other than which inherited from human's can be established in a more efficient way.

Compared with re-developing a classical car-following model for CAVs, RL iteratively tries enormous action combinations, experiences a number of trajectories far more than any human can do, and breaks the empirical constraint of a classical model. Additionally, applying RL, instead of the trajectory optimization mentioned in Ma et al. [7], solves the computational issue caused by re-optimizing when changes occur in surroundings. Namely, RL exploits a learned behavioural model in real-time to mitigate re-planning overhead. This study bridges the gap towards a real-time CAV controller at road segments with signalized intersection by proposing an RL-based learning method.

In this paper, we select Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) algorithm [14] and define a meaningful rewardguided system for CAVs to form an appropriate behaviour. It is noted that other similar methods such as the A3C [15] and PPO [16] are available for this case. Results show that a regularized and robust control model is learned that is capable of being implemented under various traffic demands and different traffic light cycle durations. Compared with oscillated trajectories triggered by human drivers, CAVs' trajectories controlled by our proposed model indicate a significant improvement in the travel efficiency and the traffic oscillation can be largely eliminated. Additionally, it shows that one single behavioural model can run in a dynamic system in real-time and eliminate the effort of re-doing optimization process for each trajectory.

This rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 summarizes the literature review regarding traffic oscillations, CAVs, and RL-based traffic research; Section 3 introduces the proposed RL-based model for CAVs and the details of corresponding experimental design. Section 4 shows the experimental results and Section 5 concludes.

## II. Literature Review

Traffic oscillations are usually formed under heavy traffic flow conditions due to the heterogeneity of driver's responses to sudden changes of surrounding environment. In a dynamic traffic flow, a simple delay in responding of a human driver's perception may trigger a stopping chain known as traffic oscillation. A negative impact caused by traffic oscillation is known as increased fuel consumption and risk in safety [17]. The formation and propagation mechanism of it have been intensively investigated during recent years [18], [19].

The diversity of human driving behaviour is the main cause of traffic oscillation. Li et al. [20] found that on a congested highway, drivers are often forced to be engaged in repeated deceleration-acceleration cycles. The trigger of this phenomenon usually consists of traffic light control (such as ramp-metering), lane-changing maneuver and changes in roadway geometric features [21]. Signalized intersections due to alternating green and red light are another reason of oscillation forming [7], [22]. A congested pattern at the signalized intersection in Fig. 1 demonstrates that red light forces fast vehicles (blue) to slow down (red) and makes vehicles stop at the intersection. Oscillation is then formed and propagated to the upstream.

One approach to reducing the traffic oscillation is smoothing vehicle trajectories by Variable Speed Limits (VSL) that dynamically regulates traveling speed depending on realtime traffic flow information. VSL harmonizes overall speed in order to mitigate the negative effect caused by sudden brakes [23]. At signalized intersections, VSL is applied to alter a vehicle's arrival time without fully stopped during the red phase [24]. However, VSL's performance in regulating traffic oscillation is constrained by the compliance of drivers and differences in vehicles' acceleration profile [25].

Connected Automated Vehicle (CAV) takes the advantages of a shared network and makes use of the public information to control individual vehicle's trajectory precisely [26]-[28].

Fig. 1. Trajectory demonstration of traffic oscillation caused by traffic signal.

They are typically based on technologies such as Inter-Vehicle-Communication (IVC) [1], [29]-[31] or Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) [4]-[6], [32], [33]. Therefore, by communicating with others, the CAVs can drive differently than human drivers. To evaluate the performance of CAVs at roadway merging points, simulations conducted by van Arem et al. [34] and Zhou et al. [4] show that CAVs can outperform human drivers when having a specific model design corresponding to a typical circumstance such as highway-merging.

Several studies have shown that connected vehicles can help the traffic management [35]-[38]. Also, by utilizing the shared information such as traffic light durations, CAVs' trajectories can be pre-defined and optimized in a way to minimize fuel consumption, risk and increase efficiency [7], [22]. Thus, the traffic oscillation can be greatly mitigated in this case. Their models can be implemented close to real-time but the optimized and planned trajectories may still need to be changed when surrounding driving environment changes due to their fixed strategy.

Reinforcement learning has also been adopted in improving traffic performance [38]-[44]. It is shown that CAVs can stabilize car platoon in road with intersections [45]. In this study, we attempt to address the inefficiency issue in aforementioned methods by adopting a reinforcement-learning based approach. In reinforcement learning, it allows us to propose a self-learned behavioural model that can be applied in realtime to predict the trajectory without overhead issue even in a dynamic environment.

## A. Reinforcement Learning Background

A typical Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework contains an agent and an environment where the agent can perform an action and receive feedback from the environment [46]. Particularly, at each time $t$, the agent receives an observation or state $s_{t}$, outputs a probability distribution over the actions $P\left(a_{t}\right)$ under a policy $\pi$, then receives a reward $r_{t}$ and a transition dynamics $(p)$ of the next state $s_{t+1}$ from the
environment based on the current $s_{t}$ and $a_{t}$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\left(a_{t}\right) & =\pi\left(s_{t}\right) \\
p\left(s_{t+1}\right), r_{t} & =\operatorname{env}\left(a_{t}, s_{t}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

The agent in RL attempts to learn a function to map the observation to its future reward. The goal in reinforcement learning is to learn a policy that maximizes the expected return from the current state. A discounting factor $\gamma \in[0,1]$ is applied to compute the future return defined as the sum of discounted future rewards $R_{t}=\sum_{i=t}^{T} \gamma^{(i-t)} r\left(s_{i}, a_{i}\right)$ where $T$ denotes total steps in an episode. Many approaches of reinforcement learning use the Bellman equation to represent the recursive relationship in the future return.

$$
Q^{\pi}\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)=\mathrm{E}_{r_{t}, s_{t+1} \sim E}\left[r\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)+\gamma \mathrm{E}_{a_{t+1} \sim \pi}\left[Q^{\pi}\left(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1}\right)\right]\right]
$$

where $Q^{\pi}$ is the state-action value based on a stochastic policy $\pi$. Normally, a deterministic target policy $\mu$ is applied instead of the stochastic policy $\pi$, so that the inner expectation in (2) disappears and it can be rewritten as

$$
Q^{\mu}\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)=\mathrm{E}_{r_{t}, s_{t+1} \sim E}\left[r\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)+\gamma Q^{\mu}\left(s_{t+1}, \mu\left(s_{t+1}\right)\right)\right] .
$$

With this deterministic policy $\mu$, the agent is possible to learn $Q^{\mu}$ off-policy, which makes use of the experience collected from other agents or from this agent but a different time. Q-learning [47] is one of the off-policy algorithms which computes the greedy policy $\mu(s)=\arg \max _{a} Q(s, a)$. The $Q(s, a)$ in this equation estimates the state-action value. Replacing the Q function with the state-of-art neural network, a non-linear function approximator, solves many challenging RL problems. Therefore, a policy $\mu$ and the Q function can be parameterized by a neural network $\theta^{Q}$ with a loss function described as below to apply gradient descent to optimize its parameters.

$$
L\left(\theta^{Q}\right)=\mathrm{E}_{s_{t}, a_{t}, r_{t}}\left(\left(Q\left(s_{t}, a_{t} \mid \theta^{Q}\right)-y_{t}\right)^{2}\right)
$$

where

$$
y_{t}=r\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)+\gamma Q\left(s_{t+1}, \mu\left(s_{t+1}\right) \mid \theta^{Q}\right) .
$$

The $y_{t}$ in (4) and (5) is recognized as a Q-target in RL. It is found in the past that increasing the depth of a neural network can also increase its learning capacity but it introduces a non-convergence problem in RL. To address this issue, Mnih et al. [8] proposed a variation of the Q-learning algorithm named DQN which learns to play video games from pixel inputs. However, the Q-learning is an algorithm restricted by its discrete action output. In other words, it cannot output a continuous action such as to control acceleration rate or rotation angle. Fortunately, the Policy Gradient (PG) algorithm [48], [49] from another RL branch is capable of outputting an action in a continuous space. Based on the concept of PG, Konda and Tsitsiklis [50] proposed the Actor-Critic algorithm combining a policy function and value function to improve the stability of updating. By utilizing a function approximator such as a neural network to parameterize the Actor-Critic algorithm, it improves the performance dramatically in terms of speeding up training process and increasing the ability in non-linearity [51].

In PG, learning a stochastic policy may not be efficient and productive when the agent only needs a deterministic action to perform in a deterministic environment. Therefore, Silver et al. [52] proposed a Deterministic Policy Gradient (DPG) method that leads to an efficiency boost in training. The key idea behind DPG is the same as Actor-Critic algorithm as it separates the training into 1) update value function (Critic) to have a better evaluation of current situation, which improves the understanding of surrounding environment and causal relationship; 2) update policy function (Actor) directly from Critic to map the state to action in order to gain the better value. These updates aim to maximize the future return an agent receives, so the objective function for this purpose is shown as follows:

$$
J_{\beta}\left(\mu^{\theta}\right)=\int_{S} \rho^{\beta}(s) Q^{\mu}\left(s, \mu^{\theta}(s)\right) d s
$$

where $\mu^{\theta}$ is the target policy parameterized by $\theta$, $\rho^{\beta}(s)$ denotes the behaviour policy at state $s, Q^{\mu}\left(s, \mu^{\theta}(s)\right)$ represents the state-action value or Q-value evaluated from a critic and its action comes from the target policy. We can rewrite this objective function to obtain the parameter update as follows:

$$
\nabla_{\theta} J_{\beta}\left(\mu^{\theta}\right)=\mathrm{E}_{s \sim \rho^{\beta}}\left[\left.\nabla_{\theta} \mu^{\theta}(s) \nabla_{a} Q^{\mu}(s, a)\right|_{a=\mu^{\theta}(s)}\right]
$$

This equation gives the off-policy deterministic policy gradient. It indicates that the actor parameter updated by moving its parameter in the direction of the critic can maximize its Q-value.

The convergence and update stability issues usually exist in a complex RL problem, although it successfully improves the learning efficiency. The on-policy updating and nonlinear function approximator are found to be the cause. In other words, a correlation between two updates in successive states introduces unstable issue for challenging problems. The DQN mentioned above successively solves those issues, thus Lillicrap et al. [14] brings the advanced concept in the DQN to DPG and rename it to Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG). The DDPG is a combination of DQN and DPG in terms of creating a memory buffer and a target network in addition to DPG aiming to de-correlate successive updates. Both of the actor and critic in DDPG keep evaluating networks ( $\mu^{\theta}$ and $Q^{w}$ ) and target networks ( $\mu^{\bar{\theta}}$ and $Q^{\bar{w}}$ ) throughout the training process. The parameters updated in a target network are delayed for the purpose of de-correlating successive updates. The formal updating rule is shown in following equations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta_{t} & =r_{t}+\gamma Q^{\bar{w}}\left(s_{t+1}, \mu^{\bar{\theta}}\left(s_{t+1}\right)\right)-Q^{w}\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right) \\
w_{t+1} & =w_{t}+\alpha_{w} \delta_{t} \nabla_{w} Q^{w}\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right) \\
\theta_{t+1} & =\theta_{t}+\left.\alpha_{\theta} \nabla_{\theta} \mu^{\theta}\left(s_{t}\right) \nabla_{a} Q^{w}\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)\right|_{a=\mu^{\theta}(s)} \\
\bar{w} & \leftarrow \tau_{w} w+\left(1-\tau_{w}\right) \bar{w} \\
\bar{\theta} & \leftarrow \tau_{\theta} \theta+\left(1-\tau_{\theta}\right) \bar{\theta}
\end{aligned}
$$

The $\bar{w}$ and $\bar{\theta}$ in $Q^{\bar{w}}$ and $\mu^{\bar{\theta}}$ then be soft-replaced to $w$ and $\theta$ with a certain ratio in each training step defined in (9).

In addition, the state-action-reward transitions are stored in a memory buffer and randomly sampled for computing gradients.

Their results show that DDPG achieves a better control when the agent works on a continuous action space. In transportation research, different RL approaches have been applied to simulate human driving behaviours [11] or modelling CAVs [12], [13]. For CAVs control, one should be a continuous action problem but all of them discretise CAV's action. While the discretised action in RL requires outputting multiple values, then chooses the action with the maximum value. By doing this, it requires more computational resources and becomes time-consuming when including a great deal of discretised actions. To bridge this gap, we focus on the continuous action method and apply the DDPG model for controlling CAV's acceleration rate.

Multi-agent model is another branch in RL and becomes popular in recent years. In multi-agent RL, it provides each agent with different policy and value network to decentralize the control. By doing this, agents could learn different policy and be able to cooperate without sharing central control system or one unique controller [53], [54]. This concept offers agents a cooperative system, which shows potential in CAVs applications. However, from the result in Peng et al. [53] and Lowe et al. [54], their multi-agent models are trained in a fixed number of agents which constrains its scalability in a large system like CAVs. Although the cooperative mechanism in multi-agent RL has an attractive property, whereas due to the aforementioned shortcomings at this stage, in this study, we still apply a centralized control with all CAVs share the same learned controller.

## C. Input Features

In reinforcement learning, we map the state to action, which takes observed features as input to the system and outputs acceleration as its action. Therefore, features are crucial in the whole process. Although RL can learn from raw or unprocessed features, e.g., images [56], [57]. A welldesigned feature representation can maximize the learning efficiency. Feature selection is to find the observation terms to best correlate with our final goal. Feature engineering is necessary for many RL implementations in order to achieve fast convergence and precise control [58]. In this paper, we include the basic features adopted in conventional carfollowing models such as relative speed, gap distance and vehicle speed. Additionally, the traffic light plays a key role in this setup. Therefore, we summarize some representative features in TABLE I and Fig. 4.

## D. Reward Function

In practice, it is common to carefully hand-design the reward function to encourage a particular solution. Fig. 6 shows a handcraft reward function presenting the current traffic conditions a CAV faces. This reward function is threefold consisting of aspects in speed, time gap, and predicted arrival time at the intersection.

Information such as vehicle speed and time gap belong to the local conditions that shape a CAV's driving behaviours locally. An interaction between the vehicle and traffic light forms a global condition, which should be distinguished from its interaction with surrounding vehicles. At a signalized intersection, both local and global conditions are necessary and serve as the foundations of this reward function.

Specifically, we initialize the reward of a CAV to zero for each time step, then aggregate other reward signals (dash line in Fig. 6) into it. Each vehicle receives its own reward signal at each step during training and puts this reward to the memory buffer. The central controller then updates its parameters at this step using sampled reward signals, actions and states in the memory buffer. Driving with higher speed indicates less travel time, which should contribute to the reward (with a maximum of +1). However, punishment must be made to avoid speeding, so negative reward of -1 is given in this situation. Additionally, -1 is aggregated to the reward if the following vehicle maintains a time gap shorter than 0.8s to its leader. For a safety reason, the value 0.8s is selected slightly greater than the minimum value (0.6s) for CAVs found in [6]. Lastly, if a vehicle predicted to arrive the intersection on a red phase based on its current speed and distance to the intersection, a -1 signal will be assigned (solid line) to fully replace the aggregated reward. Otherwise, when a CAV finds that it will pass through the green phase, it will accumulate +0.1 to the original reward. To emphasize the benefit of passing green phase and minimize the outliers (vehicle runs through red light), we include a buffer time of three seconds attached at the start and end of the green phase to re-distribute the traffic flow. ## IV. Result and Discussion

## A. Training Result

In order to validate the robustness of the DDPG model, we conduct six epochs (six training attempts) with different random seeds and plot the moving averaged episodic reward in Fig. 7. In a typical RL training, the reward for each episode can be noisy. Therefore, we apply a moving averaging method to show the tendency of the total reward change. The moving averaged episodic reward is computed as

$$
R_{t} \leftarrow 0.8 R_{t-1}+0.2 R_{t}
$$

where $R_{t}$ denotes the reward at episode $t$.
The accumulative reward in an episode grows up quickly from the beginning of training. Especially at around 80,000 steps, the episodic reward grows up rapidly. After 100,000 steps, the model tends to converge at the value of 400. We randomly select one of the trained models and conduct several tests in the following sections.

On the other hand, the VT-Micro model is a widely used model for estimating fuel consumption $e$. It is noted that the truncation of speed and acceleration may cause underestimation of fuel consumption.

$$

\left.\left(\left\lfloor a_{n}(t)\right\rfloor-5 \mathrm{~km} / \mathrm{h} / \mathrm{sec}\right)^{j}\right]
\end{array}
$$

## V. Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a DDPG-based model and trained CAVs from scratch to obtain an appropriate behavioural model to fulfil the requirement of being a cooperative and automated vehicle at signalized intersections.

The result shows that the trained central controller can utilize the advantage of CAVs communicating with surrounding vehicles and traffic light systems to improve both travel efficiency and safety and reduce fuel consumption of CAVs in passing signalized intersections. A single behavioural model is learned to be applied under different traffic demands or traffic light cycles in real-time without the re-planning overhead. However, a few necessary assumptions about the model, such as a 0.5 s updating interval, need to be made to encourage efficient training. Moreover, it is worth noting that in this study, the CAV model is only trained and tested in an environment without any MV participant. A mixed traffic flow with both CAVs and MVs would have different properties, which may need a re-design of the training procedure for the model and is not covered in this study yet. Besides, each CAV can learn a decentralized policy or an individual driving model to form a cooperative scheme and multi-agent RL has potential in this field, which can be extended from this study. We will continue to study and discuss these possible implementations in our future work.
