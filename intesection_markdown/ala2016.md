# Modeling Evaluation of Eco-Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control in Vicinity of Signalized Intersections 

Mani Venkat Ala, Hao Yang, and Hesham Rakha

Vehicle stops caused by traffic signals reduce vehicle fuel economy ratings along arterial roadways. Eco-cooperative adaptive cruise control (eco-CACC) systems are being developed in an attempt to improve vehicle fuel efficiency in the vicinity of signalized intersections. These eco-CACC systems utilize traffic signal phasing and timing data received through vehicle-to-infrastructure communication, together with vehicle queue predictions, to compute fuel-optimum vehicle trajectories that are continuously updated as the vehicle travels in the vicinity of signalized intersections. The algorithm computes a desired speed for the vehicle that is either displayed to the driver or directly integrated into the vehicle's adaptive cruise control system. In this paper, the INTEGRATION microscopic traffic assignment and simulation software is used to evaluate the performance of a proposed eco-CACC algorithm to assess its networkwide energy and environmental impacts. A simulation sensitivity analysis demonstrates that as the market penetration rate of CACCequipped vehicles increases, the energy and environmental benefits also increase, and that the overall savings in fuel consumption are as high as $19 \%$ when the market penetration rate is $100 \%$. On multilane roads, the algorithm may produce networkwide increases in the fuel consumption level when the market penetration rate is less than 30\%. The analysis also demonstrates that the length of control segments, the signal phasing and timing plan, and the traffic demand levels significantly affect the algorithm performance. The study further demonstrates that the algorithm may produce increases in fuel consumption levels when the network is oversaturated; thus, further work is needed to enhance the algorithm for these conditions.

Recently, the rapid growth of passenger car and freight truck volumes has resulted in increased energy usage and vehicle emissions. In 2013, the U.S. transportation sector alone consumed over 135 billion gallons of motor fuel, 70\% of which was consumed by passenger cars and trucks (1). Globally, 60\% of oil is consumed by the transportation sector (2). The need to reduce the transportation sector fuel consumption levels requires researchers and policy makers to investigate various advanced strategies. Ecodriving, which aims to improve the

[^0]fuel efficiency of the transportation sector, is one efficient and costeffective strategy (2). Most ecodriving algorithms provide real-time driving advice, such as advisory speed limits, recommended acceleration or deceleration levels, and speed alerts to vehicles. Drivers can then adjust their driving behavior or take driving actions to reduce their fuel consumption and emission levels. To date, numerous studies indicate that ecodriving can reduce fuel consumption and greenhouse gas emissions by about 10\% on average (3).

The major causes of high vehicle emission and fuel consumption levels have been widely investigated. For example, research in Rakha and Crowther showed that frequent accelerations associated with stop-and-go waves were one major cause of greenhouse gas emissions in transportation systems (4). Excessive speeds over 60 mph and slow movements on a congested road also increased air pollutant emissions and fuel consumption levels dramatically (5). Complete stops on roads result in increased fuel usage. Consequently, reducing traffic oscillations and avoiding idling are two critical ways to increase vehicle fuel efficiency levels. In general, studies of ecodriving can be categorized into freeway-based and city-based strategies.

On freeways, traffic streams are continuous and vehicles are rarely disturbed by signals; that is, one vehicle can travel to a particular destination without any extra constraints (except on- and off-ramps). Developing ecodriving strategies that estimate speed or speed limits on the basis of road traffic conditions on freeways to change driving behavior and minimize fuel consumption and emissions is relatively straightforward. To date, numerous ecodriving strategies have been proposed to smooth traffic along freeways. Barth and Boriboonsomsin utilized vehicle-to-infrastructure communications to collect average link speed and variation and provide advisory speeds for drivers to reduce fuel consumption and emissions (6). Yang and Jin estimated advisory speed limits for drivers on the basis of the movements of surrounding vehicles with the assistance of vehicle-to-vehicle communications (7). Furthermore, Park et al. $(8,9)$ and Ahn et al. (10) developed a moving horizon dynamic programming eco-adaptive cruise control system and demonstrated the potential benefits of this system.

Unlike freeways, traffic stream motion on arterial roads is typically interrupted by traffic control devices, including traffic signals. Vehicles are forced to stop ahead of traffic signals when encountering red indications, producing shock waves within the traffic stream. These shock waves in turn result in vehicle acceleration and deceleration maneuvers and idling events, which increase vehicle fuel consumption and emission levels. Most research efforts have focused on optimizing traffic signal timings on the basis of approach volumes and vehicular queue lengths (11, 12). Recently, with the introduction of vehicle connectivity (also known as connected vehicles), individual
vehicles have begun to be controlled to reduce fuel consumption and emission levels [i.e., eco-cooperative adaptive cruise control (ecoCACC)]. Connected vehicles can exchange traffic information and establish communications with traffic signal controllers to receive signal phasing and timing data and vehicle queue information (13). These data can be used to optimize vehicle trajectories and reduce fuel consumption.

In recent years, environmental connected vehicle applications have attracted researchers' interest. These applications typically assist individual drivers in their travel along signalized roads by computing fuel-optimum trajectories. Mandava et al. (14) and Xia et al. (15) proposed a velocity planning algorithm based on signal information to maximize the probability of encountering a green indication when approaching multiple intersections. The algorithm attempted to reduce fuel consumption by minimizing acceleration and deceleration levels while avoiding complete stops. However, lowering acceleration-deceleration levels does not necessarily reduce fuel consumption levels. Asadi and Vahidi utilized signal information to design optimal cruise speeds for equipped vehicles to minimize the probability of stopping at signalized intersections during red indications (16). Malakorn and Park used signal phasing and timing information and developed a CACC system to minimize the absolute acceleration levels of equipped vehicles (17). Barth et al. developed a dynamic ecodriving system for arterial roads that computed the optimum acceleration-deceleration levels to minimize the total tractive power demand and idling time so that the fuel consumption levels were reduced (18). Rakha and Kamalanathsharma, Kamalanathsharma and Rakha, and Kamalanathsharma et al. used recursive path-finding principles to construct a dynamic programming-based fuel-optimization strategy and evaluated it through the use of an agent-based model (19-21). De Nunzio et al. used a combination of pruning algorithms and optimal control to find the best possible green wave if vehicles were to receive signal phasing information from multiple upcoming intersections (22). Muñoz-Organero and Magaña developed a smartphone application to evaluate the impact on vehicle fuel consumption of an ecodriving assistant that maintained moderate deceleration-acceleration behavior (23).

Yang et al. proposed an eco-CACC algorithm based on vehicle-toinfrastructure communication that optimized vehicle fuel consumption levels in the vicinity of signalized intersections (24). Unlike other algorithms in the literature, the algorithm accounted for the impact of surrounding traffic. Moreover, the advisory speed limits computed by the algorithm were provided to vehicles both upstream and downstream of signalized intersections, to optimize vehicle accelerations downstream of an intersection. This paper develops a simulation environment based on INTEGRATION to assess the benefits of the proposed eco-CACC algorithm (25,26). The study first incorporates the proposed eco-CACC algorithm into the INTEGRATION software to realize vehicle-to-signal communications and to estimate advisory speed limits. Subsequently, a sensitivity analysis is conducted to quantify the impact of the eco-CACC vehicle market penetration rate (MPR), traffic signal timing, length of control segments, and demand levels on the algorithm performance. The effectiveness of the algorithm for different intersection configurations is also investigated. Finally, the limitations of the algorithm under extreme road and traffic conditions are examined.

The next section reviews the eco-CACC-Q algorithm (the ecoCACC algorithm that considers queue effects) developed in Yang et al. (24). Sensitivity analyses of the eco-CACC MPR, the intersection configuration, the signal timing and phasing plan, and the demand levels on the algorithm performance are then presented. In addition, the limitations of the algorithm are discussed. The benefits of the algorithm for a four-leg intersection are then presented. Finally, the paper summarizes the findings of the analysis and presents the conclusions of the study.

## METHODOLOGY

This study attempts to assess the environmental benefits of the eco-CACC algorithm proposed in Yang et al. (24). The INTEGRATION microscopic traffic assignment and simulation model is used in this assessment (25, 26). In this section, a brief description of the proposed eco-CACC-Q algorithm is presented.

The algorithm utilizes signal phasing and timing data obtained through vehicle-to-infrastructure communication to develop a fueloptimum vehicle trajectory in the vicinity of signalized intersections. To optimize the equipped vehicles' trajectories, the eco-CACC-Q algorithm computes a speed limit and sends it to the equipped vehicles. Unlike the algorithm in Kamalanathsharma et al.-the eco-CACC-O algorithm, which did not consider queue effects (21)-the eco-CACC-Q algorithm applies the vehicle queue length when computing the fuel-optimum trajectory.

As shown in Figure $1 a$, a series of vehicles approach and attempt to proceed through an intersection. The signal is located at position $x_{s}$. Once an equipped vehicle enters the section $\left[x_{u}, x_{d}\right]$, the eco-CACC-Q algorithm is activated and continues to be activated until the vehicle leaves the section. Upstream of the intersection, $\left[x_{u}, x_{s}\right]$, when the probe vehicle is delayed by the red light or the vehicle queue ahead, the algorithm estimates an advisory speed limit $\left[v_{e}(t)\right]$ to minimize the vehicle fuel consumption over the entire section $\left[x_{u}, x_{d}\right]$. The advisory speed limit allows the vehicle to decelerate at a constant deceleration $\left(a_{-}\right)$to a cruise speed $\left(v_{c}\right)$ so that the vehicle arrives at the tail of the queue just when the queue is dissipated $\left(t=t_{c}\right)$ or at the stop bar when the signal turns to green $\left(t=t_{g}\right)$ if there is no queue upstream of the traffic signal. Downstream of the intersection $\left[x_{u}, x_{s}\right]$, the algorithm optimizes the vehicle acceleration from $v_{c}$ to the road speed limit $\left(v_{f}\right)$ at a constant level $\left(a_{+}\right)$. Figure $1 b$ illustrates the trajectory of a vehicle that is using the eco-CACC-Q algorithm. Compared with the base case (black line), the trajectory of the eco-CACC-Q controlled vehicle is much smoother, and the stop delay is avoided completely.

The objective of the eco-CACC-Q algorithm is to minimize the vehicle fuel consumption over the distance $\left[x_{u}, x_{d}\right]$. In addition to the shapes of the vehicle trajectories, the algorithm finds the optimum upstream deceleration level ( $a_{-}$) and the optimum downstream acceleration level $\left(a_{+}\right)$that minimize the total fuel consumed while traveling over the section $\left[x_{u}, x_{d}\right]$. The mathematical formulation of the algorithm can be cast as

$$
\min _{a, a+} \int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+T} F(t) d t
$$

subject to

$$
v_{e}(t)= \begin{cases}v_{0}-a_{-}\left(t-t_{0}\right) & t_{0} \leq t<t_{0}+\delta t_{1} \\ v_{c} & t_{0}+\delta t_{1} \leq t<t_{c} \\ v_{c}+a_{+}\left(t-t_{c}\right) & t_{c} \leq t<t_{c}+\delta t_{2} \\ v_{f} & t_{c}+\delta t_{2} \leq t \leq t_{0}+T\end{cases}
$$

F(t)= & \text { vehicle fuel consumption at any instant } t \text { computed with the } \\
& \text { VT-CPFM model (27), } \\
v_{0}= & \text { speed of vehicle at location } x_{d}, \\

a_{-}^{s}= & \text { saturation deceleration level, and } \\
a_{+}^{s}= & \text { saturation acceleration level. }
\end{aligned}

Equation $1 b$ demonstrates that, given the traffic stream, including the queue length, the start and end times of each traffic signal indication, and the approaching speed of the equipped vehicles, the speed profile varies as a function of $a_{-}$and $a_{+}$. The eco-CACC-Q algorithm finds the two acceleration levels within the ranges defined in Equation $1, g$ and $h$, to minimize the fuel consumption of the equipped vehicle over the entire control section. The details of the eco-CACC-Q algorithm are described below.

1. For an equipped vehicle $k$ that enters the segment $\left[x_{u}, x_{d}\right]$, the eco-CACC-Q algorithm is activated.
2. Upstream of the intersection
-The algorithm provides a time-dependent desired speed to the vehicle's adaptive cruise control system and considers the following two scenarios (otherwise, the desired speed is set as the road speed limit $v_{f}$ ):

- Currently, the indication is green; however, the indication will turn red when the vehicle arrives at the stop bar if the vehicle continues at its current speed.
- Currently, the traffic signal indication is red and will continue to be red when the vehicle arrives at the stop bar at the vehicle's current speed.
-Once either of the scenarios described above occurs, the queue length will be computed on the basis of the number of vehicles ahead of the equipped vehicle, and the dissipation time of the queue $\left(t_{c}\right)$ will be estimated on the basis of the speed of the rarefaction wave.
-In this paper, it is assumed that vehicles in the queue stop completely, and the density along the queue is the maximum road density [i.e., the jam density $\left(\rho_{j}\right)$ ]. Once the traffic signal indication turns green, the queue is released at the saturated flow rate $\left(q_{c}\right)$. Hence, on the basis of the kinematic wave model (28, 29), the speed of the rarefaction wave is $v_{w}=q_{c} / \rho_{c}-\rho_{j}$.
-The algorithm uses Equation 1 to estimate the optimum upstream deceleration level and the downstream acceleration level to minimize the total fuel consumption level over the control segment and provides a desired speed to the CACC-equipped vehicle to be used over the next time step $t+\Delta t$, where $\Delta t$ is the updating interval.

3. Downstream of the intersection, the algorithm computes the fuel-optimum acceleration level from the vehicle's current speed to the roadway speed limit $\left(v_{f}\right)$ over the roadway segment $\left[x_{s}, x_{d}\right]$.
4. Once the equipped vehicle arrives at position $x_{d}$, the algorithm is deactivated.

## SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS

This section describes a sensitivity analysis of the proposed algorithm. The analysis considers the impact of the MPR of the equipped vehicles, the number of lanes of the controlled segments, the timing plan of the traffic signal, the length of the control segments, and the traffic demand levels. The limitations of the algorithm are also analyzed and discussed.

As a starting point, a simple intersection is simulated (as shown in Figure 1a), at which vehicles are only loaded from one origin and exit at one destination (i.e., only one-direction through traffic is simulated). For the signal phasing and timing plan, the cycle length is set at $C=84 \mathrm{~s}$ and the green and amber durations are 40 and 2 s, respectively. For all the case studies below, it is assumed that the speed limits of all roads are 50 mph and the saturation flow rates are all 1,600 vehicles per hour per lane (vphpl). The INTEGRATION software is used to model the movements of individual vehicles, including the control of CACC-equipped vehicles, and to quantify the networkwide energy and environmental impacts of the system (24, 25, 30).

## Algorithm Performance on Multilane Roads

The previous subsection investigated single-lane intersection approaches, on which lane-changing behavior is restricted. However, in reality, lane changes exist and cannot be ignored. In this subsection, the eco-CACC-Q and eco-CACC-O algorithms are both applied to multilane intersection approaches, on which the links approaching and leaving the signal have more than one lane. It is assumed that both links have two lanes and that the demand is 300 vphpl. The settings of the two algorithms are the same as in the subsection above.

Figure $2 a$ compares the energy benefits of both the algorithms for different MPRs under a two-lane intersection. Unlike the single-lane analysis, fuel savings are not always observed for different MPRs. When the MPR is less than 30\%, the application of either algorithm increases the overall fuel consumption levels. The negative impact under low MPRs is caused by the lane-changing behavior of nonequipped vehicles on the two-lane roads. In both algorithms, equipped vehicles typically travel more slowly than the nonequipped vehicles around them, leaving larger gaps ahead. When the MPR is small, the nonequipped vehicles can accelerate to pass equipped ones and cut into the gaps ahead. This behavior results in traffic oscillations on the roads and increases the networkwide fuel consumption levels. However, when the MPR is greater than 30\%, it is possible that two equipped vehicles could move side by side, block both lanes, and restrict the nonequipped vehicles from passing. Thus, the number of lane changes would be reduced significantly. Both algorithms produce positive results once the MPR is greater than $30 \%$. If all vehicles are controlled, the eco-CACC-Q algorithm produces fuel savings as high as 19\% and the eco-CACC-O algorithm produces savings of 16\%. Similar to the single-lane intersection, the eco-CACC-Q algorithm performs better than eco-CACC-O algorithm.

## Sensitivity to Phase Splits

The signal phasing and timing plan determines the split of each phase, and the performance of the intersection is highly related to the split. As the eco-CACC algorithms use the signal phasing and timing information to compute the optimum trajectories of equipped vehi- cles, the impact of the phase split has to be carefully examined. In this subsection, a sensitivity analysis of the phase split is introduced.

The previously described single-lane intersection is simulated with the same link characteristics and eco-CACC algorithm settings. Vehicles are loaded at a rate of 300 vph and an MPR of 20\%. The phase split varies from 0.3 to 0.7 along the road. Figure $2 b$ compares, for different phase splits, the average fuel consumption rates of each vehicle for the base case without control and the eco-CACC-O and eco-CACC-Q cases. The results demonstrate that for longer phase lengths, vehicles have a higher probability of passing the intersection without experiencing the red indication (i.e., they are less likely to be stopped by the signal). Therefore, they can travel smoothly to their destination with less fuel consumption. As for savings in fuel consumption, Figure $2 c$ shows that the phase length does not affect the algorithm performance and that differences do not exceed 1\%. Moreover, the comparison between eco-CACC-Q and eco-CACC-O also verifies the benefits of considering the queue. For all phase lengths, eco-CACC-Q produces the lowest fuel consumption, with savings in the range of 1\% to 2\% higher than that for eco-CACC-O control.

## Impact of Control Segment Length on Algorithm Performance

The length of the control segment, especially the upstream segment length $(d)$, is expected to have a significant impact on the algorithm performance. In this subsection, a sensitivity analysis of the impact of $d$ on the algorithm performance for the single-lane and two-lane intersections is presented.

First, the previously described single-lane intersection is simulated with the same link characteristics and signal phasing and timing plan. Vehicles are loaded at a rate of 500 vph. The settings for the eco-CACC-Q algorithm are the same, except that the upstream control segment length $(d)$ varies from 200 to 700 m. This range is based on the effective distance of dedicated short-range communication technology, which is implemented to communicate between vehicles and signals. Li indicated that the effective distance varied from 10 m to 1 km (31). Given that the control length cannot be very short, a range of 200 to 700 m was arbitrarily selected.

Figure 3a compares the fuel savings for different control lengths for various MPRs. With higher MPRs, the savings for the different $d$ values are larger. Moreover, a comparison of the different $d$ values shows that longer lengths result in larger savings. At an MPR of 100\%, a 700-m control segment reduces fuel consumption by as much as $18 \%$; for a $200-\mathrm{m}$ segment, savings in fuel consumption are approximately 12\%. The observation is reasonable as the longer length allows the CACC vehicles to receive signal phasing and timing information earlier, and the vehicles have a longer time in which to control their movements. In addition, Figure 3a shows that when the control length is longer than 500 m, the savings do not improve. The study demonstrates that a 500-m segment is long enough to achieve the desired benefits.

## Algorithm Shortcomings

In the implementation of the eco-CACC-Q algorithm, one critical assumption is that the studied network should not be oversaturated; otherwise, the estimation of queue length is not accurate. In that sense, the advisory speed limits cannot control the behavior of probe vehicles sufficiently to minimize their fuel consumption levels. In this subsection, the previously defined single-lane intersection is simulated to demonstrate this limitation.

The link parameters, the signal phasing and timing plan, and the eco-CACC-Q algorithm parameters are kept the same in the simulation runs. Vehicles are loaded at a rate of 800 vph, which is greater than the actual capacity of the control segment and thus results in oversaturated delay. Figure 5 shows the vehicular trajectories around the intersection before and after the application of the ecoCACC-Q algorithm; the signal is located at $x=2,000 \mathrm{~m}$. Figure 5a shows that the queues upstream of the signal are very long and that some vehicles in the queue have to wait for two or three cycles to proceed through the intersection. The figure therefore demonstrates that those vehicles experience more than one stop-and-go maneuver. Figure $5 b$ shows the trajectories of all vehicles when the MPR

There are two major causes of the problem. First, when the road is oversaturated, the queue might not dissipate during a single green indication. Subsequently, in the next cycle, the unreleased queue is not formed at the stop bar, located at position $x_{s}$, and, instead, rolls between the intersection and the starting point of the control segment $\left(x_{u}\right)$. Thus, the queue estimation method based on the kinematic wave model proposed in Yang et al. (24) cannot update the queue length correctly on the basis of the instantaneous traffic information collected by the loop detectors. Unless historical road conditions are provided, the estimation cannot be accurate. Second, the algorithm assumes that controlled vehicles only perform deceleration upstream of the traffic signal and that they enter the segment at a high speed (such as the road speed limit). But the rolling queue generates several stop-and-go waves on the control segment, which prevents the equipped vehicles from maintaining the recommended speed estimated by the algorithm. When those vehicles enter the rolling queue, they slow down and maintain a low speed
even if there is a large gap ahead (see the black box in Figure $5 b$ ). These two causes can be eliminated when vehicle-to-vehicle communications are introduced. With the assistance of that technology, the queue length can be updated in real time, and the stop-and-go behavior can be identified. The algorithm also has to be updated to reflect that the vehicle will stop multiple times.

## EVALUATION OF ECO-CACC-Q

In the previous sections, only one approach to an intersection was simulated. In reality, vehicles can pass through an intersection from, typically, four approaches (see Figure 6a). In this section, a comprehensive simulation analysis is conducted that considers a four-leg intersection and examines the energy efficiency of the eco-CACC-Q algorithm.

## CONCLUSIONS

The paper presents the results of an evaluation of an eco-CACC-Q algorithm through the use of INTEGRATION simulation software. The algorithm uses signal phasing and timing data received from the traffic signal controller through vehicle-to-infrastructure communication and predicts the expected queue at the approach to compute a fuel-optimum vehicle trajectory, both upstream and downstream of the signalized intersection. The algorithm provides desired speed estimates to adaptive cruise control-equipped vehicles. The study presents a comprehensive sensitivity analysis to quantify the environmental benefits of the algorithm in reducing fuel consumption levels. The simulation of a single-lane intersection indicated that with higher MPRs of equipped vehicles, the fuel consumption savings were greater. When all vehicles were equipped with the eco-CACC-Q algorithm, the overall fuel consumption was reduced by as much as $19 \%$. The results on multilane intersections were different because of the intense lane-changing behavior around equipped vehicles that resulted in increased overall fuel consumption levels for MPRs less than 30\%. Only when the MPR was greater than 30\% could positive benefits be achieved, and with higher MPRs, the benefits increased. In addition, the comparison of the eco-CACC-Q and eco-CACC-O algorithms demonstrated that considering the impact of vehicle queues ahead of traffic signals enhanced the algorithm performance.

The study also demonstrated that for longer phase lengths, the overall fuel consumption was reduced. However, the phase length had a minimal impact on the algorithm performance. In addition, the results indicated that the fuel consumption savings increased with an increase in the control length, and a 500-m control segment provided the greatest fuel consumption savings. Finally, in the case of a four-leg intersection, the eco-CACC-Q algorithm reduced the networkwide fuel consumption by as much as 12.5\%.

The current algorithm has not been designed to deal with oversaturated conditions. There are two potential solutions to this drawback. First, through vehicle-to-vehicle communication a better estimate of vehicle queues could be achieved. Second, the introduction of speed harmonization could be used to restrict traffic from entering intersections to ensure that the traffic signal did not become oversaturated (32). Alternatively, the green driving algorithm developed earlier could be used to mitigate the impact of stop-and-go waves (7). Currently, the algorithm only minimizes the fuel consumption of vehicles across a single intersection. Further enhancements would entail extending the logic to consider multiple intersections. Finally, the algorithm could be tested while other factors-including road capacity, speed limit, vehicle composition, and road grade- were considered.
