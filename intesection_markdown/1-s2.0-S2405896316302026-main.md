# Intersection control for automated vehicles with MILP 

Eduardo Rauh Müller, Rodrigo Castelan Carlson, Werner Kraus Junior<br>Department of Automation and Systems Engineering Federal University of Santa Catarina Florianópolis, SC, 88040-900 Brazil<br>(e-mail: edurauh@gmail.com, rodrigo.carlson@ufsc.br, werner.kraus@ufsc.br).


#### Abstract

We present a centralized Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP)-based strategy for intersection control in an urban environment of highly automated vehicles. The proposed strategy minimizes vehicle delay at the intersection approaches. This is achieved by decomposing the overall problem in three subproblems: (i) the computation of feasible arrival times for each vehicle; (ii) a MILP scheduling problem of vehicle arrival time at the intersection; and (iii) a motion planning problem for vehicles to reach the intersection at the scheduled time. The resulting optimal arrival strategy is shown in simulation to adequately manage traffic for the simple intersection considered.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Intersections on urban roads are characterized by two or more conflicting traffic streams willing to pass through its area. Intersections are critical points in transportation networks both in terms of traffic efficiency and safety. Besides constituting bottlenecks for traffic flow, a large portion of accidents take place at or near intersections. In the United States, over 23\% of vehicle crashes in 2013 were intersection related (NHTSA, 2013).

Currently, the usual solution for managing traffic at busy intersections is the use of traffic lights to coordinate human drivers. However, the advances in automation and communication systems are giving a new direction to intersection management research that is now pushed by the increasing automation of vehicles. The fact that automated vehicles should be able to react much faster and reliably than human drivers, and the prospect of a connected vehicles environment where vehicles are capable of sharing information via vehicle-to-vehicle or vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I) communication systems, enables strategies that were not possible (or practical) before. Proposed strategies for intersection management when automated vehicles are present involve the use of multi-agent systems (Dresner and Stone, 2008), Petri Nets (Naumann et al., 1997), tree search (Li and Wang, 2006), dynamic programming (Yan et al., 2009), heuristics such as ant colonies (Wu et al., 2012), and sophisticated optimization models (Lee and Park, 2012; Zhu and Ukkusuri, 2015), to cite a few.

In this work, we present a strategy for intersection control with automated vehicles aiming at minimizing vehicle

[^0] delay while guaranteeing safety. In our strategy vehicles arriving at the intersection are scheduled by a Mixed Integer Linear Program (MILP). The constraints of the MILP are obtained by finding the intervals of feasible arrival times for each vehicle, a subproblem for which there is an analytic solution. The obtained schedules are then used as the input for a motion planning problem. This approach is inspired by Oliveira et al. (2002), who decomposed the intersection control problem in a similar way. However, an optimization problem was not formally defined and they obtained the schedule using a tree search. The strategy proposed here is valid for a simple intersection, and will be expanded upon in future work. The Aimsun microscopic simulator (TSS, 2013a) is used for evaluating the proposed strategy.

## 2. PROBLEM FORMULATION

### 2.1 Intersection layout and automated vehicles description

We design our control strategy for a simple intersection with two orthogonal one-lane approaches, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Vehicles approaching the intersection follow a straight path, i.e., turning is not considered. We assume free flow conditions both upstream of the approaches and downstream of the intersection.

For the intersection in Fig. 1, we define the Control Region (CR) as the area comprised by the intersection itself and a portion of each of the two approaches within a distance $C_{\mathrm{d}, k}$ (Control Distance) upstream from the intersection, $k \in\{\mathrm{e}, \mathrm{n}\}$, with e and n denoting the eastbound and northbound approaches, respectively. A centralized Intersection Controller (IC) is responsible for coordinating the movement of all vehicles inside the CR.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/5ffc3c2b-4c44-4f09-8ade-3da6c1f7d5ef-2.jpg?height=590&width=838&top_left_y=217&top_left_x=159)
Fig. 1. Intersection layout of two single-lane approaches and a controlled region (in gray).

All vehicles have length $l$ and width $w$; maximum and minimum accelerations $a_{\text {max }}>0$ and $a_{\text {min }}<0$; and maximum desired speed $v_{\text {max }}$. Vehicles can only travel forward along the road direction and are able to communicate their current position and speed to the IC.

Vehicles obey all commands received from the IC while inside the CR without driver interference. The commands are received in the form of speed profiles to be followed, and we assume vehicles are able to follow these profiles while keeping in their lanes. As such, vehicles should be (at least) highly automated (SAE International, 2014). Vehicle behavior outside the CR is beyond the scope of this work, but we assume they are able to travel safely and (attempt to) keep speed $v_{\text {max }}$. We disregard the interference of pedestrians or obstacles. Communication, sensor data and the ability of vehicles to follow commands are all assumed to be perfect.

### 2.2 Problem description

Given the intersection and vehicles described above, we are interested in the problem of allowing all vehicles that enter the CR to leave it with minimum delay while avoiding collisions.

For our simple intersection, minimizing vehicle delay is equivalent to minimize the time to exit the intersection. For simplicity, we assume that all vehicles cross the intersection with speed $v_{\text {max }}$. In this case the given problem is equivalent to finding the minimum time to enter the intersection. Since vehicles should reach the intersection on time while avoiding collisions, we must find feasible speed profiles for each vehicle inside the CR such that we guarantee that each vehicle reaches the intersection at the scheduled time instant. We call the overall strategy developed to solve this problem the Optimal Arrival Time Strategy (OATS).

In order to avoid collisions, we stipulate two safety rules:

(i) The headway between two vehicles from the same approach must be at least as great as a predetermined longitudinal headway $h_{\mathrm{L}}$.
(ii) The time between two vehicles from different approaches crossing the intersection must be at least as great as a transversal headway $h_{\mathrm{T}}$.

### 2.3 Solution approach

We divide the OATS into three subproblems. The first subproblem (SP1) consists in determining, given the current position and speed of a vehicle inside the CR, the minimum and maximum time instants at which it can reach the intersection, while constrained by maximum and minimum acceleration and speeds. This is done by solving linear motion equations.

The second sub-problem (SP2) is the problem of obtaining, given the feasible time interval at which vehicles can reach the intersection (obtained in SP1), an optimum schedule for the instant vehicles in the CR must reach it (i.e., the schedule with the minimum aggregate arrival time). We formulate this problem as a MILP.

The third and final sub-problem (SP3) consists in determining, given the scheduled arrival time, current speed and position for each vehicle, a feasible speed profile such that the vehicle is able to arrive at the expected time at the intersection, while obeying speed, acceleration and headway constraints. Note that this problem typically has multiple solutions, unless the vehicle was scheduled to arrive exactly at either its maximum or minimum feasible arrival time.

With this approach most nonlinear aspects of the OATS are treated in SP1 and SP3, so the optimization of vehicle arrivals at the intersection can be modeled as a MILP. Each of these sub-problems is solved every time a given arbitrarily small time interval $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ elapses, so that even if vehicles do not exactly follow the planned trajectories, new ones are calculated at the next interval. This allows to compensate for eventual errors and to formulate SP3 merely as the problem of finding a speed profile to be followed during the next interval.

Another possible approach would be to calculate the entire speed profile (until the arrival at the intersection) in SP3 and design a supervisory system that calculates vehicles trajectories only when a new vehicle enters the CR or a vehicle already inside the CR strays from its planned trajectory. However, this has no impact in the formulation of SP2, and the former (simpler) approach is adopted in this work.

Next, we define each of the three sub-problems in detail. For simplicity, the current time instant is always considered to be $t_{0}=0$, and all time instants are given relative to $t_{0}$.
2.4 SP1 - Finding the interval of feasible arrival times for each vehicle

Consider vehicle $i$ inside the CR, traveling with speed $v_{i}$ at a distance $d_{i}$ from the intersection at time instant $t_{0}$. Vehicle speed and acceleration are constrained by $v_{\text {max }}$, $a_{\text {max }}$ and $a_{\text {min }}$, and every vehicle must be traveling with speed $v_{\text {max }}$ at the time it reaches the intersection. Thus, an interval $\left[t_{i}^{\text {min }}, t_{i}^{\text {max }}\right]$ of feasible arrival times exist for vehicle $i$ to reach the intersection, with $0 \leq t_{i}^{\text {min }} \leq t_{i}^{\text {max }}$. SP1 is the problem of finding this interval.

Clearly, $t_{i}^{\text {min }}$ is the time at which vehicle $i$ would reach the intersection if it applied acceleration $a_{\text {max }}$ until $v_{\text {max }}$
is reached, and then traveled at constant speed $v_{\text {max }}$. We assume the $C_{\mathrm{d}}$ is sufficiently long such that this is always possible for a vehicle entering the CR with $0 \leq v_{i} \leq v_{\text {max }}$.

For the purpose of determining $t_{i}^{\text {max }}$, suppose there is a vehicle with speed $v_{i}=0$ located in a position $s_{0}$ at an intersection approach such that, if this vehicle accelerates with constant acceleration $a_{\text {max }}$, it reaches speed $v_{\text {max }}$ exactly when it arrives at the intersection (i.e., $s_{0}$ is the furthest position at which a vehicle with initial speed $v_{i}=0$ and maximum acceleration $a_{\text {max }}$ is able to reach the intersection with speed $v_{\text {max }}$ ). We call the trajectory performed by this vehicle the boundary trajectory (BT). The dashed line in Fig. 2 illustrates the BT while the trajectory of a vehicle decelerating from $v_{\text {max }}$ down to zero with $a_{\text {min }}$ is represented by the solid line. The arrows indicate the direction of vehicle movement, and the intersection is located at $s_{i}$.

Given the problem constraints, any given position and speed pair that is between the BT and the intersection in Fig. 2, i.e., to the right of the BT, is a state from which it is impossible to reach $v_{\text {max }}$ before arriving at the intersection. Conversely, it is always possible to find a trajectory for a state touching or to the left side of the BT. Therefore, $t_{i}^{\text {max }}$ is equal to the time it would take for vehicle $i$ to touch the BT while decelerating with $a_{i}=a_{\text {min }}$, and then follow the remaining of the BT. This can be obtained analytically by solving equations derived from linear motion equations. The black colored portion of both curves in the figure corresponds to this trajectory.

There are several ways of obtaining $t_{i}^{\text {max }}$. As an example, given vehicle $i$ traveling initially with speed $v_{i}$ at a distance $d_{i}$ from the intersection, the distance $d_{i, \mathrm{~d}}$ it travels while decelerating with $a_{\text {min }}$ until it touches the BT is given by

$$
d_{i, \mathrm{~d}}=\frac{1}{2} \frac{v_{\max }^{2}-v_{i}^{2}-2 a_{\max } d_{i}}{a_{\min }-a_{\max }}
$$

and the minimum speed $v_{\text {min }}$ it is traveling the moment it touches the BT is given by the positive real solution of

$$
v_{\min }=\sqrt{v_{i}^{2}+2 a_{\min } d_{i, \mathrm{~d}}} .
$$

### 2.5 SP2 - Scheduling vehicle arrivals at the intersection

We formulate SP2 as a MILP, in which we minimize the total time vehicles spend to arrive at the intersection. The problem constraints are given by the safety rules mentioned in Section 2.2 and the feasible arrival intervals. Formally:

$$
S P 2: \min \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} t_{\mathrm{e}, i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m} t_{\mathrm{n}, j}\right)
$$

s.t.:

$$
\begin{array}{lr}
t_{\mathrm{e}, i}^{\min } \leq t_{\mathrm{e}, i} \leq t_{\mathrm{e}, i}^{\max } & i=1, \ldots, n \\
t_{\mathrm{n}, j}^{\min } \leq t_{\mathrm{n}, j} \leq t_{\mathrm{n}, j}^{\max } & j=1, \ldots, m \\
t_{\mathrm{e}, i}+h_{L} \leq t_{\mathrm{e}, i+1} & i=1, \ldots, n-1 \\
t_{\mathrm{n}, j}+h_{L} \leq t_{\mathrm{n}, j+1} & j=1, \ldots, m-1 \\
t_{\mathrm{e}, i}+h_{T} \leq t_{\mathrm{n}, j}+M b_{i, j} & i=1, \ldots, n ; j=1, \ldots, m \\
t_{\mathrm{n}, j}+h_{T} \leq t_{\mathrm{e}, i}+M\left(1-b_{i, j}\right) & i=1, \ldots, n ; j=1, \ldots, m \\
b_{i, j} \in 0,1 & i=1, \ldots, n ; j=1, \ldots, m
\end{array}
$$

in which the decision variables $t_{\mathrm{e}, i}$ and $t_{\mathrm{n}, j}$ are the time instants, counting from the current time, in which vehicle $i$ (or $j$ ) traveling in the eastbound (or northbound) approach reaches the intersection and $t_{\mathrm{e}, i}^{\text {max }}$ and $t_{\mathrm{e}, i}^{\text {min }}$ (or $t_{\mathrm{n}, j}^{\text {max }}$ and $t_{\mathrm{n}, j}^{\text {min }}$ ) are the maximum and minimum feasible arrival times for each vehicle obtained by solving SP1. Vehicles are numbered $i=1, \ldots, n$ and $j=1, \ldots, m$ for the eastbound and northbound approaches, respectively, and are ordered by proximity to the intersection. Constraints (5)-(6) assure that a feasible speed profile exists for each vehicle $i$ (or $j$ ) to reach the intersection at the scheduled time $t_{\mathrm{e}, i}$ (or $t_{\mathrm{n}, j}$ ), according to SP1. Constraints (7)-(8) guarantee safety rule (i), and constraints (9)-(11) guarantee safety rule (ii) (i.e., either vehicle $i$ must cross the intersection at least $h_{\mathrm{T}}$ ahead of vehicle $j$, or vehicle $j$ must pass at least $h_{\mathrm{T}}$ ahead of vehicle $\left.i\right)$. Auxiliary binary variables $b_{i, j}$ and a sufficiently large constant $M$ are instrumental in modeling disjunction constraints (9)-(10). If vehicle $i$ reaches the intersection before vehicle $j, b_{i, j}=0$, otherwise $b_{i, j}=1$.

### 2.6 SP3 - Obtaining speed profiles for approaching vehicles

Consider vehicle $i$ currently located at a distance $d_{i}$ from the intersection with speed $v_{i}$. Infinite speed profiles may exist such that the vehicle arrives at the intersection with speed $v_{\text {max }}$ at the scheduled time $t_{\mathrm{e}, i}>0$, while constrained by $v_{\text {max }}, a_{\text {max }}$ and $a_{\text {min }}$.

Given the multiple solution nature of SP3, one can choose among a variety of strategies to find a suitable speed profile. Although it is possible to formulate a sophisticated motion planning problem (Katrakazas et al., 2015), possibly taking into account criteria such as fuel consumption
(Minett et al., 2011) or passenger comfort (Le Vine et al., 2015), we leave these developments for future research. For the purposes of this work any solution for SP3 is acceptable, and we propose a simple algorithm that can find a solution relatively fast.

We define SP3 as the problem of determining a feasible average speed $\bar{v}_{i}$ for vehicle $i$ to follow during the next interval $\left[t_{0}, t_{0}+T_{\mathrm{s}}\right)$. For simplicity we assume that vehicles travel with constant speed during each such (small) interval, but this does not need to be the case as long as their average speed during the interval remains $\bar{v}_{i}$. At each $T_{\mathrm{s}}$ the IC informs each vehicle at the CR the average speed $\bar{v}_{i}$ they must follow during the next step.

Consider that for vehicle $i$ at least one Feasible Speed Profile (FSP) exists such that the vehicle can arrive at the intersection at the scheduled time. Among the possible FSP, there may be some profiles for which the vehicle never decelerates. We call these the Non Decelerating Feasible Speed Profiles (NDFSP). Within the existing NDFSP,the profiles in which the vehicle remains with constant speed $\bar{v}_{i}$ during the next step (i.e., $\bar{v}_{i}=v_{i}$ ) are called NDFSP'.

Let $v^{\prime}$ be the maximum average speed at which a vehicle can travel during the next step such that at least one NDFSP exists for this vehicle; and let $v^{\prime \prime}$ be the maximum average speed at which a vehicle can travel during the next step such that at least one FSP exists for the vehicle. Algorithm 1 illustrates a procedure that finds an average speed $v_{\text {avg }}$ for the next step such that the vehicle still has a FSP after that step, provided it had at least one FSP at the current step (which is guaranteed by SP2).

The behavior obtained by Algorithm 1 is such that in the course of several time steps the vehicle will first decelerate as much as needed (possibly not decelerating at all) until there is one NDFSP; then the vehicle will travel at constant speed for as long as doing so guarantees existence of an FSP; next it will accelerate to $v_{\text {max }}$; and, finally, it will travel at constant speed $v_{\text {max }}$ until reaching the intersection. This behavior maximizes the time vehicles spend traveling at constant speed, but implies maximum acceleration or deceleration when speed adjustments are performed.

Determining the existence of any of these types of trajectories, and the values of $v^{\prime}$ and $v^{\prime \prime}$ are problems that can be solved analytically with linear motion equations.

## 3. SIMULATION SETUP

### 3.1 Simulation and OATS parameters

The proposed control strategy was implemented in the Aimsun microscopic traffic simulator via the Aimsun API (TSS, 2013b). Subproblem SP2 was modeled using PuLP (Mitchell et al., 2011), a mixed integer linear programming framework in the Python programming language (Python Software Foundation, 2015), and solved using CBC (Forrest and Lougee-Heimer, 2005), an open-source mixed integer programming solver.

The intersection layout is as depicted in Fig. 1 and discussed in Section 2.1. Both approaches are 200 m long and 3 m wide. Both roads extend 100 m downstream the

intersection. Control Distance is set to $C_{\mathrm{d}, k}=150 \mathrm{~m}$ for $k \in\{\mathrm{e}, \mathrm{n}\}$. This distance is more than sufficiently long for any vehicle entering the CR to be able to reach $v_{\text {max }}$ at the intersection. Considering a CD much longer than needed would increase the problem complexity by increasing the number of vehicles inside the CR.

The control time step is selected as $T_{\mathrm{c}}=0.2 \mathrm{~s}$. The vehicle related parameters of the control strategy are defined as $a_{\text {max }}=3 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}, a_{\text {min }}=-4 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$ and $v_{\text {max }}=50 \mathrm{~km} / \mathrm{h}$ for all vehicles. Note that these data could be informed to the IC via V2I systems. Extending the formulation to account for vehicle dependent parameters is straightforward.

We set the minimum transversal headway as $h_{\mathrm{T}}=0.6 \mathrm{~s}$ and the longitudinal headway as $h_{\mathrm{L}}=1.2 \mathrm{~s}$. We chose arbitrarily low values to obtain high capacity, but still large enough to avoid collisions. The first value is slightly more than the time an object moving at 50 km/h takes to travel a distance of 7 m (the time taken is actually 0.504 s). Since the intersection width and a vehicle have a combined length of 7 m and vehicles travel at 50 km/h, this is sufficient to avoid longitudinal collisions in the modeled intersection with a narrow safety margin. The value of $h_{\mathrm{L}}=1.2$ provides a much larger safety margin, but using a lower $h_{\mathrm{L}}$ makes little difference because of the road capacity and the vehicle entrance process in the simulated network (since vehicles do not enter the network with headways lower than 1.2 s and the OATS does not lower vehicle speed appreciably).

Vehicle parameters in Aimsun for maximum acceleration, normal deceleration and maximum desired speed are the same as the ones set for the control strategy, $a_{\text {max }}, a_{\text {min }}$ and $v_{\text {max }}$, respectively. Vehicles have length $l=4 \mathrm{~m}$ and width $w=2 \mathrm{~m}$. The simulation time step equals $T_{\mathrm{c}}$. Driver reaction time is set to $T_{\mathrm{r}}=0.8 \mathrm{~s}$, and reaction time while stopped is 1.8 s. All simulation parameters not mentioned in this work are left at the default values provided by Aimsun v7.

Road saturation flow was observed to be 1783 veh/h when vehicles are not being directly controlled by the IC (i.e., they are behaving according to the Aimsun microscopic simulation model, which is based on the Gipps (1981) car following model). Vehicle entrance intervals in the network are sampled from an exponential distribution.

The IC implementation uses Aimsun API to obtain vehicle information (position, and speed) and alter vehicle speed directly (i.e., perfect information and communication).

### 3.2 Scenarios and Performance Indexes

We compare the OATS with Fixed Time (FT) traffic control. The FT plan has a cycle time of $C=90 \mathrm{~s}$ and each
approach is served by a 40 s long stage. Yellow time and clearance red are set to 3 and 2 s, respectively. Considering effective green time to be $g=40 \mathrm{~s}$ and saturation flow as $q_{\text {sat }}=1783 \mathrm{veh} / \mathrm{h}$, the capacity (as given by Webster (1958)) for each approach should be approximately 792 veh/h.

Given the specified parameters, the theoretical capacity for the OATS should be at best 3000 veh/h for each approach. This is the capacity resulting from an uninterrupted vehicle flow with headway corresponding to $h_{\mathrm{L}}=1.2 \mathrm{~s}$. Since $h_{\mathrm{L}}$ is twice as large as $h_{\mathrm{T}}$, it is always possible for a vehicle coming from one approach to pass between two vehicles from the other approach without interrupting flow, but depending of the moment vehicles enter the CR (which is uncertain) they may have to decelerate upstream of the intersection. Measuring the OATS capacity in simulation proved difficult because both vehicle behavior when outside the CR (i.e., obeying the simulator car-following model) and the entrance of vehicles in the simulation were more restrictive than the OATS.

We define four test scenarios to compare the performance of the OATS and FT when traffic demand is lower or higher than the intersection capacity under FT control. In Scenarios 1 and 3 the intersection is managed by the FT controller, while in Scenarios 2 and 4 the OATS is employed. Traffic demand is set to 700 veh/h for each approach in Scenarios 1 and 2 (low demand scenarios), and 1400 veh/h in scenarios 3 and 4 (high demand scenarios). Simulation duration is 30 min .

We evaluate each scenario in terms of vehicle delay, virtual queue length and vehicle speed.

Vehicle delay (measured in s/veh) is the difference between the time a vehicle takes to reach the exit of the network and the minimum time it could possibly take, $t_{\text {ideal }}$, which is the time the vehicle would spend if it was the only vehicle in the network and it could always pass intersections without impediment. For the simulated network, $t_{\text {ideal }}=$ 21.816 s.

Vehicle delay takes into account the time a vehicle spends in the entrance virtual queue if its entrance time (sampled from an exponential distribution) is too close for the previous vehicle to enter safely, or there is congestion impeding the entrance. The average length of this queue is also a relevant performance indicator, as it shows if congestion spills back from the modeled network.

The very large virtual queue in Scenario 3 (FT, high demand) indicates that there was a congestion spilling back from the network, which led to an unbounded delay and low average speeds, less than one fifth of $v_{\text {max }}$. In Scenario 1 there is no such spillback (i.e., traffic is not saturated at the intersection), but even so delay is significantly larger than with the OATS and average vehicle speed is less than half the value of $v_{\text {max }}$.

Overall, simulation results show that vehicle delay can be greatly decreased with the OATS. The strategy allowed vehicles to travel nearly unimpeded in the proposed scenarios and greatly increased intersection capacity. Besides efficiency gains, the OATS in itself guarantees safety by directly controlling the vehicles and enforcing headway constraints. It should be noted that the high capacity of the OATS is related to the problem constraints and, for the choice of parameters such as $a_{\text {max }}$, there is a trade-off between intersection capacity and passenger comfort that should be further investigated (Le Vine et al., 2015).

## 5. CONCLUSION

In this work we developed an intersection control strategy for highly automated vehicles in which the problem was decomposed in three subproblems: the problem of analytically finding the interval of feasible arrival times for ve-
hicles to reach the intersection; a MILP-based scheduling problem; and a motion planning problem.

Although the network considered is simple and many assumptions were made, the overall strategy proved successful for this particular application. Simulation results show that the developed strategy performed significantly better than a typical FT control plan, enabling vehicles to travel with average speeds very close to ideal values even with relatively high demands.

Several aspects should be addressed in future research, such as: the formulation should be extended to account for more generic intersection layouts, considering the possibility of lane changing at the approaches in SP1 and SP3; more sophisticated motion planning strategies should be studied in SP3 and inside the intersection; formal proofs for properties of the modeled problems should be obtained; the possibility of congestion downstream, as well as uncertainties in sensors, communication and actuation should be taken into account. Future research could also focus on designing a sufficiently robust intersection management strategy to enable the development of higher level strategies for the coordination of multiple intersections in a network.
