# A model and genetic algorithm for area-wide intersection signal optimization under user equilibrium traffic 

Jianhua Guo ${ }^{\mathrm{a}, *}$, Ye Kong ${ }^{\mathrm{a}}$, Zongzhi Li ${ }^{\mathrm{b}}$, Wei Huang ${ }^{\mathrm{a}}$, Jinde Cao ${ }^{\mathrm{c}}$, Yun Wei ${ }^{\mathrm{d}}$,<br>${ }^{\mathrm{a}}$ Intelligent Transportation System Research Center, Southeast University, Nanjing, China<br>${ }^{\mathrm{b}}$ Illinois Institute of Technology, Illinois, USA<br>${ }^{\mathrm{c}}$ School of Mathematics, Southeast University, Nanjing, China<br>${ }^{\mathrm{d}}$ National Engineering Laboratory for Green \& Safe Construction Technology in Urban Rail Transit, Beijing, China.<br>* Corresponding author: seugjh@163.com


#### Abstract

The use of signal control systems to reduce traffic movement conflicts at intersections often affects operational efficiency of an urban street network. This study introduces a new method for area-wide traffic signal timing optimization under user equilibrium traffic. The optimization model is formulated as a multi-dimensional search problem aimed to achieve minimized product of the total travel time associated with urban street network and the variance of travel time for unit distance of travel. A genetic algorithm is developed to derive the model solution. A simulation control protocol embedded in PARAMICS software tool capable of conducting area-wide microsimulation is adopted to design the logic frame and function module of the area-wide traffic signal control system. Computational experiment is performed for model application on the Central Business District of Nanjing, China for validation. The results show that mobility improvements are achieved after applying the proposed model along with the genetic algorithm for area-wide signal timing optimization, assessed by extended capacity ratio, and reductions in through and turning movement delays, as well as average and variance of travel time for unit distance of travel.


Keywords: Signal control; User equilibrium; Traffic simulation; Optimization

## 1 Introduction

The economic prosperity and continuing urbanization have led to rapid increase in travel demand in urban areas. Meanwhile, the capacity expansion of urban street network has not been kept abreast of the demand growth. The demand and supply mismatching has triggered deterioration in urban traffic mobility. Owing largely to land scarcity and prohibited cost of expanding urban streets, solutions have been thought to improve the operational efficiency of the existing street network. One of the effective means is to optimize traffic signal timing plans to reduce vehicle delays at intersections and, in turn, to improve traffic mobility. However, the current signal control systems are often focused on isolated intersections or parallel corridors, which may not improve the mobility of the overall street network. Hence, some road users will experience lower levels of mobility (Stathopoulos and Karlaftis, 2002; Long et al., 2008). According to Wardrop's user equilibrium (UE) principle, the network will be in balance when the road users are aware of exact conditions of the network traffic and are able to actively select the paths with shortest travel times along the respective origin-destination (O-D) paths. When the influence of congestion on travel times of the
network is considered, the network will likely be in balance and the travel times of the O-D pairs can be kept equal and minimal. At the same time, travel times of unused paths will be greater than or equal to the minimum travel times (Wardrop, 1952).

Conversely, when the O-D travel times of an urban street network are not in balance, some road users will adjust travel routes to reduce travel times until the balance is reached. It also shows that travel times spent by road users can represent interests of road users preferring to experience shorter travel times. In this respect, interests of road users will be higher when intersection signal timing plans are adjusted to minimize vehicle delays and hence the travel times. Based on a study conducted by Dailey (1993), the variance of travel times for unit distance of travel was found to be an effective measure to reflect the user equilibrium state of traffic on the urban street network. Also, the total travel time of an urban street network was used as an area-wide measure to assess the mobility performance (Nam and Drew, 1996; Nam and Drew, 1999; Petty et al., 1998). As a result, the product of the total travel time associated with an urban street network and the variance of the travel time for unit distance of travel can be selected as the objective for intersection signal optimization. Particular to the current study, intersection signal optimization is achieved by minimizing the above term as the objective function according to user equilibrium based traffic. With vehicle delays reduced from the optimal signal timing plans, it may mitigate traffic congestion as well (Smith, 1979; Dafermos, 1980).
The remainder of this paper is arranged as follows: Section 2 discusses existing methods for signal optimization according to urban street network equilibrium. Section 3 present the proposed method for intersection signal timing optimization using user equilibrium based traffic. Section 4 elaborates the solution algorithm. Section 5 describes the model application in a computational experiment. Finally, Section 6 provides study summary and draws conclusion.

## 2 Literature Review

Signalized traffic control has significant effect on reducing vehicle delays at intersections, balancing traffic flow, and improving operational efficiency of an urban street network (Dafermos, 1980). The intersection signal control systems can be largely classified into fixed-time or real-time adaptive signal control systems, such as SCAT and SCOOT (Chiou, 2003). The modeling of the intersection signal control system is usually based on the changes of traffic volumes (Stevanovic et al., 2011). Through the system, traffic condition of an urban street network can be optimized by adjusting the regional signal timing plans (Zhang et al., 2010). Practically, the temporal and spatial distributions of traffic flow on an urban street network in time and space are found to be consistent with the distributions of travel times associated with the urban street network that are closely affected by traffic signal timing plans (Aashtiani and Magnanti, 1981). For instance, Allsop and Turner (1986) studied the interrelation between traveler's route choice behavior and traffic signal control when studying area-wide traffic control. With insights gained from the analysis, the stability of signal timing plans are able to be increased. Subsequently, equilibrium network traffic signal timing was investigated.

Some studies focused on optimizing the integrated operation performance measure of the traffic signal system based on travel path and distribution of traffic flow and then develop
optimal control plans for intersections in an urban street network. The modeling process was largely formulated as the design model with continuous flows. Marcotte (1983) and Powell and Sheffi (1983) introduced heuristic algorithms to derive solutions for models targeting small sized urban street networks. Magnanti and Wong (1984) introduced a network-wide intersection signal optimization model by adjusting parameters including cycle lengths, green splits, offsets, and phase sequences. Fisk and Schneider (1984) compared optimization models addressing issues between superior and subordinate about management and feedback. Specifically, the signal control system dealing with area-wide traffic signals and responding to traffic volume changes is considered as superior. Travelers choosing the optimal routes for their own purposes according to Wardrop's first principle are regarded as subordinates. Smith et al. (1979, 1981, 1985, 1990, 1993) conducted extensive research on traffic assignment and signal timing optimization. An urban street network equilibrium model was developed for real-time adaptive traffic control. The limitations of using minimum total travel time as the evaluation performance measure of the system operation were identified. Alternatively, the urban street network operation performance measure based on traffic capacity was proposed. With intersection vehicle control delays, queuing delays, and other factors considered, Yang and Yagar (1995) employed a multi-level programming model to solve the area-wide signal timing optimization and urban street network traffic assignment problem. The optimization models were formulated by identifying the best green splits for given cycle lengths and offsets. Further, Roshandeh et al. (2014) introduced an area-wide intersection signal timing optimization model based on shockwave theory to obtain the best green splits for individual intersections. Stevanovic et al. (2013) proposed to optimize signal timings for improving safety metrics or their surrogates. Zhang et al. (2013) proposed a bi-objective model to optimize cycle length, offsets, green splits, and phase sequences to minimize total system delay and mean excess exposure simultaneously. Ma et al. (2016) proposed a multi-stage stochastic programming method based on phase gap reliability with the objective function of minimizing the average intersection delay.

For iterative signal optimization methods, the essence is how to determine the relationship between the intersection signal timing method and the cost-traffic. Charlesworth and Charlesworth (1975) developed an iterative algorithm based on TRANSYT8 signal timing technology to determine the cost-traffic relationship curve through traffic simulation and estimate vehicle delays in different traffic conditions. Cascetta and Cantarella (1991) proposed an iterative optimization model that can be applied to large urban street networks based on traditional signal timing methods such as the Webster method. A computational experiment showed that this method did not effectively improve the operation efficiency of urban street network.
The review of existing methods and models for area-wide intersection signal optimization are all based on the assumption that cycle lengths and offsets are as given, and the focus has been on fine-tuning the green splits to develop the optimal signal timing plans. The current study simultaneously adjusts the three parameters including cycle lengths, green splits, and offsets of the signal timing plans of urban street network with user equilibrium traffic. Therefore, the proposed model along with the solution is likely to improve traffic mobility for travelers using urban street network.

## 3 Proposed Model

The target of the proposed model is to minimize the product of the sum of the travel time on regional urban street network and the variance of the travel time of unit mileage. The model will identify the best combination of three parameters including cycle length, green time, and offset of the regional urban street network. Therefore, constraints of the three parameters are all considered. In this section, the model formulation is described in detail.

### 3.1 Objective function

The priorities of road users and the operation efficiency of the whole network need to be considered holistically. In model formulation, the variance of travel time of unit mileage for each link is used to measure the user equilibrium status of the urban street network traffic. At the same time, the sum of the travel time for each base station pair of regional urban street network is required to be minimal. The user equilibrium principle for traffic assignments is applied to the area-wide control, which is in line with the demand for actual traffic control.

The objective function of the area-wide control model based on user equilibrium traffic is defined as:

$$
y=\left(\sum X_{i}\right) * \frac{1}{n} \sum\left(\frac{1}{S_{i}}\left(X_{i}-\bar{x}\right)\right)^{2}
$$

where $y$ is the objective function of the area-wide control model based on user equilibrium; $x_{i}$ is the mean of corresponding travel times of the $i^{\text {th }}$ base station pair during the selected period; $\bar{x}$ is the mean of the travel time of each base station in the urban street network during the selected period; $s_{i}$ is the distance of the $i^{\text {th }}$ base station pair in the selected network; $\sum x_{i}$ is the sum of the average travel time for each base station pair in the selected period; $\frac{1}{n} \sum\left(\frac{1}{s_{i}}\left(x_{i}-\bar{x}\right)\right)^{2}$ is the travel time variance of unit mileage for the base station pairs in the selected urban street network during the selected period; $n$ is the number of base stations.

The target of the function is to minimize the product of the sum of the average travel time for base station pairs in the selected period and the variance of the unit mileage for base station pairs in the selected urban street network during the selected period. In order to optimize the regional urban street network traffic in the direction of user equilibrium, the travel time variance of unit mileage should be minimal. Moreover, in order to avoid the situation that there is no optimization solution to the timing scheme with a single control target, the product should be minimal.

### 3.2 Constraints

As part of model formulation, constraints of the offset between two adjacent intersections, effective green time, and cycle length are imposed as below.

First, the offset between two adjacent intersections is non-negative, i.e.,

$$
\phi_{i, i+1} \geq 0
$$

where $i$ is the intersection number, and $\phi_{i, i+1}$ is the offset between two adjacent intersections.
Second, the length of effective green time is non-negative, i.e.,

$$
\gamma_{i, k} \geq 0
$$

where $k$ is the phase number, and $\gamma_{i, k}$ is the effective green time (s) of the $k^{\text {th }}$ phase of the $i^{\text {th }}$
intersection.
Third, green time of the lane group should be greater than the minimum green time and less than the maximum green time, i.e.,

$$
\gamma_{i(\min )} \leq \gamma_{i, k} \leq \gamma_{i(\max )}
$$

where $\gamma_{i(m i n)}$ is the minimum effective green time controlled by the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal; $\gamma_{i(\text { max })}$ is the maximum effective green time controlled by the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal.

Finally, cycle length of an intersection is the sum of total effective green time of all phases and total loss time and cycle length is between a maximum cycle length and a minimum cycle length, i.e.,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \sum_{k=1}^{k} \gamma_{i, k}+L_{i}=C_{i(\max )} \\
& C_{i(\min )} \leq C_{i} \leq C_{i(\max )}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $L$ is the total loss time of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal control; $C_{i}$ is the cycle length of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal control; $C_{i(\text { min })}$ is the minimum cycle length of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal control; $C_{i(\max )}$ is the maximum cycle length of the $i^{\mathrm{th}}$ intersection signal control.

### 3.3 Optimization formulation

The performance measure of the proposed model is multi-objective. Based on the objective function, constraint conditions, and control strategy, an area-wide control optimization model based on user equilibrium principle is established as below.

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \left.Z=\min (y)=\min \left[\left(\sum x_{i}\right) * \frac{1}{n} \sum \frac{1}{s_{i}}\left(X_{i}-\bar{x}\right)\right)^{2}\right] \\
& \left\{\begin{array}{c}
\gamma_{i(\min )} \leq \gamma_{i, k} \leq \gamma_{i(\max )} \\
C_{i(\min )} \leq C_{i} \leq C_{i(\max )} \\
\sum_{k=1}^{k} \gamma_{i, k}+L_{i}=C_{i} \\
\gamma_{i, k} \geq 0 \\
\phi_{i, i+1} \geq 0
\end{array}\right.
\end{aligned}
$$

In equation (7), $Z$ is the objective function of the area-wide control model based on user equilibrium principle; $x_{i}$ is the mean of corresponding travel times of the $i^{\text {th }}$ base station pair during the selected period; $x$ is the mean of the travel time of each base station in the urban street network during the selected period; $s_{i}$ is the distance of the $i^{\text {th }}$ base station pair in the selected network; $\sum x_{i}$ is the sum of the average travel time of each base station pair in the selected period; $\frac{1}{n} \sum\left(\frac{1}{s_{i}}\left(x_{i}-\bar{x}\right)\right)^{2}$ is the travel time variance of unit mileage during the selected period; $n$ is the number of base stations.

In constraints, $i$ is the intersection number; $\phi_{i, i+1}$ is the offset between two adjacent intersections; $k$ is the number of phases; $\gamma_{i, k}$ is the effective green time (s) of the $k^{\text {th }}$ phase of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection; $\gamma_{\text {(min) }}$ is the minimum effective green time of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal; $\gamma_{i(\text { max })}$ is the maximum effective green time of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal; $L$ is the total loss time of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal; $C_{i}$ is the cycle length of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal; $C_{i(\text { min })}$ is the
minimum cycle length of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal; $C_{i(\text { max })}$ is the maximum cycle length of the $i^{\text {th }}$ intersection signal.

## 4 Genetic Algorithm (GA) Solution

### 4.1 Genetic algorithm Design

Genetic algorithm is one kind of heuristic algorithms that utilizes randomized search based on the genetic mechanism of survival of the fittest in biological evolution field, and is first introduced in (Holland, 1975). It operates directly on the structured problem, and imposes no restrictions on the derivatives and continuity of the objective function. It has the inherent hidden parallelism and global searching ability. The search space can be automatically guided through the searching process. The search direction can also be adaptively adjusted. Genetic algorithm searches the most feasible solution by simulating the survival and reproduction processes of natural organisms. Through evolution, the individuals of higher fitness will be retained. The most feasible individual will be converted back to the most feasible solution (Tsukahara and Kanasugi, 2009). In genetic algorithm, chromosome coding, fitness function, genetic operators, and GA parameters are needed.
(1) Chromosome coding

For applying genetic algorithm, certain code is needed to represent the solution to the problem. Choosing a reasonable coding mechanism has great impact on the quality and efficiency of the algorithm. In general, the problem can be coded using binary systems. However, additional parameters are utilized in the proposed model and the binary coding and decoding operations required by the evolutionary process will make the solution time longer. Therefore, real number coding is used in this paper to speed up the computing speed and improve the solution accuracy.

According to the modeling process, the signal timing parameters need to be optimized. The information of chromo-gene should contain the offset of the intersection and the green time of each phase. Decimal encoding is adopted according to the positive integer real number characteristics of signal timing parameters. Considering a road network of 10 intersections, there are 65 genes in the chromosome, corresponding to the timing parameters of the phases of the 10 signals. Phase 0 corresponds to the offset of the signal intersection. Phase 1-7 corresponds to the green time of each phase, respectively. The specific chromosome coding is shown in Figure 1. Note that the number of phases at different intersections is not necessarily the same with a maximum number of phases set as 7 in this paper.

(2) Fitness function

The merits and demerits of the individuals can be evaluated through the fitness function. It directly affects the speed of the calculation and the reliability of the results. In the process of parameter optimization, the fitness function is used to screen the signal timing plans. The reciprocal of the objective function is taken as the fitness function. The calculation formula of
the fitness function (Fitness ${ }_{1}$ ) is given as Equation (8). The timing scheme will be better if the value is greater.

$$
\text { Fitness }_{1}=\frac{1}{\left(\sum x_{i}\right) *\left|\operatorname{Var}\left(x_{i} * \frac{1}{s_{i}}\right)\right|}
$$

where $x_{i}$ is the average travel time of $i^{\text {th }}$ base station pair, and $s_{i}$ is the spacing of the $i^{\text {th }}$ base station pair.
(3) Genetic operator

The design of genetic operators mainly includes the design of selection operator, crossover operator, and the mutation operator. For the selection operator, the probability of each individual being selected is calculated by sorting selection operators, and the probability of each individual being selected is assigned according to the individual fitness ranking. For the crossover operator, individuals which cannot meet the constraints are improved according to the crossover operators in the GA toolbox of MATLAB software. For the mutation operator, individuals which do not meet the constraints are improved according to the mutation operators in the GA toolbox of MATLAB software.
(4) GA parameters

Parameters of the genetic algorithm include number of evolution generations, population size (N), crossover probability (Pc), mutation probability (Pm), and the stopping criterion, i.e., the maximum number of evolution generations. Usually the maximum number of evolution generations is greater than 20. The population size is in the range of 30 to 160. The crossover probability is in the range of 0.6 to 0.95 . The mutation probability is in the range of 0.001 to 0.01. In this paper, these parameters are set according to the chromosome coding length and the algorithm efficiency, with the maximum number of evolution generations as 100, the population size as 60 , the crossover probability as 0.5 , and the mutation probability as 0.01 .

## 5 Computational Experiment

### 5.1 Study network and data collection

In order to validate the proposed model and solution algorithm for area-wide intersection signal timing optimization, a computational experiment is conducted. In particular, the proposed model along with the iterative computation process of the solution algorithm is coded in the PARAMICS microsimulation tool where the street network of central business district (CBD) of Nanjing, China is utilized for the experiment. The area-wide intersection signal control system in PARAMICS microsimulation environment mainly includes PARAMICS simulation module and genetic algorithm execution module which facilitates travel demand, network supply data entry and processing, simulation execution, optimal signal timing designs, and result outputs. The urban street network is created using geometric design data of the street segments and intersections and original signal timing plans of the constituent intersections. At the same time, vehicle detectors based on radio frequency identification (RFID) technique are installed on individual street segments and intersection approaches to collect actual traffic flow data that can be used for validation of the model
outputs. Note that the vehicle detectors installed at each base station can register passing records for vehicles equipped with the RFID tag.

### 5.2 Simulation model calibration

Prior to initiate intersection signal timing optimization, the O-D trip parameters of the simulation model for the study area need to be calibrated. The essence is to adjust travel demand and vehicle routing and dynamics parameters such as O-D estimation, car-following, lane change, acceleration and deceleration rates to ensure that the model outputs properly reflect the real world traffic measurements. Note that GA is used to calibrate the simulation.
(1) GA design

Decimal code is chosen because the O-D value is positive real number. Given the size of the selected network, there are 144 genes and the coding length is 144, i.e., the data in the O-D table of the selected urban street network can be arranged into 144 rows. Figure 4 shows the specific chromosome coding.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/6915eeb9-f40e-4b3a-89a3-287b3fe33199-11.jpg?height=176&width=1333&top_left_y=2430&top_left_x=390)
Figure 4 Illustration of chromosome coding of O-D calibration

The fitness function is determined to evaluate the parameter calibration. In order to ensure that the simulated traffic flow are consistent with the real situation, the difference between the simulated traffic flow and the real traffic flow is taken as the fitness function. The calculation of the fitness function (Fitness ${ }_{2}$ ) is given as Equation (9). Note that larger fitness value indicates better calibration performance.

$$
\text { Fitness }_{2}=\frac{10}{\max \left|o b s_{i}-\operatorname{sim}_{i}\right|+1}
$$

where $o b s_{i}$ is the measured value of traffic flow of section $i$ and $\operatorname{sim}{ }_{i}$ is the simulated traffic flow value of section $i$.

For GA parameters, the maximum number of evolution generations is 50 , the population size is 30 , the crossover probability is 0.5 , and the mutation probability is 0.05 . In addition, three genetic operators are designed. For the selection operator, the probability of each individual being selected is calculated by Roulette selection operators. For the crossover operator and the mutation operator, individuals which do not meet the constraints are improved using the GA toolbox of MATLAB software.

## 6 Summary and Conclusion

This study introduces an area-wide control model for intersection signal timing optimization using user equilibrium based traffic. The objective is established as the product of two elements. One is the sum of the travel time for each base station pair of regional urban street network, and the other is the variance of travel time of unit mileage for each base station pair. Moreover, the method of solving the model is based on a genetic algorithm, which provides a reliable theoretical basis for the realization of the simulation for area-wide intersection signal timing optimization. Meanwhile, the logical framework and function module of simulation control system are designed to apply the proposed model. The performance measures adopted for evaluating the proposed model are multi-targeted, aiming to optimize three parameters of intersection signal timing plans, i.e., cycle length, green splits, and offset for intersections within an urban street network. The user equilibrium based traffic is used as input for model execution, overcoming the limitation that signal control objectives are often targeted at isolated intersections or arteries. The computational results using an urban road network in Nanjing, China show that optimal signal timing design is feasible. The proposed model has shed light on area-wide intersection signal timing optimization based on user equilibrium traffic.
The following remarks are given. First, the model of the area-wide control of the urban traffic is established by the product of two elements as defined previously. However, the smallest product cannot mean the two elements are both smallest. In other words, the shape of the objective function might not be convex, and in this end, the global searching ability offered by GA will be helpful in finding a best feasible solution. Of course, other optimization techniques such as multi-objective optimization model might offer more power in finding the best solution. Second, the simulation environment established in the computational experiment reveals that O-D trip generation capability is important for simulation package, and more accurate O-D backstopping method can be used to obtain the O-D parameters of the urban street network. Third, it has to be pointed out that the proposed method relies greatly on the microsimulation software PARAMICS, and hence the underlying assumptions of PARAMICS will affect the optimization results.Finally, cross comparisons of performances are conducted for the urban street network with the original intersection signal timing plans and the optimal timing plans derived from the proposed mode, and future research direction can be conducted to expand the coverage of the proposed model and other notable models for intersection signal timing optimization in model performance evaluation.
