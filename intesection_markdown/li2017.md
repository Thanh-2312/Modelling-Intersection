#### Abstract

It is a common vision that connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) will increasingly appear on the road in the near future and share roads with traditional vehicles. Through sharing real-time locations and receiving guidance from infrastructure, a CAV's arrival and request for green light at intersections can be approximately predicted along their routes. When many CAVs from multiple approaches at intersections place such requests, a central challenge is how to develop an intersection automation policy (IAP) to capture complex traffic dynamics and schedule resources (green lights) to serve both CAV requests (interpreted as request for green lights on a particular signal phase at time $t$ ) and traditional vehicles. To represent heterogeneous vehicle movements and dynamic signal timing plans, we first formulate the IAP optimization as a special case of machine scheduling problem using a mixed integer linear programming formulation. Then we develop a novel phasetime-traffic (PTR) hypernetwork model to represent heterogeneous traffic propagation under traffic signal operations. Since the IAP optimization, by nature, is a special sequential decision process, we also develop sequential branch-and-bound search algorithms over time to IAP optimization considering both CAVs and traditional vehicles in the PTR hypernetwork. As the critical part of the branch-and-bound search, special dominance and bounding rules are also developed to reduce the search space and find the exact optimum efficiently. Multiple numerical experiments are conducted to examine the performance of the proposed IAP optimization approach.

## 1. Introduction

With the rapid development of wireless communication, sensing and computing techniques, it has become a common vision that connected and automated vehicles (CAV) will soon go beyond testbeds to appear on roads with traditional vehicles at large scale. In the meantime, it is also envisioned that we will live in an era with both CAVs and traditional vehicles for a long time before CAVs completely replace traditional vehicles. The traditional vehicles refer to the vehicles operated by human drivers. The human drivers plan their routes primarily based on their historical experiences and personal pref-

[^0]erences with limited real-time road information. Therefore they mostly follow the originally planned routes. The human drivers also have perception-reaction (P-R) times to respond the intersection control measures. By contrast, the CAVs have short or even zero P-R times, can continuously share their locations and receive guidance from infrastructure. As a result, the CAVs can notify the downstream intersections when to arrive, offering more time for those intersections to schedule appropriate control strategies. The CAVs can change its route dynamically to avoid delays and congestions.

CAVs will undoubtedly bring new potential of improving the automation level at intersections. However, the co-existence of CAVs and traditional vehicles in the real world makes it challenging to describe the dynamic interaction between heterogeneous vehicle movements and signal operations. It is necessary to determine an optimal policy to schedule resources (green lights) to serve CAV requests at the minimal cost like the total delays in this paper to achieve optimal intersection automation for both CAVs and traditional vehicles. We refer to such a policy as intersection automation policy or IAP.

### 1.1. Intersection automation policy with 100\% CAVs: a special resource scheduling problem

Within an ideal 100\% CAV traffic environment, the traffic system will become completely controllable and share many similarities with manufacturing systems. If we view CAVs as jobs, an intersection would in essence be a group of competing machines to process arriving jobs and each machine can be open to allow CAVs (jobs) on an approach to cross (processed) or be closed. In comparison, classic machine scheduling problems typically consider parallel or sequence machines but without the tight coupling between machine transitions. On non-competing machines (concurrent movements), jobs can be processed simultaneously whereas on competing machines (conflict movements), jobs on one machine (waiting CAVs at one stop line) must be suspended (stopped) when the other machine is open.

### 1.2. Intersection automation as a special case of sequential decision process

Within the heterogeneous traffic environment, CAVs are viewed as a player with dual roles at intersections: (a) a part of traffic flow which interacts with traditional vehicles and (b) identities sending green requests to intersection(s). The CAV's first role (as vehicles) is explicitly separated from the second (as green request senders). Furthermore, we view the urban traffic dynamics as a sequential decision process or SDP. Let $T$ denote the time horizon and traffic vehicle locations or states at time $t$ are the direct result of traffic signal operations from time 0 to time $t$ where $t \sim[0, T]$. On the other hand, traffic signal operations from $t$ to the future also needs to consider both current and future traffic states according to their inherent traffic vehicular dynamics. As such, the target problem modeling and solution algorithms developed in this paper aim to fully capture the nature of the sequential decision process through coupling heterogeneous traffic and traffic signal operations.

Extended from the notations due to Karp and Held (1967), traffic propagation over time can be represented by a quintuple $\mathbb{N}=\left(A, R, r_{0}, r_{T}, \Gamma\right)$, where $\mathbb{N}$ is the subject traffic system; $A$ is the set of time-dependent inputs such as traffic lights; $R$ is the set of all feasible traffic states including vehicles' locations, traffic signal status and the set of served green requests; $r_{0}$ represents the initial state; $r_{T}$ represents the final traffic state and $\Gamma$ is the state transition function. In addition, it is also necessary to define $\rho$, an input data set for SDP including the link travel times, saturation flow rates, etc. and let $P$ denote all possible input data sets $(\rho \in P)$ as well as two conceptual functions:

1. State transition function $H=h(R, A, P)$ : for a given data set $\rho \in P, a \in A$, the quantity of $h(r, a, \rho)$ gives the cost of transition from the initial state $r$ to an updated traffic state $r^{\prime}=\Gamma(r, a)$ due to the control measure $a$, where $\Gamma$ is the traffic state transition function. $H$ is critical in traffic signal modeling and optimization because it is necessary to evaluate the traffic state evolution as well as incurring costs under a feasible traffic operation during the optimization. As examples, in addition to signal phasing transition rules, traffic state transition function $h$ is most commonly defined based on cumulative vehicle counting curves in which traffic signal operations change the departure curve (the "D curve") and control delays (Daganzo, 1997). $H$ is also defined as changes to the arterial "green band" under traffic signal timings at intersections in a mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) form in the literature due to Little et al. (1981) and Gartner et al. (1991); defined as the changes to queue length and delay changes at intersections under a traffic signal operation by Mirchandani and Head (2001) and Tiaprasert et al. (2015); defined as traffic dynamics changes under given traffic signal operations in the cell transmission model (CTM) developed by Daganzo (1994) in the literature due to Lin and Wang (2004) and Lo (1999); as the propagation of vehicles stored on links by Aboudalas et al. (2009); as the propagation of traffic shockwaves by Wu and Liu (2011); as the total vehicle waiting time by Wang et al. (2017); as the queue length changes estimated from vehicle trajectories by Zheng and Liu (2017) and as changes to a vehicle routing plan in the literature due to Li et al. (2015).
2. Cost function $K=k(P, R)$, for a given data $\rho \in P$, the quantity of $k(\rho, r)$ gives the cost of traffic state $r \in R$.

Based on the concept of SDP, the intersection automation policy optimization can be defined as follows: construct an algorithm which schedules the traffic signal operations during the time horizon $T$, given the (prior known) data set $\rho$ so as to make $\mathbb{N}$ reach a feasible final state $q_{T}$ at the minimum cost. In particular, within the heterogeneous traffic environment, a traffic state $r$ contains two groups of objects: CAV requests for green and traffic dynamics comprised of all vehicles. A CAV request can be represented in a form like "Requesting green light on a particular signal phase at time t" while all vehicle locations on roads are governed by traffic dynamics defined in $R$ and $\Gamma$.

The rest of this paper is organized as: literature on intersection automation policies are first reviewed and summarized. Then a mixed-integer-linear-programming formulation for IAP optimization based on individual CAV maneuvers is provided within the 100\% CAV environment. Third, a new concept of phase-time-traffic (PTR) hypernetwork model is presented to represent a heterogeneous traffic environment including traffic signal operations, traffic dynamics on a road network, and CAV requests for green. In the following optimization algorithm development, a "phase-time prism" concept is introduced to reduce the search space without loss of global optimum, and the optimization algorithms are presented to search for the exact optimal IAP at intersections based on a sequential branch-and-bound search framework. The sequential solution algorithm is further enhanced with parallel computing technique. In the end, several numerical experiments are conducted to examine the efficiency of the proposed algorithms.

## 2. Literature review

Dresner and Stone (2008) propose a traffic-light-free autonomous intersection management (AIM) policy. In their AIM policy, intersections are divided into grids and viewed as a type of space-time resource. Whenever a CAV places a request to cross, the vehicle is in essence requesting the space-time resources along its planned trajectory. The AIM policy will first examine the availability of the required resources. If available, the agent will reserve the resources for this vehicle until it crosses. When multiple CAVs compete for the same space-time resources (i.e., cross the intersection at the same time), the AIM agent will grant the resource in a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) manner. The rejected CAVs are also allowed to replace the request later. Multiple variants of the AIM policy are developed later for mixed traffic environments with both CAVs and traditional vehicles (Au et al., 2014) and path-based AIM policy (Hausknecht et al., 2011). The AIM policy and its variants follows the FIFO manner for CAVs to cross intersections and ignore the mandates in current traffic signal systems. Similarly, Müller et al. (2016) design a centralized MILP formulation to control highly automated intersections. They decompose the target problem into three sub-problems: feasible arrival times for CAV vehicles, a MILP formulation constrained by the FIFO rule and minimum headways to schedule CAV arrival times, and motion planning for CAVs to arrive at intersections at the minimum cost. Li et al. (2014) enumerate all feasible traffic signal operations first and use each traffic operation as the external constraints imposed on the CAV trajectory optimization in their proposed joint optimization for CAV trajectories and traffic signal operations at an isolated intersection. Kamal et al. (2015) formulate the intersection automation as a nonlinear programming problem to minimize the total collision risk, subject to CAV kinematic capacities through adjusting approaching speeds. They also present solution algorithms based on model predictive control.

Zhu and Ukkusuri (2015) propose a linear programming formulation to optimize network-wide intersection operations and CAV paths in the context of dynamic traffic assignment and the CAV environment. The authors propose a new lanebased traffic flow model based on which a dynamic traffic network loading tool is developed to optimize and evaluate the intersection control policy and CAV path plans. The objective is to minimize the penalties for holding flows during the traffic propagation, and the constraints consider the flow and occupancy conservation laws in different lanes. Li et al. (2015) optimize vehicle trajectories and traffic signal operations simultaneously with the Lagrangian relaxation framework to manage intersections and vehicle route guidance within a network. The authors decompose the target problem into two sub-problems: a time-dependent shortest path finding problem and a proactive traffic signal operations problem. Their experiment results show that problem decomposition with Lagrangian relaxation still have challenges in reducing the solution optimality gaps because the traffic signal operations are essentially a sequential decision process, and it is difficult to estimate the traffic state over the whole time horizon without tightly superimposing traffic signal operations.

While the above literature considers optimization for both CAVs and intersection resource schedules, other relevant literature only focuses on CAV trajectory optimization and often treats the traffic signal operations as exogenous constraints. Zohdy and Rakha formulate the CAV trajectory optimization problem as a non-linear programming problem constrained by the FIFO rule and minimum safe headway to minimize the overall delays at intersections by adjusting CAV approaching speeds. In their proposed system architecture, Zohdy and Rakha (2016) requires all CAVs first to accelerate to their maximal speeds and then their trajectories will be optimized to minimize the total delays, defined as non-delay crossing times minus actual crossing times. Zhou et al. (2017) and Ma et al. (2017) propose CAV trajectory optimization using the heuristic shooting algorithm, a widely applied algorithm in motion planning. The authors formulate the CAV trajectory optimization problems as a nonlinear optimization problem subject to macroscopic traffic shockwave propagation constraints and individual vehicles' kinematic characters.

From the perspective of optimization, the optimization methodologies in the aforementioned literature can be divided into two types: (a) formulate the underlying problem into a mathematical programming problem to optimize certain collective goals (e.g., the total delays); (b) develop rule-based decision-making policy for individual identities. The first methodology can help us to understand the underlying mathematical principles of intersection automation policies and serve as the theoretical foundation of further development. However, suitable solution algorithms to the mathematical programming formulations often need careful selection and serious customization in practice to capture the domain-specific characteristics or satisfy the real-time decision making challenges. On the other hand, rule-based intersection automation policies are typically effective and efficient for the specific sequential decision making system of interest, but it is difficult to ensure that the derived policies have reached globally optimal solutions since most rules are heuristically designed and highly dependent on the configuration of specific problems. In essence, the intersection automation policies proposed in the literature is to design different approaches to scheduling space-time resources within intersections to achieve certain goals. It is observed that most related literature assumes simplified traffic pattern or traffic control operations. As an example, a popular methodology is to take vehicles as homogeneous agents and adopt the simple FIFO rule to schedule the available space-time resources for them to cross. An intersection automation policy based on the FIFO rule may possibly deteriorating traffic progression by allowing one individual vehicle to slow down and split a main-line platoon in reality. On the other hand, most literature considers approaching vehicles' arriving times at intersections time points rather than time windows in optimizing the control policies. Such a methodology lacks robustness within the heterogeneous traffic environment. In addition, most literature either adopt simplified traffic control strategies or discard the existing traffic control mechanism and we believe that it is still necessary to consider most existing traffic control constraints with high fidelity in developing intersection automation policies within the heterogeneous traffic environment.

This paper aims at addressing the above issues and its contributions include: (a) we propose the phase-time-traffic (PTR) hypernetwork model with a hierarchical structure to address the target problem. The top level has linear attributes of network models while the secondary level, the traffic propagation dimension, is nonlinear. The heterogeneous traffic propagation component is not part of the optimization of the master problem (scheduling green lights for CAV within heterogeneous traffic environment) but it takes most of the computing time (to calculate the PT arc costs). Within the proposed optimization framework and search algorithms, it is possible to increase the optimizing efficiency simply by increasing the computing power for traffic loading. By contrast, the existing literature mostly present a "flat" structure in the formulation in which the variables of controls and vehicles maneuvers are considered together, making development efficient solution algorithms challenging. In the meantime, the proposed solution algorithms can tightly couple high-fidelity traffic propagation, traffic signal operations and CAV request scheduling for the first time. A CAV request is defined as "requesting green light on a particular signal phase within a time window". In the PTR hypernetwork, this problem can be modelled as a scheduling problem which aims at serving all CAV requests as many as possible and at the minimal total delays. (b) Based on the PTR hypernetwork, a scheduling-oriented sequential branch-and-bound algorithm is developed to sequence green lights over time. The pruning and bounding operations can be determined by all vehicles' possible locations and the service status of CAV requests. The crux of the dominance rule is that partial schedules can be compared if they refer to the same
phase-time node. Compared with the standard optimization solvers, the proposed BB algorithms is enhanced with parallel computing techniques and search phase reduction, referred "phase-time prism"; (c) we present a scheduling-oriented timeindexed mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) formulation and an arc-time-indexed mixed-integer programming (MIP) formulation for the optimization with 100\% CAVs and with heterogeneous traffic. In these two formulations, interactions among vehicles and among CAV requests are explicitly reflected.

## 3. A mixed integer linear programming (MILP) formulation for sequential scheduling process within 100\% CAV environment

### 3.1. Proposed architecture of automated intersections

In an ideal environment of $100 \%$ CAVs, CAVs are fully controllable at intersections, and intersection automation policy can be viewed as a multiple competing machine scheduling problem with the goal to process all jobs as soon as possible (minimize the total delay). Each intersection has two control boundaries, the entrance (outer) and exit (inner) as illustrated in Fig. 1. The entrance boundary can range up to a few blocks away from the subject intersection while the exit boundary can be right after it passes the intersection. Once a CAV crosses an intersection's outer boundary, this vehicle will be exclusively controlled by that intersection until the subject CAV crosses the intersection's exit boundary.

A CAV may be within the scope of two intersections along its route at the same time when the vehicle crosses the entrance boundary of a new downstream intersection before it completely exits the currently controlling intersection. In that case, if the new intersection is closer to the vehicle's current location than the controlling intersection, then the new intersection can override the control right of that vehicle. This policy reflects the priority of scheduling urgency at the close downstream intersection.

Whenever a CAV crosses the entrance boundary of an intersection, it is considered released into and overseen by this intersection. Any path along which a vehicle enters an intersection's boundary, goes through this intersection and then exits the intersection is referred to as a vehicle "movement". Without the loss of generality, each movement is further divided into three road segments: a vehicle uses the 1st segment to move from entrance to the stop line on which vehicles may be suggested to slow down or re-route to avoid this intersection if the intersection control policy decides that other competing vehicles need to cross the intersection first; once a vehicle leaves the stop line, it enters the 2nd segment on which vehicles strictly move until they reach the end of the 2nd segment; the 3rd segment is the exit segment in which vehicles strictly move until they reach the boundary of the next intersection. Each movement at an intersection is viewed as a separate group comprised of three sequential machines.

The IAP policy at each intersections must be optimized continuously in a "rolling-horizon" manner because CAVs may not be guaranteed the green light resources at all their upstream intersections and therefore the CAVs may have to choose stand with the delay at certain intersections or just change it route to avoid those congested intersections, resulting in either late arrivals at the next intersections or changes to involved intersections along the new paths. Whenever this situation occurs, it means all the downstream intersections will have a new optimization problem to solve.

### 3.2. MILP formulation

In principle, machine scheduling problems have multiple mixed integer programming (MIP) or MILP formulations according to the selected variable set. According to the literature due to Keha et al. (2009), feasible variable sets include jobs' completion times; each job's starting time; all jobs' linear ordering and; each job's assignment and positional date. After discretizing the time, we adopt the jobs' completion times as the variable set to formulate the automated vehicle scheduling problem at an intersection into a MILP problem. Table 2 shows the notations, with most decision variables being timeindexed, and the arrival times and departure times of vehicles at each movement segment are scheduled to minimize the total delay.

If the optimization goal is to minimize the total weighted travel time of all vehicles, and T is long enough for all vehicles to cross the intersection, the optimization problem can be formulated as:

$$
\min \sum_{u \in U}\left[w_{u} \times\left(e_{u, m_{3}}-r_{u}\right)\right]
$$

Since it is assumed that vehicles strictly move after they enter the 2nd segment, Problem (1) is equivalent to minimize the total lateness of those vehicles behind their scheduled times crossing the intersection. A vehicle's lateness is defined as the actual time for a vehicle to cross the intersection $\left(e_{u, m_{3}}-p t_{u, m_{3}}-p t_{u, m_{2}}\right)$ minus the ideal non-delay crossing time $\left(r_{u}+p t_{u, m_{1}}\right)$.

\end{aligned}

$$

## 4. Construct phase-time-traffic (PTR) hypernetwork to model heterogeneous traffic propagation under various intersection automation policies

Within a more realistic heterogeneous traffic environment, additional challenges surface other than the constraints in the above MILP mathematical formulation. With the SDP modeling framework, the challenge is that elements including traffic signal operations, heterogeneous traffic propagation as well as CAV schedules all involve additional hard constraints. The complex interactions between those elements make it nearly impossible to solve the problem efficiently with mathematicalprogramming-based solvers. To address these issues, we develop a new phase-time-traffic hypernetwork model, referred to as the PTR hypernetwork, to "pre-build" most hard constraints and complex interactions among heterogeneous traffic, traffic signal operations and CAV requests into the PTR hypernetwork itself to ensure the traffic dynamics feasibility and signal transition feasibility during the sequential traffic decision process.

### 4.1. Traffic signal operation representation

Three items are necessary to define a complete traffic signal control strategy at intersections: signal phases, phasing sequence and respective phase durations. The first two items can be well represented in the classic state-transition diagram, but it cannot reflect the phase durations. To address this issue, Li et al. (2015) developed the phase-time network model or PT network model to reflect all three items in an acyclic, discrete and forward network as shown in Fig. 3. Each arc in the PT network is interpreted as one feasible traffic signal operation. The inter-phase transitions (e.g., yellow and all-red) clearance can be considered part of the phase duration. Just for argument purpose, let us assume the minimum and maximum greens of two phases are 1 s and 4 s respectively in Fig. 3. The inter-phase duration is uniformly 1 s. Therefore, then the phase durations range from 2 s to 5 s. Apparently, traffic propagations during yellow, all-red and the initial minimum green need special treatments to generate a more realistic delay. For example, in calculating the phase-time arc cost, it would be necessary to use reduced or zero signal link capacities during the yellow, all-red clearance and start-up loss period. Depending on the magnitude of applications, the time steps for IAP optimization can be long or short. While longer time resolution will result in sub-optimal IAPs, the computing efficiency can be significantly increased for super large problems. Without loss of generality, we set the time steps as 1 s in this paper.

Seeking the optimal traffic signal timing in the phase-time network is, in essence, to seek the shortest path from the start PT node (current signal phase at current time) to the super sink PT node (a virtual node after the time horizon in order to). All hard constraints for traffic signal control systems, including the minimum and maximum green requirements on each phase, mutual exclusiveness of incompatible phases and cyclic or flexible phasing sequences, can be "prebuilt" into the PT network. Please note that the term "phase" has two different definitions all over the world. In North America, the phase refers to one feasible vehicle movement within intersections whereas it refers to a group of compatible vehicle movements within intersections in Europe, also called "stages". The European definition is used in this paper because hard constraints based on the European phases ("Stages") are fewer than those based on the North America phases in formulating the same traffic signal optimization problem. Li et al. (2015) proves that the two definitions are interchangeable in representing traffic signal operations both in mathematics and in practice. In contrast, Head et al. ### 5.2. Vehicle-centered traffic state representation in space-time network for heterogeneous traffic

### 5.2.1 Mapping from space-time arc choice variables to vehicle locations

Let us define an arc-time-indexed arc choice variable $x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}$ in space-time networks which is equal to 1 if $u$ moves on link $(i, j)$ from time $s$ to $s^{\prime}$ and equal to 0 otherwise. If vehicles are assumed to move at link free-flow speeds unless they meet congestion, $u$ 's exact location on $(i, j)$ at $t$ can be obtained according to the time elapsed since $s$. Using a threesegment route similar to the MILP formulation defined in (1)-(27), Fig. 8 illustrates this concept and the notations for the corresponding mathematical formulation is summarized in Table 5.

Based on the above definition, while a vehicle $u$ is moving, its location can be exactly expressed with the 0-1 values of relevant $x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}$ along its path. As a result, with the vehicle-centered traffic state representation, a traffic state $r$ at $t$ can be expressed collectively as all vehicles' instantaneous locations at $t$ or $r=\left\{x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}\right\}, \forall u \in U, s \leq t \leq s^{\prime},(i, j) \in A$.

When multiple vehicles are moving simultaneously, traffic propagation is also constrained by road capacity and traffic signal operations. Assuming a traffic signal phase $p$ is green from $t_{0}$ to $s_{0}$, then during that time, traffic propagation must comply with the following constraints.

### 5.2.2. Constraints

Link capacity constraints

$$
\sum_{u \in U} x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)} \leq \mathcal{M}(p, i, j) * S R_{(i, j, s)}, \forall s, s^{\prime} \sim\left(t_{0}, s_{0}\right), \forall(i, j) \in A, p \in P
$$

Network flow conservation constraints
The network flow conservation constraints guarantee vehicles to find feasible trajectories along their paths. For a spacetime node $\left(j, s^{\prime}\right)$ where waiting is not allowed:

$$
\sum_{\left(i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right) \in A} x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}-\sum_{\left(j, s^{\prime}, i^{\prime}, s^{\prime \prime}\right) \in A} x_{\left(u, j, s^{\prime}, i^{\prime}, s^{\prime \prime}\right)}=\left\{\begin{array}{rl}
-1 ; & \left(j, s^{\prime}\right)=o_{u} \\
1 ; & \left(j, s^{\prime}\right)=d_{u} \\
0 ; & \text { otherwise }
\end{array}, \forall u \in U\right.
$$

Where waiting is allowed:

$$
\sum_{\left(i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right) \in A} x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}-\sum_{\left(j, s^{\prime}, i^{\prime}, s^{\prime \prime}\right) \in A} x_{\left(u, j, s^{\prime}, i^{\prime}, s^{\prime \prime}\right)}+x_{\left(u, j, s^{\prime}-1, j, s^{\prime}\right)}-x_{\left(u, j, s^{\prime}, j, s^{\prime}+1\right)}=\left\{\begin{array}{rl}
-1 ; & \left(j, s^{\prime}\right)=o_{u} \\
1 ; & \left(j, s^{\prime}\right)=d_{u} \\
0 ; & \text { otherwise }
\end{array}, \forall u \in U\right.
$$

First-in-first-out (FIFO) constraints
If $x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}=1, \forall u \in U$, then $\sum_{u^{\prime} \in U} \sum_{\left(s^{\prime \prime}\left\langle s, s^{\prime \prime \prime}\right\rangle s^{\prime}\right)} x_{\left(u^{\prime}, i, s^{\prime \prime}, j, s^{\prime \prime \prime}\right)}=0, \forall\left(i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right),\left(i, s^{\prime \prime}, j, s^{\prime \prime \prime}\right) \in A$. Such "IF-THEN" restrictions guarantee FIFO constraints on link $(i, j)$ and it can be formulated as linear constraints using a large integer $Q$ as:

$$
\sum_{u^{\prime} \in U} \sum_{\left(s^{\prime \prime}\left\langle s, s^{\prime \prime \prime}\right\rangle s^{\prime}\right)} x_{\left(u^{\prime}, i, s^{\prime \prime}, j, s^{\prime \prime \prime}\right)} \leq\left(1-x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}\right) \times Q
$$

In (36), if $x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}=1$ (i.e., $u$ enters link $(i, j)$ at $s$ and leaves at $\left.s^{\prime}\right), \sum_{u^{\prime} \in U} \sum_{\left(s^{\prime \prime}\left\langle s, s^{\prime \prime \prime}\right) s^{\prime}\right)} x_{\left(u^{\prime}, i, s^{\prime \prime}, j, s^{\prime \prime \prime}\right)}=0$, meaning that, for $\forall u^{\prime} \neq u$, if $u^{\prime}$ enters link $(i, j)$ earlier than $u\left(s^{\prime \prime}<s\right)$, it will not leave link $(i, j)$ later than $u\left(s^{\prime \prime \prime}>s^{\prime}\right)$; if $x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}=0$, $x_{\left(u^{\prime}, i, s^{\prime \prime}, j, s^{\prime \prime \prime}\right)}$ is not constrained.

### 5.2.3. Edge cost estimation

In theory, heterogeneous traffic state changes under a feasible signal operation include changes of both traffic delay and served CAV requests, and therefore it cannot be expressed as simple cost values. However, in practice we can also distinguish CAVs and traditional vehicles by allocating different weights. In doing so, it is possible to compare two traffic states just with weighted summation of delays. During a feasible signal operation from $t_{0}$ to $s_{0}$, only vehicles approaching the intersection but arriving later than the scheduled times $a_{u}$ (delayed in-progress vehicles) are counted in calculating total delays at $t_{0}$ and $s_{0}$. Mathematically, the delay changes (i.e., the edge cost $c_{\left(p, t, r, p^{\prime}, t^{\prime}, r^{\prime}\right)}$ ) can be calculated as:

$$
\sum_{u \in U, s \geq a_{u}}\left(1-\sum_{\left(t_{0} \leq t \leq s_{0}\right)} \sum_{(i, j) \in A_{s}} x_{(u, i, t, j, s)}\right) w_{u}\left(s_{0}-a_{u}\right)-\sum_{u \in U, s \geq a_{u}}\left(1-\sum_{\left(t \leq t_{0}\right)} \sum_{(i, j) \in A_{s}} x_{\left(u, i, s, j, s^{\prime}\right)}\right) w_{u}\left(t_{0}-a_{u}\right)
$$

Constraint (37) imposes that the total delay at $t_{0}$ is aggregated from the delays of those individual vehicles which are approaching ( $1-\sum_{\left(a_{u} \leq t \leq t_{0}\right)} \sum_{(i, j) \in A_{s}} x_{(u, i, t, j, s)}=0$ ) but have delays ( $t_{0} \geq a_{u}$ ). The total delay at $s_{0}$ is calculated similarly. Please note that, if a vehicle crosses the stop line between $t_{0}$ and $s_{0}\left(\sum_{\left(t_{0} \leq t \leq s_{0}\right)} \sum_{(i, j) \in A_{s}} x_{(u, i, t, j, s)}=1\right)$, it is considered leaving the scope of the subject intersection and is not counted in the delay calculation at $s_{0}$.

It is worthwhile noting that (37) is similar to the MILP formulation with 100\% CAVs in which the total delays are summed from individual vehicle delays (each vehicle's actual finishing time minus scheduled finishing time) and, in practice, traffic propagation can also be approximated using the dynamic network loading (DNL) tools.

## 6. Sequential branch-and-bound search algorithm in PTR hypernetwork

When the new vehicle-centered traffic state representation is used, the master optimization problem (33) could be very challenging to solve with mathematical programing solvers. To address this challenge, we develop a sequential branch-andbound (BB) search algorithm over time to seek the optimal IAP in $\mathcal{H}$. The proposed BB search algorithm is to construct a tree through repetitive branching. At each branching level, all leafs (i.e., sub-problems) are first compared with existing search efforts to examine whether any leaf is promising to obtain a better solution. If improvement from a leaf is impossible, further exploiting this leaf will just achieve an inferior solution and therefore this leaf can be pruned. Otherwise, leaves are further branched until the termination condition is satisfied. The rationale of the proposed BB search algorithm is that each BB node has a uniquely matched PT node in the phase-time plane of the PTR hypernetwork and it makes BB nodes at the same or different branching levels "comparable" whenever they refer to the same PT node for pruning purposes.

Apparently, in the worst case, the BB algorithm could be slow due to: (a) excessive searching space; (b) poor pruning rule. To mitigate these two problems, we propose the concept of a "phase-time prism" to reduce the searching space and develop efficient pruning rules to improve the search efficiency.

### 6.1. Phase-time prism to reduce search space for sequential decision making

The optimization problem defined in (33) can be viewed as a variant of the shortest path problem in $\mathcal{H}$ with dynamic edge costs only known when this sequential traffic system is running forward. The shortest path in $\mathcal{H}$ must visit all intermediate CAV requests. Therefore if a path cannot service all CAV requests, it will not be the optimal solution to (31). We can further show that, starting from any edge belonging to the shortest path in $\mathcal{H}$, there is at least one feasible path (e.g., the shortest path itself) capable of serving all subsequent green requests.

The above analysis leads us toward a promising operation on search space reduction: we may first exclude those PTR edges incapable of serving all the subsequence green requests, namely "unreachable edges" prior to a fully-loaded (considering background traffic) BB search. The benefits of this operation are twofold: (a) the edge elimination can be quickly carried out in $\mathcal{H}^{\prime}$ s phase-time plane; (b) unreachable edges/arcs can be efficiently eliminated through polynomial-time search algorithms. Fig. 9a illustrates some types of unreachable PT arcs between two CAV requests. For instance, PT arc $l_{1}$ cannot serve the green request on phase 3 within its time window and $l_{2}$ cannot serve a CAV request on phase 2 within the time window and so they should be eliminated from the search space since they will not be part of any feasible traffic signal operations. The remaining PT arcs compose the phase-time prism. To eliminate those unreachable edges efficiently, we propose an algorithm in the phase-time network described in Appendix 1.

Please note that a CAV request may have a time window rather than just a time point. In that case, Algorithm 1 should be slightly modified to perform backward and forward searches from any feasible time point of a CAV request to any feasible time point of the next CAV request. The resulting backward reachable edge set $L I S T{ }^{\text {backward }}$ and forward reachable edge set LIST ${ }^{\text {forward }}$ will accordingly be the union of such multiple searches. A heuristic procedure obtaining a phase-time prism over time in this paper is to call Algorithm 1 iteratively between any consecutive green requests. Fig. 9b illustrates the concept of a phase-time prism.

In the meantime, identifying unreachable PT arcs can be viewed as a vehicle routing problem in the phase-time network in which it is desired to identify whether a "vehicle" can find a feasible path from the current PT arc to the super sink and serve ("pick up") all the intermediate CAV requests ("passengers"). 6.2. Sequential branch-and-bound search algorithm for optimal IAP in the reduced $\mathcal{H}$

The fully-loaded (both automated and traditional vehicles) optimization is based on the branch-and-bound (BB) search framework. The structure of the PTR hypernetwork naturally allows for branching with respect to the feasible next phases and phase durations. Although the BB algorithms usually take exponential time to get an exact solution, it is found that the proposed BB algorithm with branching, bounding and pruning rules appropriately designed is efficient in solving the problem of this paper from our preliminary experiments.
6.2.1. Branching rule

The proposed PTR hypernetwork model can be naturally branched from the current phase (parent level) to generate children BB nodes according to the pre-built feasible next phases and durations. In Fig. 10-a, we assume four traffic signal phases with flexible phasing sequence and the feasible green time of each phase is 2 s. Phase 1 (current phase) at $t=0$ is considered the root BB node and the branching efforts can be conducted two levels before reaching the end of the time horizon.
6.2.2 Pruning (Dominance) rule

Pruning occurs when a partial traffic signal operation dominates another. The dominated (inferior) traffic state can be safely pruned without losing the global optimum because any subsequent traffic signal operations starting from the domi-
nated (inferior) traffic state can be exactly replicated by another timing plan starting from the dominating (superior) traffic state. The key to the pruning rule is how to compare two intermediate traffic states to identify which dominates. A traffic state in this context includes traffic signal operations, traffic dynamics (individual vehicle locations) and CAV request service status (served vs. unserved). As such, two traffic states are considered the same only when they are on the same phase-time node (same traffic signal status), have the same traffic state and have the same CAV status (served vs. unserved). Ideally, a traffic state dominates another if and only if all the above elements are better than or equal to each other. However, such a rigorous pruning rule will obviously minimize the pruning operations and degrade Algorithm 2 to an exhaustive search. Inspired by the literature due to Zhou and Zhong (2007), we design the dominance rule as follows:

An intermediate BB node at various levels in essence represents a partial traffic signal plan under which any vehicle (both CAV and traditional vehicles) will only have two states: either completed (i.e., cross the stop line) or in progress (before the stop line).

Let $N_{b b}^{1}, N_{b b}^{2}$ denote two branch-and-bound nodes; $C_{p, t}^{1}, C_{p, t}^{2}, I_{p, t}^{1}, I_{p, t}^{2}$ denote the sets of completed vehicles and in-progress vehicles from the root BB node to $N_{b b}^{1}, N_{b b}^{2}$ respectively. BB node $N_{b b}^{1}$ dominates $N_{b b}^{2}$ if:

a) $N_{b b}^{1}, N_{b b}^{2}$ refers to the same phase-time node
b) All served CAVs in $C_{p, t}^{2}$ are also in $C_{p, t}^{1}$, meaning $N_{b b}^{1}$ serves equal or more CAV requests than $N_{b b}^{2}$;
c) For those vehicles in both $C_{p, t}^{1}$ and $C_{p, t}^{2}$, they generated equal or larger delay in $C_{p, t}^{2}$ than in $C_{p, t}^{1}$;
d) All CAVs in $C_{p, t}^{2}$ are either in $I_{p, t}^{1}$ or in $C_{p, t}^{1}$;
e) For each vehicle in $I_{p, t}^{2}$, its location at $N_{b b}^{2}$ is the same or behind its location at $N_{b b}^{1}$

If (a)-(e) are all met, then $N_{b b}^{2}$ is pruned. Simple proof for this dominance rule is inspired by Demeulemeester and Herroelen's work (1992) and provided as below. For the extensive proof, please refer to their literature.

The requirements (a), (b), and (d) provide comparable conditions between two BB nodes with respect to the subset of vehicles. Requirement (c) indicates that the total historical delay generated by the completed vehicle at $N_{b b}^{2}$ is equal to or larger than its counterpart at $N_{b b}^{1}$. After constructing any complete traffic signal schedules, we can further prove that the newly generated delay by vehicles in $I_{p, t}^{2}$ will be equal to or larger than the delay generated by vehicles in $I_{p, t}^{2}$. That is, we can always construct the same delaying alternative in $N_{b b}^{1}$, after the same phase-time node. The definition of delaying alternative, often used in the project scheduling domain, indicates that it is always possible to delay an in-progress vehicle with advantages in $N_{b b}^{1}$ to create a comparable vehicle trajectory for the in-process vehicle in $N_{b b}^{2}$ in order to maintain the overall comparable conditions.

Based on the above description, the pruning rule is defined as below:
Denote LIST ${ }_{\text {EXTENSION }}$ as an extension list composed of a set of pair $\left\langle C_{(p, t)}, P_{(p, t)}\right\rangle$ where $P_{(p, t)}$ denotes a sub-problem after a few traffic signal operations has been determined and reach the phase-time node $(p, t) . C_{(p, t)}$ represents the status of vehicles updated last time at $(p, t)$, including the sets of complete and in-progress vehicles under partial traffic signal schedules. From the perspective of the PTR hypernetwork, the partial traffic signal operations can be viewed as a partial path and each $P_{(p, t)}$ represents a new path-finding problem from the new PT node to super sink.

The branching routine will generate multiple new sub-problems first, and the pruning routine then examines whether any of the new sub-problem $P_{(p, t)}$ is already in LIST ${ }_{\text {EXTENSION }}$. If not, generate a new pair of $\left\langle C_{(p, t)}, P_{(p, t)}\right\rangle$ and add it into LIST ${ }_{\text {EXTENSION }}$. If $P_{(p, t)}$ is already in LIST ${ }_{\text {EXTENSION }}$, it means that the new source has been visited by other sub-problems before. Then the pruning algorithm further compares the $C_{(p, t)}$. If new $C_{(p, t)}$ dominates existing $C_{(p, t)}$ in LIST EXTENSION, then update $C_{(p, t)}$ with the new traffic state. Otherwise this sub-problem can be pruned. Fig. 10-b illustrates the pruning process, and all values are given for argument's sake. All the BB nodes are aligned by time, and the comparable BB nodes refer to the same phase-time node in the phase-time plane of the PTR hypernetwork. As an illustration, BB node (iii) and (v) in Fig. 10-b are comparable because they refer to the same PT node in the phase-time plane of the PTR hypernetwork.

### 6.2.3 Bounding rule

Since we consider both completed and in-progress vehicles, as well as CAV request statuses (served vs. unserved), the bounds at each BB node are not simple values but compound representations. In order to prune, it is necessary to store the latest status (i.e., the most dominating partial traffic signal schedule so far) with respect to the conditions (a) through (e) defined in the dominance rule as the bound (or benchmark) at each phase-time node.

After defining the branching, pruning and bounding rules, the branch-and-bound search algorithm is described in detail in Appendix Two.

Discussion: The pruning (dominance) rule is the most critical in determining the efficiency of BB search algorithms. The above pruning rule is based on the vehicle-centered traffic representation described in Sections 5.1 and 5.2. New features of the vehicle-centered traffic representation include that individual vehicle's path-based cumulative traffic delay, as well as the vehicle type (weight), can be explicitly marked and therefore additional flexibilities are introduced in modeling heterogeneous traffic dynamics. On the other hand, the vehicle-centered traffic representation is in essence an extension from the classic flow-based traffic representation in the sense that all individual vehicles propagate subject to the same constraints like the first-in-first-out rule and link capacities defined in a classic flow-based traffic representation. As a result, even within the traffic environment of 100\% traditional vehicles, the above pruning rule can also apply to traffic state comparison based on the classic cumulative counting curves.

If a request is rejected, the corresponding CAV is allowed to resend its request later. Based on our finding, we argue that when there are excessive CAVs attempting to cross an intersection, the reject-and-resend approach may not work effectively if the CAV arrivals continue high for a long period. In that case, some CAVs must be re-routed to other intersections in order to ensure that the total number of CAV green requests is not beyond the intersection capacity of serving CAV requests. Otherwise, those CAVs will have to be treated as traditional vehicles at intersections.

## 8. Concluding remarks

This paper is inspired by emerging research needs for intersection automation policies (IAP) within a heterogeneous traffic environment comprised of both CAVs and traditional vehicles. A feature of CAVs is to place requests for green lights along their paths minutes ahead. Serving CAV requests at intersections is in essence to schedule green lights to serve approaching CAVs at its maximal potential. By contrast, traditional vehicles will only be identified near or at intersections, leaving little time for traffic control systems to react. Therefore, the higher the CAV ratio is, the earlier intersections can begin to schedule their green resources for approaching vehicles and the more likely the total control delay is minimized.

A mixed-integer-linear-programming (MILP) formulation for the IAP optimization problem within a 100\% CAV environment is first presented and then the IAP optimization problem within a heterogeneous environment is formulated in a mixed-integer programming (MIP) form based on the new phase-time-traffic (PTR) hypernetwork to closely couple three sub-problems: traffic signal operations, heterogeneous traffic dynamics and CAV requests for green lights. Then the IAP optimization is viewed as a CAV scheduling problem to serve the CAV requests at the minimal total delays. It is also concluded that intersections have a capacity limit of serving CAV requests due to the mandates in traffic signal operations. In light or moderate traffic, intersections can serve the CAV requests effectively with up to a 10\% CAV ratio. Beyond that ratio, many CAV requests will be rejected and the rejected CAVs should be suggested to reschedule their paths. In contrast, other intersection automation policies at intersections seldom consider the CAV rerouting option to balance CAV request demand among intersections and mostly adopt a simple reject-and-resend strategy for CAV requests at local intersections.

The crux of the proposed IAP optimization is that we extend the classic flow-based traffic representation and formulate a new vehicle-centered traffic state representation to reflect the heterogeneous traffic flow with CAV requests. Such extensions allow for distinguishing traditional vehicles from CAVs in traffic dynamics modeling and can explicitly reflect certain features of CAVs, such as pre-scheduled routes, route-based cumulative vehicle delay expressions, etc. These features of vehicle-centered traffic representation are critical in modeling heterogeneous traffic propagation, over congestion, and complex interactions with infrastructure in the future.

We further develop a new concept of the PTR hypernetwork, as well as a sequential branch-and-bound (BB) optimization algorithm to seek the exact optimal IAP in the PTR hypernetwork. Compared with other mathematical programming formulations, one main convenience in our approach is "pre-building" most hard constraints of the IAP optimization problem into the PTR hypernetwork (the objective function itself) and the BB search algorithm can be deployed in the PTR hypernetwork straightforwardly. As the core part of the BB search algorithm, the pruning rule is designed considering both vehicles' location status (cross the stop line or not) and CAV request serving status (served vs. unserved). The same pruning rule can be applied not only to the new vehicle-centered traffic representation but also to the classic flow-based traffic representation. In addition, parallel computing technique is used to enable the proposed optimization algorithm to be scalable for future large-scale problems.

Although the IAP optimization framework proposed in this paper targets the heterogeneous traffic environment, it can also be applied to the scenarios of 100\% traditional vehicles or 100\% CAV vehicles. With 100\% traditional vehicles, we can either use the classic cumulative counting curves to compare and prune dominated partial solutions or use the newly proposed vehicle-centered traffic representation. With the heterogeneous traffic or 100\% CAVs condition, the vehicle-centered traffic representation can explicitly reflect individual vehicle trajectories and interactions. The optimal IAP will serve the CAV requests at its maximum potential and maintain an acceptable traffic mobility. Special vehicles like fire trucks or buses have highly connected and therefore can provide most information as the CAVs. Therefore the proposed IAP optimization framework can also be promising to solve special-vehicle-related problems at intersections.

The IAP optimization framework proposed in this paper also has potential for network-wide CAV scheduling as well in the sense that (a) the definition of "phases" at intersections can be generalized as a group of "openable simultaneously" movements across intersections (See Fig. 11 for illustration); (b) all the CAV requests are proactively placed according to their scheduled paths and the proposed IAP optimization framework at congested intersections can also suggest certain CAVs to drop their requests and re-schedule their paths to avoid congestion. In the future, we plan to combine the proposed IAP with other CAV path scheduling policies to ensure that the CAV requests do not exceed intersections' maximal capacity. In doing so, it is expected that balanced CAV requests can be served at each intersection and so travel time reliability will be greatly improved. We also plan to further improve the efficiency of parallel computing in the branch-and-bound search algorithm for large-scale applications.

## Appendix 2. Branch-and-bound search algorithm (Algorithm 2)

Let $U B_{g}$ denote the global upper bound and set $U B_{g}=+\infty$ initially, $c_{l_{k}}$ denote the "cost-to-arrive" of each BB node $l_{k}(l=1$, $2, \ldots, k=1,2, \ldots$ ) at branching Level $k$, indicating the cumulative cost from the root BB node to $l_{k}$ and set $c_{\text {root }}=0$ and $c_{l_{k}}=$ $+\infty$, denote $\Delta c_{l_{k} \rightarrow l_{k+1}}$ as the new cost from $l_{k}$ to $l_{k+1}$, denote $p_{0}$ as the current signal phase and $\left(p_{0}, 0\right)$ as the source PT node, $\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k}\right)$ as the mapping function from a BB node $l_{k}$ to a PT node $(p, t)$ in the phase-time plane of PTR hypernetwork $\left(l_{k} \rightarrow(p, t)\right)$, denote $\operatorname{List}_{\text {EXTENSION }}=\left\{\left\langle\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k}\right), c_{l_{k}}\right\rangle\right\}$ as the list of all PT-node $\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k}\right)$ visited at least once during search and the most dominating "cost-to-arrive" $c_{l_{k}}(k=0,1,2, \ldots)$ so far; denote $c_{l_{k}}^{\prime}$ as a new "cost-to-arrive" for a PT-node and; denote List $_{\text {INACTIVE }}=\left\{l_{k}\right\}$ where $l_{k}$ is either pruned or no longer further branched;

Step 0 Initialization: Set List $_{\text {EXTENSION }}=\emptyset$, List $_{\text {INACTIVE }}=\emptyset$. Set the initial branching level $k=0$ and $\mathcal{F}\left(1_{k=0}\right)=\left(p_{0}, 0\right)$. Conduct the initial branching to get $\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k=1}\right)=(p, t)$ along all admissible PT $\operatorname{arc}\left(p_{0}, 0, p, t\right)$, where $(l=1,2, \ldots),$.$p a feasible next signal$ phase of $p_{0}$ and $g_{\text {min }}\left(p_{0}\right) \leq t \leq g_{\text {min }}\left(p_{0}\right)$ (i.e., $(p, t)$ is incident to $\left(p_{0}, 0, p, t\right)$. Please note that we ignore yellow and all-red clearance of all signal phases for simplification.

Step 1 Examine terminal BB nodes: At Level $k$, first examine each active node $l_{k}$ (i.e., $l_{k} \notin$ List $_{\text {INACTIVE }}$ ). If the cumulative operating time from root to $l_{k}$ has reach $T$, it means a new feasible solution has been reached from 0 to $T$ and $U B_{g}=$ $\min \left(U B_{g}, c_{l_{k}}\right)$. Insert $l_{k}$ into List ${ }_{\text {INACTIVE }}$.

Step 2 Prune with extension list: For each active BB node $l_{k}$ at Level $k$, if $\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k}\right) \in$ List $_{\text {EXTENSION }}$ and the existing $c_{l_{k}}$ dominates new $c_{l_{k}}^{\prime}$, insert $l_{k}$ into $\operatorname{List}_{\text {INACTIVE }}$ (i.e., $l_{k}$ is pruned); if $\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k}\right) \in \operatorname{List}_{\text {EXTENSION }}$ and new $c_{l_{k}}^{\prime}$ dominates the existing $c_{l_{k}}$, then replace $\left\langle\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k}\right), c_{l_{k}}\right\rangle$ with $\left\langle\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k}\right), c^{\prime} l_{k}\right\rangle$ in $\operatorname{LIST}_{\text {EXTENSION }}$.

Step 3 Branching: For each remaining active node at Level $k$, if $\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k}\right) \notin$ List $_{\text {EXTENSION }}$, call the branching rule to get $l_{k}$ 's children nodes $\left\{l_{k+1}\right\}$ and calculate $\Delta c_{l_{k} \rightarrow l_{k+1}}$ and set $c_{l_{k+1}}^{\prime}=c_{l_{k}}+\Delta c_{l_{k} \rightarrow l_{k+1}}$. If $\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k+1}\right) \notin \operatorname{List}$ EXTENSION, insert $\left\langle\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k+1}\right), c^{\prime} l_{k+1}\right\rangle$ into List $_{\text {EXTENSION }}$. If $\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k+1}\right) \in$ List $_{\text {EXTENSION }}$ and $c_{l_{k+1}}^{\prime}$ dominates the existing $c_{l_{k+1}}$, then update $c_{l_{k+1}}$ with $c_{l_{k+1}}^{\prime}$; if $\mathcal{F}\left(l_{k+1}\right) \in$ List ${ }_{\text {EXTENSION }}$ and the existing $c_{l_{k+1}}$ dominates new $c_{l_{k+1}}^{\prime}$, then insert $l_{k+1}$ into List $_{\text {INACTIVE }}$ (i.e., $l_{k+1}$ is pruned).

Please note that Step 3 consumes most of the computing time during search and it can be parallelized with respect to green durations and the feasible next signal phase(s).

Step 4 Stopping criterion: If all $l_{k} \in$ List $_{\text {INACTIVE }}$, the algorithm stops. Otherwise, set $k=k+1$ and go to Step 1 .
After the BB search, it is possible to trace back to get an optimal traffic signal timing plan with the minimum overall delay.
