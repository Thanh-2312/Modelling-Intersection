#### Abstract

In this paper, a decentralized traffic signal control strategy named max pressure control is reviewed and examined. This control strategy aims at optimizing overall network throughputs, but applies a distributed approach that only requires local information to generate timing plans for each intersection. A Vissim simulation study is conducted to compare existing max pressure schemes. The results show that a recently proposed cyclic-based approach performs more poorly than the original non-cyclic approach. Further, to address two issues that hinder existing schemes: frequent changes of phase and queue spillover/blockage, two modifications are suggested. The simulation reveals that network performance can be improved after modifications.


Max pressure signal control (MP) is a distributed traffic signal control strategy that has attracted much attention in recent years (1-4). Unlike traditional centralized control strategies that involve high computational complexity, the essence of MP is a decentralized algorithm that requires only local information to perform a flexible control for each intersection. MP holds great promise for real-time adaptive control of traffic signals, particularly when robust communication is made available with the deployment of connected vehicle technologies. The control is highly scalable and can be readily implemented for large-scale urban traffic networks. More importantly, MP has been theoretically proven to bound the queue length under largest possible arrival demands, a global optimality that has not yet been established for some other decentralized approaches (1-3, 5).

Since the global optimality of MP is theoretically guaranteed by assuming point or vertical queues (2-4), simulation studies are necessary to validate the effectiveness of MP signal control. However, previous simulation studies either adopted macroscopic models that do not well represent driving behaviors and traffic dynamics, or considered overly simplified network settings in which MP control cannot be adequately examined. For example, Kouvelas et al. (6) developed an event-based simulator to compare two variations of MP, but this macroscopic simulation study neglects considerations of car following and lane changing behaviors. Other studies applied Simulation of Urban Mobility (SUMO) as their simulation platform (4, 7), which, again, is weak in replicating realistic driving behaviors. Although some other studies applied microscopic traffic simulators, the scenario settings are oversimplified, for example, isolated intersection (8) or two-phase timing plans (9). These studies cannot fully examine the distributed nature of MP signal control and thus impede fair validation of its performance. As a conclusion, a simulation study that employs a widely-accepted microscopic traffic simulator and well-defined traffic network becomes necessary.

In this paper, we review the potential benefits and drawbacks of MP policies through a comprehensive simulation study based on a real traffic network. Unlike many of the previous simulation studies (4, 7, 10), Vissim is applied in this study for better replication of traffic network topology and flow dynamics. The original non-cyclic MP, cyclic phase MP, and the currentlyimplemented actuated signal control are compared in terms of travel time, delay, throughput, and queueing dynamics. Possible modifications of MP to overcome policy deficiencies are further discussed.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. A literature review on MP control is presented first, followed by mathematical formulations of two existing MP policies as well as modifications of the original non-cyclic MP policy. The next two sections introduce the simulation settings, and then analyze and compare the

[^0]performances of the control policies in the simulated traffic network. The last section presents the conclusions of the paper.

## Literature Review

Network-based signal control strategies can be classified into centralized and decentralized controls depending on their system architectures, while the latter is also referred to as distributed control. Centralized control systems usually possess a central computing unit where all control decisions take place. In practice, centralized controls normally have limited scalability since they are subject to increasing computational complexity and the lack of reliable communication links. In contrast, fully distributed signal control systems have no central computing units so that control operations rely on local intersection controllers. In some classic multi-hierarchy control strategies, such as OPAC (11) and RHODES (12), distributed controls are implemented for individual intersections using local information from detection and prediction. In recent years, research interest in distributed signal control has been aroused worldwide because of its scalability and flexibility (13-16). MP control policy, which is also referred to as back-pressure control policy, is one such system.

The idea of MP was first proposed for packet scheduling problems in communication networks (5). The control policy was constructed as a linear programming to maximize the total network throughput with queueing dynamics being described as a Markov process while a new terminology of pressure was introduced to denote the difference of upstream and downstream queues. An efficient algorithm was designed and has been successfully implemented in wireless network controls. Several adjustments were made when applying MP to traffic signal control. For example, since in wireless network control the destinations and routing information of packets are known, control units directly control flow rates. In contrast, hard to obtain destinations and routing information of vehicles are in signalized traffic networks: MP signal control determines green time durations instead of flow rates (1). It is also worth noting that MP signal control differs from another pressure-based signal control proposed by Lämmer and Helbing earlier (17), which calculates pressure with information at each isolated intersection while neglecting dependency and coordination between adjacent intersections.

To prove that MP achieves maximum throughput for traffic networks, previous studies considered point or vertical queues and assumed that storage capacities are infinite (1, 2). A store-and-forward network is often used to describe queueing dynamics (18). However, performing store-and-forward model in microscopic simulation studies may lead to traffic conservation failures and congestion propagation (8). Several efforts have been made to relax this assumption. A concept called weighted queues was introduced and tested by an event-based macroscopic simulation model $(2,6)$ and showed significant improvement of total throughput as compared with the original MP control. Gregoire et al. reformulated pressure and proposed a variation of MP named "capacityaware backpressure control." This capacity-aware control was also validated by a simulation study and proven to be stabilized in a grid network under high demand scenarios where original MP fails. Another denotative study was focused on MP control in a switching network with limited buffer size (19). Although not considering traffic networks, this pioneering study proved that when the control strategy is still modeled as a linear programming problem, the maximum throughput cannot be achieved: the actual throughput achieved is regulated by the buffer size and number of nodes in the network.

While achieving maximum throughput, MP produces unpredictable phase sequence. A straightforward remedy is to fix cycle length and phase sequence before determining the phase durations based on pressure. Kouvelas et al. suggested determining phase durations proportionally based on the pressure magnitudes (6). The suggestion fails when pressures of some phases become non-positive. An enhanced version named "cyclic phase backpressure control policy" was later proposed in which an exponential function of pressure is introduced to determine the green splits, and was proved to stabilize the traffic network for a certain demand region (4). This policy was further extended to allow different priorities for different phases to be activated. However, this extended cyclic phase MP maintains network stability only under mild traffic conditions where the maximum queue length does not exceed half of the maximum storage capacity (20, 21).

Another relevant stream of research is to integrate MP control with dynamic traffic routing due to its simplicity and adaptivity. For example, Zaidi et al. (22) applied MP in a multi-commodity network, where vehicles adopt fixed or adaptive routing respectively. The comparison study showed that adaptive routing performed better when traffic demand is high. Cai et al. (10) incorporated another variation of MP with dynamic traffic routing. This MP variation not only considers the difference between in-link and out-link queue lengths, but also includes upstream demand during service time as a part of pressure calculation. However, the authors did not prove or demonstrate the stability of the proposed MP variation.

## Max Pressure-Based Signal Control

In this section, the control of a general traffic network with signalized intersections is studied. Traffic related
variables as well as control related variables are introduced first. Control policies stated by Vairaya (2) are then reviewed. Mathematical models are established to describe the vehicle arrivals and departures and queueing process to facilitate a better understanding of the stability property of MP. Two modifications on MP and another category of cyclic control proposed by Le et al. (3) are also presented.

## Notations and Terminologies

The notations used are listed below. As for the important terminology "phase," the Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) defines it as the right-ofway, yellow change and red clearance intervals in a cycle that are assigned to an independent traffic movement (23). In the NEMA standard, eight phases corresponding with through and left turning movements from four approaches in a typical intersection are numbered from 1 to 8. Meanwhile, the Signal Timing Manual defines a "phase" as a controller timing unit associated with the control of one or more movements (24). Both manuals use the term "phase pair" to describe a couple of phases that can be activated simultaneously in an actuated signal scheme. In this paper, we use the term "phase" consistent with the usage in the Signal Timing Manual.

## Max Pressure Control-Varaiya's Model

Varaiya $(2,3)$ described the MP signal control policy (referred to hence as non-cyclic MP) as follows.

Considering the weight associated with each movement from link $l$ to link $m$ as the difference between the queue and the average queue from downstream intersections:

$$
w_{l, m}(t)=q_{l, m}(t)-\sum_{n \in O_{m}} p_{m, n}(t) q_{m, n}(t)
$$

The associated pressure for each phase is thus given by:

$$
\gamma_{\sigma}(t)=\sum_{(l, m) \in \sigma} s_{l, m}(t) w_{l, m}(t)
$$

in which $(l, m) \in \sigma$ means that phase $\sigma$ serves movement from $l$ to $m$.

The phase with the maximum pressure is activated in $t+1$ th time slot:

$$
\sigma^{*}(t+1)=\operatorname{argmax}\left\{\gamma_{\sigma}(t) \mid \sigma \in \Pi_{i}\right\}
$$

Equation 3 is a decomposition of the global maximization objective

$$
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}(t+1)=\operatorname{argmax} \sum_{i}^{N}\left\{\gamma_{\sigma}(t) \mid \boldsymbol{\sigma} \in \Pi_{i}\right\}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{*}(t)$ is the vector of the activated phases of all intersections, and $N$ is the total number of intersections. Notice that the phase sequence determined by this rule will always be changeable, which makes settings on cycle length and offsets meaningless.

With regard to queueing process, the store-andforward modeling paradigm (18) is applied to describe it in a discrete manner. Equation 5 describes how the queue length of movement from link $l$ to link $m\left(m \in O_{l}\right)$ progresses from time $t$ to $t+1$. In this equation, $s_{l, m}(t) I_{l, m}^{\sigma}(t) \wedge q_{l, m}(t)$ is equal to the minimum value of $s_{l, m}(t) I_{l, m}^{\sigma}(t)$ and $q_{l, m}(t)$, measuring the maximum number of vehicles that could leave link $l$ to link $m$ under the infinite storage capacity assumption. $\left(\sum_{k \in I_{l}} s_{k, l}(t) I_{k, l}^{\sigma}(t) \wedge q_{k, l}(t)\right) p_{l, m}(t)$ is the endogenous arrivals from upstream of $l . a_{l}$ is the exogenous arrival rates at time $t$, which is known during the controling process. If $l \notin \mathcal{A}_{I}$, then $a_{l}(t)$ is zero for all $t$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_{l, m}(t+1)= & q_{l, m}(t)-s_{l, m}(t) I_{l, m}^{\sigma}(t) \wedge q_{l, m}(t)+a_{l}(t) \\
& +\left(\sum_{k \in I_{l}} s_{k, l}(t) I_{k, l}^{\sigma}(t) \wedge q_{k, l}(t)\right) p_{l, m}(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

MP control policy defined in this way is proved to guarantee maximum throughput. To elaborate, let us introduce the concept of the traffic demand region. Consider that in the controling horizon $T_{0}$, the average demand and turning proportions are defined in Equations 6 and 7:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_{l} & =\frac{1}{T_{0}} \sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}} a_{l}(t) \\
r_{l, m} & =\frac{1}{T_{0}} \sum_{t=1}^{T_{0}} p_{l, m}(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

With the notations listed above, we can express the flow conservation conditions as follows:

$$
f_{i}=d_{i}, i \in \mathcal{A}_{I}
$$

$$
f_{j}=\sum_{i \in I_{j}} r_{i, j} f_{i}, j \in \mathcal{A} \backslash \mathcal{A}_{I}
$$

For the overall network, vector $\mathbf{f}=\left\{f_{l}, f_{m}, f_{n}, \ldots\right\}$ denotes all link flows; demand vector d denotes traffic demands, and the combinations of turning ratios can be defined by a proportion matrix P. We thus have:

$$
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{d P}
$$

For the supply side, define

$$
\Sigma_{l, m}=\frac{1}{T_{0}} \sum_{t=0}^{T_{0}} I_{k, m}^{\sigma}(t)
$$

Evidently, $\Sigma_{l, m}$ is the average amount of time in each time slot that movement $(l, m)$ is activated. The average number of vehicles driven from link $l$ to $m$ thus will be bounded by $\Sigma_{l, m} s_{l, m}$. Consequently, we have

$$
f_{l} r_{l, m} \leq \Sigma_{l, m} s_{l, m}
$$

Let $D$ be the set of demand vectors d that follow conservation Equation 10 and capacity constraint (Equation) 12; clearly, the demand region $D$ is a convex set. Varaiya (2) demonstrated that all queue lengths in the network are bounded under MP if traffic demand is in the demand region $D$. It has been also proved that any demand region larger than $D$ cannot be stabilized by any control policy, meaning that MP achieves the maximum throughput. More detailed proofs and analyses about stability can be found in Varaiya (2) and Tassiulas and Ephremides (5).

## Modifications on Non-Cyclic MP

As demonstrated in the simulation study below, although setting a smaller time interval utilizes the most up-to-date traffic information, it results in frequent phase switches and relative short green durations for each phase. This limitation has also been identified in a previous test experiment on MP using a discrete event simulator, and a modified rule is established to reduce the number of phase switches (25). As a remedy, we propose to introduce a minimum green time to the MP algorithm. To let the control algorithm be aware of the finiteness of the storage capacity, we further employ a normalized queue instead of the absolute queue length in the pressure calculation. The idea of normalized queue is similar to weighted queue proposed by Varaiya (2), but differs in weight calculation. The modified normalized MP is shown as follows. The weight is defined as

$$
w_{l, m}(t)=\frac{q_{l, m}(t)}{L_{l, m} N_{l, m}}-\sum_{n \in O_{m}} \frac{p_{m, n}(t) q_{m, n}(t)}{L_{m, n} N_{m, n}}
$$

## Cyclic Phase Max Pressure Control-Le's Model

The cyclic phase MP control policy (referred to as cyclic phase MP) requires setting fixed cycle lengths, offsets, and phase sequence in the first place. The timing plan is determined by the following procedure: weight associated with each movement and pressure associated with each phase are still determined by Equations 1 and 2. In contrast, green splits for intersection $i$ are given by the following equation:

$$
P_{\sigma}^{i}(t+1)=\frac{\exp \left\{\eta \gamma_{\sigma}(t)\right\}}{\sum_{\psi \in \Phi_{i}} \exp \left\{\eta \gamma_{\psi}(t)\right\}}
$$

Equation 14 yields phase proportions in a cycle, and further determines phase durations when cycle length is known. Here, $\Phi_{i}$ is the predetermined phase sequence and $\eta$ is a parameter to help control the distribution of green time. If $\eta$ is relatively small, the proportion of each group will tend to be uniform, albeit Le et al. (4) did not elaborate how to determine the value of $\eta$. Although this MP scheme provides fixed phase sequence and right of way for each phase in a cycle, tests of its stability property indicate that this control policy cannot achieve the maximum throughput (4, 20, 21).

## Simulation Settings

For the simulation study, we selected a 1.5 -mile-long corridor with 12 signalized intersections located in Gainesville, FL (Figure 1). Each intersection normally has three or four phases. The origin-destination demand matrix was obtained from a field survey conducted in the evening peak hours (5 p.m. to 6 p.m.) in April 2015. The data can be provided to interested readers on request.

The simulation study was operated using Vissim, which allows external programs to control traffic signals in the simulation via the COM Interface. In this study, we mainly used C\# to implement MP control algorithms and interact with the COM Interface. The detailed algorithms are attached in the appendix.

Figure 2 shows the simulation procedure when a COM Interface is involved in the control study. In cyclic phase MP, phase and phase sequence are predetermined. An initial timing plan is provided to the simulator at the beginning to activate the system. Once activated, Vissim transmits the required data to the COM Interface at every customized time step. Static data including network topology, signal controlers, vehicle inputs, and other fixed parameters are transferred only at the beginning of the simulation, while queueing information is updated dynamically. The COM Interface thus performs control algorithms based on those data and outputs signal status to Vissim for the next step's simulation. In simulation, the observation of queueing process and pressure calculation are performed for each lane. For safety concerns, mandatory yellow and red clearance times are also inserted in the simulation settings.

## Simulation Results

This section mainly compares the simulation performance of non-cyclic MP, cyclic phase MP, and a benchmark control strategy-a coordinated actuated signal control scheme implemented through the Ring Barrier Control (RBC) module in Vissim (26). The actuated control scheme adopts timing plans used in practice. However, since the signal control system in the field location is SynchroGreen (27), whose control logic is not available in Vissim, the benchmark simulation study did not replicate the real-world control logic, but used actuated signal control provided in the RBC module. For fair comparisons among different control strategies, basic timing parameters from practice for signal coordination purpose, including cyclic length, offsets, and green splits, are further optimized and adjusted by using TRANSYT-7F before implementation. Parameters in cyclic phase MP are fine-tuned at first by examining different cycle lengths and two types of queueing information. After that, a comparison is made among the cyclic phase MP settings with greatest throughput, non-cyclic MP, and actuated control. The performance of modified MPs is demonstrated at the end.

## Simulation Performance of Cyclic Phase MP

We first determine the cycle length for the corridor. According to the Traffic Signal Timing Manual, two commonly accepted methods are applied (23). The optimal cycle length of the critical intersection, in this case, 162 seconds, is chosen when using the critical intersection method. Since field data implies that flow rates vary significantly among all the intersections, the network approach considering minimum cycles for all intersections is applied; as a result, another cycle length of 90 seconds is considered. Optimal offsets are calculated accordingly. Regarding the fact that time slot is aborted in cyclic phase MP, Le et al. suggested that the queueing information is obtained at the beginning of each cycle, with pressures calculated once per cycle (4). Such an instantaneous measurement of queues greatly reduces communication costs and computation time, but fails to capture the queueing dynamics of the whole cycle. In contrast, calculating average queue lengths based on multiple measurements in a cycle can address this weakness.

Based on variations of cycle lengths and queueing measurements, we conduct four scenarios of cyclic phase MP. The cycle lengths are 162 and 90 seconds, respectively; queue lengths are measured instantaneously and calculated on average, respectively. For scenarios using average queue lengths, queueing information is read from the simulator every 5 seconds. The control parameter $\eta$ is determined via a heuristic approach. The average delay and travel time of four scenarios are compared in Table 1a. Here, cyclic_162i indicates the scenario that applies cyclic phase MP with 162 seconds as cycle length and using the instantaneous queueing lengths for calculation; while cyclic_90a indicates the scenario which applies cyclic MP with 90 seconds as cycle length and average queueing lengths. The simulation period is one simulation hour, and each scenario was run five times due to possible randomness.

Figure 1a represents the re-scaled cumulative vehicle departure curves $N(t)-q_{0}(t)$. For each simulation scenario, $N(t)$ is the network overall cumulative departures up to time $t, q_{0}$ is a background departure rate. A suitable choice of $q_{0}$ is chosen to highlight the visual identification of change on departure rates from re-scaled curves (28). At the beginning of the simulation, the departure rates of all the scenarios are nearly the same. Divergences begin when the number of vehicles in the network increases. Figure $3 b$ shows queued vehicles at each moment. Correspondingly, a scenario with a higher average departure rate generates a smaller number of queued vehicles. For this network, hypothesis tests show that the average throughput under the scenario with 90seconds cycle length and applying average queue lengths (cyclic_90a) is statistically higher than that under other scenarios with a level of significance of 0.01.

## Conclusion

This paper firstly reviewed previous work on MP control policies. A comprehensive simulation study on the Vissim platform was then conducted. To mitigate the instability created by the infinite storage capacity assumption and frequent phase switches, modifications considering the minimum green time and normalized queues, respectively, were introduced to the non-cyclic MP control scheme. Our simulation results showed that non-cyclic MP outperformed cyclic phase MP and both MP control policies performed better than the actuated control scheme which adopts the timing-plans being implemented in the field. Under control by the modified MP scheme with a minimum green time, the average travel time was reduced and the network throughput was significantly improved. The results also showed that MP using normalized queue was more capable of handling relatively larger demands and prevented the network from being blocked in nearly saturated conditions. These results have proved that the modifications on MP are helpful.

Our future work will explicitly consider the physical queueing dynamics and then theoretically compare the performance of MP and MP with normalized queues. Also, since this study mainly focuses on fixed routings, MP control under adaptive routing scenario or dynamic traffic assignment would also be an interesting topic.

    Loop \(T=T+\Delta s\)
        If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=0\)
            \(t=t+1\)
            For each intersection \(i\)
                \(u_{i}^{*}(t)=\operatorname{argmax}\left\{\gamma_{\sigma}(t) \mid \sigma \in \Pi_{i}\right\}\)
                If \(u_{i}^{*}(t) \equiv u_{i}^{*}(t-1)\)
                    \(g_{i}(t)=g_{i}(t-1)+\Delta\)
                Else
                    \(g_{i}(t)=0\)
                    stop green light and activate yellow light for
                    \(u_{i}^{*}(t-1)\)
                End if
            End for
        End If
        If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=a\)
            Activate red light for \(u_{i}^{*}(t)\)
        End If
        If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=a+r\)
            Activate green light for \(u_{i}^{*}(t)\)
        End If
    Until \(T=S T\)
```

Algorithm 2. Cyclic MP

```
Initial settings, \(t=0, T=0\), deciding phase sequence.
For each intersection \(i\)
    Apply one actuated control timing plan
End for
Loop \(T=T+\Delta s\)
    If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=o_{i}\)
        \(t=t+1\)
        For each phase \(k\)
            \(P_{k}^{i}(t)=\frac{\exp \left\{\eta \gamma_{k}(t-1)\right\}}{\sum_{\psi \in \Phi_{i}} \exp \left\{\eta \gamma_{\psi}(t-1)\right\}}\)
        End for
    End if
    If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=o_{i}+C \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} P_{k}^{i}(t)-a-r\)
        Activate yellow light for phase \(\sigma_{k-1}^{i}\)
    End if
    If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=o_{i}+C \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} P_{k}^{i}(t)-a\)
        Activate red light for phase \(\sigma_{k-1}^{i}\)
    End if
    If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=o_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{k-1} P_{k}^{i}(t) C\)
        Activate green light for phase \(\sigma_{k}^{i}\)
    End if
Until \(T=S T\)
```

```
Algorithm 3. MP with minimum green time
    Initial settings, \(t=0, T=0\)
    For each intersection \(i\)
        \(g_{i}(t)=0\)
        \(w_{i}^{*}(t)=\operatorname{argmax}\left\{\gamma_{\sigma}(t) \mid \sigma \in \Pi_{i}\right\}\)
    Loop \(T=T+\Delta s\)
        If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=0\)
            \(t=t+1\)
            For each intersection \(i\)
                \(u_{i}^{*}(t)=\operatorname{argmax}\left\{\gamma_{\sigma}(t) \mid \sigma \in \Pi_{i}\right\}\)
                If \(g_{i}(t)<g_{\text {min }}\)
                    \(g_{i}(t)=g_{i}(t-1)+\Delta\)
                Else
                    If \(u_{i}^{*}(t)=w_{i}^{*}(t-1)\)
                        \(g_{i}(t)=g_{i}(t-1)+\Delta\)
                        \(w_{i}^{*}(t)=w_{i}^{*}(t-1)\)
                    Else
                        \(g_{i}(t)=0\)
                        activate yellow light for \(w_{i}^{*}(t-1)\)
                        \(w_{i}^{*}(t)=u_{i}^{*}(t)\)
                    End if
                End if
            End for
        End If
        If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=a\)
            Activate red light for \(w_{i}^{*}(t-1)\)
        End If
        If \(\bmod (T, \Delta)=a+r\)
            For each intersection \(i\)
                If \(g_{i}(t)=0\)
                    Activate green light for \(w_{i}^{*}(t-1)\)
                End if
            End for
        End If
    Until \(T=S T\)
