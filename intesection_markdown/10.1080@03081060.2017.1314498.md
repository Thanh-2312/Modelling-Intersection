# Pre-timed control for an under-saturated and over-saturated isolated intersection: a Bee Colony Optimization approach 

Aleksandar Jovanović ${ }^{\mathrm{a}}$ and Dušan Teodorović ${ }^{\mathrm{a}, \mathrm{b}}$<br>${ }^{\mathrm{a}}$ Faculty of Transport and Traffic Engineering, University of Belgrade, Belgrade, Serbia; ${ }^{\mathrm{b}}$ Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts, Belgrade, Serbia


#### Abstract

In this paper we study the problem of determining the optimum cycle and phase lengths for isolated signalized intersections. Calculation of the optimal cycle and green phase lengths is based on the minimization of the average control delay experienced by all vehicles that arrive at the intersection within a given time period. We consider under-saturated as well as over-saturated conditions at isolated intersections. The defined traffic signal timing problem, that belongs to the class of combinatorial optimization problems, is solved using the Bee Colony Optimization (BCO) metaheuristic approach. The BCO is a biologically inspired method that explores collective intelligence applied by honey bees during the nectar collecting process. The numerical experiments performed on some examples show that the proposed approach is competitive with other methods. The obtained results show that the proposed approach is capable of generating high-quality solutions within negligible processing times.

## 1. Introduction

Traffic lights, or traffic signals, located at roadway intersections and/or pedestrian crossings, are basic devices used in traffic control. The majority of miles driven in developed countries are driven on signalized roads. An intersection consists of areas necessary for different transportation modes (pedestrians, bicycles, vehicles, and public transit). Control strategies represent the key ingredient for the successful operation of signalized intersections. Traffic control represents a surveillance of the motion of vehicles and pedestrians in order to secure maximum efficiency and safety of conflicting traffic movements. Adequately designed and located traffic signals provide well-ordered traffic movements, increased intersection capacity, provide progression for traffic, decrease the number of crashes at the intersection, improve fuel consumption, and decrease vehicle emissions. On the other hand, improper traffic signal timing could increase vehicle delays, number of vehicle stops, travel costs, air pollution, noise level, and alter the network traffic assignment.

The greatest number of existing signals in many cities in the world could be, without difficulty, upgraded. Traffic intersection control problems are the most significant
problems faced by traffic authorities. These traffic engineering problems belong to the class of difficult combinatorial optimization problems. Various optimization concepts and techniques have been developed in the past in order to create better signal performance. The widely used objective functions are total vehicle delay, total number of vehicle stops, a linear combination of these quantities, etc. Various commercial software packages (such as Synchro, PASSER V, TRANSYT, Highway Capacity Software (HCS) Signals) have also been developed with an idea to optimize intersection timing plans.

In this paper, a model that optimizes the performance of isolated intersections has been developed. The proposed model determines the optimal cycle length and phase split. The average control delay experienced by all vehicles that arrive at the intersection within a given time period represents the objective function that we seek to minimize.

Our approach is based on Bee Colony Optimization (BCO) metaheuristics. The BCO algorithm is a stochastic, random search technique that belongs to the class of population-based algorithms. This technique uses an analogy in which bees search for food, and the way in which optimization algorithms search for an optimum of a combinatorial optimization problem. We propose an artificial system composed of a number of precisely defined agents (artificial bees). In other words, we perform a multi-agent simulation. We specify behavior rules of artificial bees and simulate the interaction between them (the way in which artificial bees communicate with each other). The numerical experiments are performed on the problems generated by the authors of the paper. We show clearly that the proposed approach is competitive with other approaches in the literature, and that it can generate high-quality solutions within negligible processing times.

The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 contains a statement of the problem; a proposed solution is given in Section 3; the BCO approach to the isolated intersection control problem is explained in detail in Section 4; the experimental evaluation of the proposed approach is given in Section 5; and conclusions and recommendations for future research are provided in Section 6.

## 2. Statement of the problem

We consider the pre-timed (fixed time) operations at an intersection, using the terms 'pretimed' and 'fixed time' interchangeably. Drivers move toward the intersection from different approaches. Typical crossroad intersections are composed of a number of approaches and crossing area, illustrated in Figure 1.

Each approach can have one or more lanes. During the green time, vehicles from the observed approach can leave the stop line and cross the intersection. Many isolated intersections operate under fixed-time control strategies. These pre-timed strategies assume existence of the signal cycle that represents one execution of the basic sequence of signal combinations at an intersection. ## 3. Mathematical formulation of the problem

Let us introduce the following symbols: $F$, set of chosen phases; $K$, set of all lanes at the intersection; $i$, lane index, $i=1,2, \ldots,|K| ; j$, phase index, $j=1,2, \ldots,|F| ; q_{i}$, flow in the $i$ th lane $(\mathrm{veh} / \mathrm{h}) ; s_{i}$, saturation flow in the $i$ th lane $(\mathrm{veh} / \mathrm{h}) ; g_{i}$, green time length in the $i$ th lane $(\mathrm{s}) ; g_{j}$, green time length in the $j$ th phase $(\mathrm{s}) ; X_{i}$, volume-to-capacity ratio in the $i$ th lane; $c_{i}$, capacity of the $i$ th lane $(\operatorname{veh} / \mathrm{h}) ; d_{i}$, average control delay per vehicle in the $i$ th lane (s/veh); $T$, analysis period duration (h); $C$, cycle length (s); $L$, cycle lost time (s); $D$, the total average delay for all vehicles at the intersection during the analysis period (s); $g_{\text {min }}$, minimum green time $(\mathrm{s}) ; g_{\text {max }}$, maximum green time (s); $C_{\text {min }}$, minimum allowed cycle length (s); $C_{\text {max }}$, maximum allowed cycle length (s);

$$

$$

$Y_{j}$, sum of all flow to saturation flow ratios (for all lanes that belong to the $j$ th phase); $d_{1 i}$, uniform delay per vehicle in the $i$ th lane $(\mathrm{s} / \mathrm{veh}) ; d_{2 i}$, incremental delay per vehicle in the $i$ th lane (s/veh); $d_{3 i}$, initial queue delay per vehicle in the $i$ th lane (s/veh); $d_{1 i}^{*}$, uniform delay in the case where there is an initial queue in the $i$ th lane $(\mathrm{s} / \mathrm{veh}) ; d_{u i}$, saturated delay in the $i$ th lane (s/veh); $d_{s i}$, under-saturated delay in the $i$ th lane (s/veh); $S_{i}$, opposed saturation flow rate (veh/h); $q_{o}$, opposing flow $($ veh $/ \mathrm{h}) ; t_{c}$, critical gap $(\mathrm{s}) ; t_{f}$, follow-up time (s); $b$, period index; $Q_{b i}$, initial queue at the start of $b$ th period $T$ in the $i$ th lane (veh); $t_{i}$, duration of unmet demand in $T$ in the $i$ th lane (h); and $u_{i}$, delay parameter in the $i$ th lane.

The capacity $c_{i}$ of the $i$ th lane group equals:

$$
c_{i}=s_{i} \frac{g_{i}}{C} .
$$

We consider a random supply and demand for gaps when considering gap acceptance for the opposed flow. The opposed saturation flow rate (only for permitted left turns) equals:

$$

$$
X_{i}=\frac{q_{i}}{c_{i}}=\frac{q_{i}}{s_{i}\left(g_{i} / C\right)}=\frac{q_{i} / s_{i}}{g_{i} / C} .
$$

### 3.1. Mathematical formulation of the traffic signal timing problem in the case of under-saturated flows

We want to determine the optimal cycle length and the green phase lengths in such a way to minimize the average control delay experienced by all vehicles that arrive at the intersection within a given time period. According to the U.S. Highway Capacity Manual (TRB 2010), the control delay equals:

$$
\begin{gathered}
d_{i}=d_{1 i}+d_{2 i}, \\
d_{1 i}=\frac{0.5 \cdot C \cdot\left(1-\left(g_{i} / C\right)\right)^{2}}{1-\left[\min \left(1, X_{i}\right) \cdot\left(g_{i} / C\right)\right]}, \\
d_{2 i}=900 T\left[\left(X_{i}-1\right)+\sqrt{\left(X_{i}-1\right)^{2}+\frac{4 \cdot X_{i}}{c_{i} T}}\right] .
\end{gathered}
$$

The mathematical formulation of the traffic signal timing problem in the case of undersaturated flows reads:
Minimize

$$
D=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{|K|} q_{i} \cdot d_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{|K|} q_{i}},
$$

subject to:

$$
\begin{gathered}
C_{\min } \leq C \leq C_{\max } \\
g_{\min } \leq g_{j} \leq g_{\max }, \quad \forall j \in F \\
\sum_{j=1}^{|F|} g_{j}=C-L
\end{gathered}
$$

The objective function (7) to be minimized represents the average control delay experienced by all vehicles that arrive at the intersection within a given time period. Constraint (8) defines the interval of the feasible cycle length values and constraint (9) defines the interval of the feasible green time length values. The relationship between cycle length, green time lengths and lost time is described by constraint (10).

### 3.2. Mathematical formulation of the traffic signal timing problem in the case of over-saturated flows

Our mathematical formulation of the traffic signal timing problem in the case of undersaturated flows is based on the hypothesis that there is no initial queue at the beginning of the analysis period. In cases when, in the $i$ th lane, $X_{i}>1$, within time period $b$, (usually 15 min period), the next period ( $b+1$ st period) begins with an initial queue of $Q_{b i}$ vehicles. An initial queue causes an additional delay $d_{3 i}$ for the vehicles that arrive within the analysis period. An additional delay $d_{3 i}$ depends on the number of vehicles $Q_{b i}$ in the initial queue, analysis period $T$, and the $X_{i}$ value.

The following three cases are possible. In the first case, there is no initial queue after the time period $b\left(Q_{b i+1}=0\right)$. This case will happen when the sum of the $Q_{b i}$ 's and the total demand during the time period $b$ is less than available capacity. The second case happens when the queue at the beginning of the period $b+1$ is less than the queue at the beginning of the period $b\left(Q_{b i+1} \leq Q_{b i}\right)$. Finally, the third case is related to the situation when the queue at the beginning of the period $b+1$ is greater than the queue at the beginning of the period $b\left(Q_{b i+1}>Q_{b i}\right)$. The initial queue $Q_{b i+1}$ equals (TRB 2010):

$$
Q_{b+1 i}=\max \left[0, Q_{b i}+c_{i} \cdot T \cdot\left(X_{i}-1\right)\right] .
$$

The additional delay $d_{3 i}$ equals (TRB 2010):

$$
d_{3 i}=\frac{1800 \cdot Q_{b i} \cdot\left(1+u_{i}\right) \cdot t_{i}}{c_{i} \cdot T},
$$

where $Q_{b i}$, initial queue (veh); $c_{i}$, capacity of the $i$ th lane $($ veh $/ \mathrm{h}) ; T$, duration of analysis period (h); $t_{i}$, duration of unmet demand; and $u$, delay parameter.

The duration of unmet $t_{i}$ demand equals:

$$
t_{i}=\min \left\{T, \frac{Q_{b i}}{c_{i} \cdot\left[1-\min \left(1, X_{i}\right)\right]}\right\},
$$

if $t_{i}<T$ then $u_{i}=0$; else

$$
u_{i}=1-\frac{c_{i} \cdot T \cdot\left[1-\min \left(1, X_{i}\right)\right]}{Q_{b i}} .
$$

The uniform delay, in the case of the existing initial queue in the $i$ th lane, equals:

$$
d_{1 i}^{*}=d_{s i} \cdot \frac{t_{i}}{T}+d_{u i} \cdot \frac{\left(T-t_{i}\right)}{T},
$$

where

$$
\begin{gathered}
d_{s i}=0.5 \cdot C \cdot\left(1-\frac{g_{i}}{C}\right), \\
d_{u i}=d_{1 i} .
\end{gathered}
$$

## 4. Proposed solution to the problem

The isolated intersection control problem is one of the fundamental problems in the area of traffic engineering and control. Due to its hardness and importance, it has continuously been a test for researchers who approached it from a variety of perspectives. Webster (1958) proposed one of the first traffic signals models. Webster's model determines the cycle time, as well as the green splits for the single isolated intersection. During the last five decades, numerous researchers have studied the isolated intersection control problem. Total vehicle delay, the total number of stopped vehicles and total throughput represent the usual objective functions to be optimized (cf. Allsop 1971, 1972; Yagar 1974, 1975, 1977; Pappis and Mamdani 1977; Allsop and Murchland 1978; Improta 1978; Zuzarte and Murchland 1978; Improta and Cantarella 1982, 1984; Chang 1985; Saka 1986; Kronborg and Davidsson 1993; Trabia, Kaseko, and Ande 1999; Dion and Hellinga 2002; Jacques, Niitymaki, and Pursula 2002; Lan 2004; Murat and Gedizlioglu 2005; Murat 2006; Yang et al. 2006; Nair and Cai 2007; Wunderlich et al. 2008; Renfrew and Yu 2009, 2012; Yang, Ma, and Zeng 2009; Zarandi and Rezapour 2009; Soh and Sarkan 2010; Zhang and Wang 2011). Various aspects of the isolated intersection control problem, in the case of over-saturated flows, were studied by Han (1996), Park, Messer, and Urbanik (1999), Chang and Lin (2000), Dion, Rakha, and Kang (2004) and Zhao et al. (2011).

The approach to the isolated intersection control problem, proposed in this paper, is based on BCO. BCO represents one of the Swarm Intelligence techniques (Beni 1988; Beni and Wang 1989; Beni and Hackwood 1992; Bonabeau, Dorigo, and Theraulaz 1997) and is part of Artificial Intelligence based on the study of actions of individuals in different decentralized systems. These decentralized systems are composed of physical individuals (robots, for example) or artificial ones that communicate among themselves, cooperate, collaborate, exchange information and knowledge and perform some tasks in their environment (Teodorović 2008).

The BCO metaheuristic has been introduced by Lučić and Teodorović (2001, 2002, 2003a, 2003b). BCO has been successfully applied to various engineering and management problems (cf. Teodorović and Dell'Orco 2005, 2008; Šelmić, Teodorović, and Vukadinović 2010; Davidović et al. 2011, 2012; Teodorović, Šelmić, and Mijatović-Teodorović 2013; Todorović and Petrović 2013). Nikolić and Teodorović (2013) performed an empirical study of the BCO algorithm, applying it to optimize numerous numerical test functions. They compared the obtained results with results in the literature, and showed that the BCO is competitive with other methods.

BCO is motivated by bees' behavior in nature. The artificial bees apply the rules used by honey bees for the phase of the nectar collection process. The essential idea behind BCO is to design a colony of artificial bees able to effectively solve difficult combinatorial optimization problems. Artificial bees investigate through the search space, seeking for the feasible solutions. Every artificial bee creates one solution to the problem. In order to discover good solutions, artificial bees collaborate and exchange information. By the use of collective knowledge and sharing information among themselves, artificial bees concentrate on
more promising areas, and step by step reject solutions from the less promising ones. Artificial bees together generate and/or improve their solutions.

The BCO algorithm consists of two alternating phases: forward pass and backward pass. In each forward pass, every artificial bee explores the search space. It applies a predefined number of moves, which construct and/or improve the solution, yielding a new solution. Having obtained new solutions, the bees go again to the nest and start the second phase, the so-called backward pass. In the backward pass, all artificial bees share information about their solutions. In other words, bees make known the quality of the solution, i.e. the objective function value. Through the backward pass, every bee decides with a certain probability whether to abandon the created solution and become an uncommitted follower, or dance and thus recruit nest mates before returning to the created solution (bees with a higher objective function value have a greater chance to continue their own exploration). Every follower chooses a new solution from recruiters (see Figure 6) by the roulette wheel method (better solutions have a higher probability of being chosen for exploration). The two phases of the search algorithm, forward and backward pass, are performed iteratively, until a stopping condition is met. The possible stopping conditions could be, for example, the maximum total number of forward/backward passes, the maximum total number of forward/backward passes without any improvement in the objective function, etc.

The BCO algorithm could be of the constructive or improving type. The constructive approach starts from the scratch (Lučić and Teodorović 2001, 2002, 2003a, 2003b; Teodorović 2003, 2008, 2009; Davidović, Šelmić, Teodorović 2009; Davidović et al. 2011, 2012; Šelmić, Teodorović, and Vukadinović 2010; Dimitrijević et al. 2011). Within this approach the analyst constructs a solution step by step. Quite the opposite, the improving approach begins from a complete solution. The complete solution (possibly a feasible one) is typically generated randomly or by some heuristic. By perturbing that solution, attempts are made to improve it (Davidović et al. 2011; Nikolić and Teodorović 2013; Todorović and Petrović 2013).

In this paper, we apply the improvement version of the BCO algorithm whose parameter values need to be set prior to the algorithm execution, as follows: $B$, the number of bees involved in the search; IT, the number of iterations; $N P$, the number of forward and backward passes in a single iteration; $N C$, the number of changes in one forward pass; and $S$, the best known solution. ## 5. The BCO approach to the isolated intersection control problem

In this paper, the authors propose the BCO algorithm tailored for the isolated intersection control problem based on the improvement concept. The initial feasible solution (the initial signal plan) is first generated. Then, artificial bees investigate the solution space in the neighborhood of the current solution, and try to improve the solution. The modification of the solution is performed through $N P$ forward passes within a single iteration. The authors assume that at the beginning of signal plan construction, all artificial bees are in the hive. The hive is an artificial location.

### 5.1. Generating the initial solution

The initial solution is generated by determining the initial cycle length. The initial cycle length is chosen from the prescribed interval in a random manner. In the next step, we subtract from this value the cycle lost time $L$, and allocate the remaining time period among competitive phases. The green times are allocated to the phases in the initial solution proportionally to the $Y_{j}$ values, i.e.:

$$
Y_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{|K|} \alpha_{i j} \frac{q_{i}}{s_{i}}, \quad \forall j \in F .
$$

The solution generated in this way represents the initial solution at the beginning of each iteration that should be modified.

### 5.2. Solution modification

The algorithm for the initial solution modifications consists of the following steps:
Step 1: Generate the initial solution. Denote by $C$ the cycle length of the initial solution. Step 2: Choose in a random manner the new cycle length $C_{n}$ from the interval:

$$
C_{\min } \leq C_{n} \leq C_{\max } .
$$

Step 3: Calculate $\Delta C=C_{n}-C$.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/7b36715c-a1c5-4c26-9888-4b85351a2937-11.jpg?height=364&width=1264&top_left_y=183&top_left_x=223)
Figure 3. Solution modification in the case when $\Delta C>0$.

If $\Delta C>0$ (the new cycle length $C_{n}$ is greater than the initial cycle length $C$ ) add additional $\Delta C$ seconds of green time to phase lengths by using the random number generator and relation (20). If $\Delta C<0$ (the new cycle length $C_{n}$ is smaller than the initial cycle length $C$ ) subtract $\Delta C$ seconds of green time from the phase lengths by using the random number generator and relation (21). If $\Delta C=0$, do not change phase lengths.

The probability $P_{j}$ of adding the $\Delta C$ seconds to the $j$ th phase equals (Figure 3):

$$
P_{j}=\frac{Y_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|F|} Y_{j}}, \quad j \in F .
$$

The probability $Q_{j}$ of subtracting the $\Delta C$ seconds to the $j$ th phase equals:

$$
Q_{j}=1-\frac{Y_{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|F|} Y_{j}}, \quad j \in F .
$$

The bigger the ordinary number of the forward pass, the higher the impact of the previously discovered solution. This is expressed by the term $U$ in the denominator of the exponent (relation (23)). In other words, at the start of the search process bees are 'more brave' to explore the solution space. The more forward passes they make, the 'less bravely' the bees explore the solution space. The more we are approaching the end of the search process, the bees are paying more attention to the already discovered solutions.

By using relation (23) and a random number generator, every artificial bee decides to become either a uncommitted follower or to continue exploration from the generated solution (Figure 6).

### 5.4. Recruiting process

In the situation when at the start of a new stage a bee does not like to begin from the earlier generated solution, the bee will go to the dancing area and will follow another bee (Figure 7). Within the dance area the bee-dancers (recruiters) 'advertise' different solutions. It is assumed that the probability recruiter $b$ 's solution will be chosen by any uncommitted bee equals:

$$
P_{b}=\frac{O_{b}}{\sum_{k=1}^{R C} O_{k}}, \quad b=1,2, \ldots, R C
$$

where $O_{k}$ is the normalized objective function value of the $k$ th advertised solution and $R C$ the number of recruiters.

By using relation (24) and a random number generator, every uncommitted follower joins one bee dancer (recruiter). The recruiter's solution is copied to a solution of uncommitted follower. At this moment, all bees are free to autonomously explore the solution space all over again. The recruiter's solution becomes a follower's one, and after that, bees apply different modification steps to the same solution. An iteration completes when forward and backward passes are performed $N P$ times. Best among all solutions is identified and used to update the global best one. The $B C O$ runs iteration by iteration until a stopping condition is met.

The stopping criteria that includes information about the state of the optimization run is used in this paper. If a solution that is better than the best known solution cannot be discovered during IT iterations, the search process stops. The number of iterations IT is given in advance by the analyst.

## 6. Numerical example

The BCO approach proposed here is compared with other approaches from the literature. In the proposed BCO algorithm we set $B=15, N P=15, N C=1$. These values are chosen based on our prior experience.

The proposed BCO model was tested in the case of three different scenarios. The first scenario is related to one analyzed time period in under-saturated flow conditions. We analyzed two time periods in the case of the second scenario. In this case, the traffic flows are higher in the first considered time period than in the second one. The third considered scenario also has two time periods. In the case of this third scenario, the traffic flows are higher in the second considered time period than in the first one.

We did not optimize the number of phases at the intersection. In other words, the number of phases was treated in this paper as an input parameter given by the analyst. We analyzed the cases when the total number of phases is respectively equal to $2,3,4$, 5, or 6 phases. These numbers of phases were considered in all three scenarios.

Cycle lost time $L$ (in seconds) depends on the number of phases. Table 2 shows the cycle lost time values that were used in the calculation.

The minimum allowed cycle length $C_{\text {min }}$ and the maximum allowed cycle length $C_{\text {max }}$ were respectively equal to 30 and 120 s, while the minimum value of the green time $g_{\min }$ and the maximum value of the green time $g_{\text {max }}$ were respectively equal to 7 and 80 s.
