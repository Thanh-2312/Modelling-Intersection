#### Abstract

Compared with a conventional intersection, a signal-free and lane-free intersection enables the vehicles to drive more flexibly, thereby to promote the throughput and to reduce congestions. This work proposes a fault-tolerant cooperative motion planning method for a number of connected and automated vehicles (CAVs) which are passing through a signal-free and lane-free intersection. Partial loss of actuator effectiveness is considered as the fault type. Even a subtle fault may significantly affect the overall team performance. Thus correctly judging whether each faulty/healthy CAV can still achieve its original goal is impossible until a corresponding motion replanning problem turns out to be solvable or not. To maximize the completeness of the original task, we propose a parallel computation framework, wherein the faultrecovery motion replanning problems in multiple levels of task completeness are tried simultaneously. Concretely, i) each faulty/healthy CAV should accomplish its original goal; ii) only each healthy CAV is required to accomplish its original goal; and iii) all the faulty/healthy CAVs try to stop safely without the need to achieve any original goals. Problems iii), ii), and i) are ranking with increasing computational difficulty. Among all the three problems, the achievable solution to the most difficult one is implemented as the fault-recovery strategy.

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Intersection management plays a key role in the development of intelligent transportation systems, and is undergoing revolutionary changes brought by connected and automated Vehicles (CAVs) (Ni et al., 2017; Lee and Park, 2012). However, the benefit of using CAVs in promoting the intersection capacity is far from being well exploited, because i) the conventional traffic laws which were enacted to regularize human drivers are now preventing the CAVs from showing their full abilities (Sun et al., 2017); and ii) the cooperation potential in a multi-vehicle team is not maximized due to the weak motion planning capabilities (Li et al., 2017a).

This study aims to address the two limitations mentioned above. Regarding the first issue, the conventional traffic rules related to the signals and lanes are re-defined. Concretely, the lane keeping regulations for the vehicles approaching an intersection are removed. Through this, the vehicles can drive more flexibly, thereby promoting the travel efficiency. The left-turn vehicles may even temporarily utilize the road in its opposite direction if such a trajectory is really beneficial and safe. The removal of traffic lights is due to the same purpose. Simplified versions of this idea have been proposed in (Zhang et al., 2016; Xuan et al., 2011; Dresner and Stone, 2008; Goldblatt et al., 1994), but they are not as generic as this work. To address the second issue, a model-based centralized problem formulation is adopted to describe the concerned multi-vehicle motion planning scheme. Compared to the predominant decentralized motion planning formulations which are incorporated with priority, coordination, and/or task division based logics (Demesure et al., 2017; Ma et al., 2017; Luo et al., 2016), a centralized one can preserve the possibility to find complete and optimal solutions. Therefore, this work pursues for the optimal cooperative motions at a signal-free and lane-free intersection.

Compared to a single-vehicle system, a multi-vehicle one is far more complicated, thus faults happen at a relatively large probability. Faults in a multi-vehicle system may degrade the overall performance or even cause catastrophic disasters. Besides that, faults are difficult to foresee in general. Therefore, it deserves to investigate how a system can operate safely in the presence of such faults (Zhang and Jiang, 2008). Faulttolerant cooperative control (FTCC) has been viewed as an efficient way to handle the unexpected faults in a multi-robot system. Chen and Song (2015) proposed an active faulttolerant robust control approach for the multi-agent synchronization and tracking problems. Arrichiello et al. (2014) considered excluding the faulty vehicle from the team, and then making the centroid of the new formation follow a reference trajectory. In contrast, Hua et al. (2017) aimed to complete the original task without exclusion of the faulty vehicle. Zhou et al. (2014) focused on the leader-follower consensus tracking problem using locally available information. A similar problem was handled in Yang and Yue (2017), which utilized neural networks to approximate the time-varying dynamics of the followers. In handling the partial
failures in a robot team, Kamel et al. (2017) introduced a threshold to judge whether to exclude the faulty robot: if not, the rest healthy robots are required to work with degraded performances, aiming to keep consistent with the faulty robot.

The predominant methods mentioned above share some common limitations. Firstly, the existing fault-tolerant cooperative control methods are inherently just fault-tolerant simultaneous control methods. Since the interactions among the robots are not completely incorporated in the controller models, some harsh problems which largely rely on the cooperation skills cannot be handled. In fact, the existing FTCC methods are limited to solving tracking problems, and/or highly explicit formation reconfiguration problems. Secondly, how to utilize the partially faulty robot's capability is not considered. At this point, whether to exclude the faulty robot from the team, and how the faulty robot should co-work with the rest healthy robots are determined by manual logics, which, in most cases, are not good choices. As a summary, the decision making capabilities in the existing FTCC methods deserve improvement.

Through proposing a new fault-tolerant motion planning method for multiple CAVs, this paper aims to address the aforementioned drawbacks. Highlights lie in the following aspects. Firstly, generic multi-vehicle motion planning schemes are considered. Instead of simple tracking or explicit formation reconfiguration problems, this work considers generic tasks with implicit and complicated constraints and conditions. Secondly, a centralized cooperative motion planning formulation is adopted, which enables to thoroughly utilize the collaboration talents in a multi-vehicle team. Thirdly, a parallel computation framework is developed to provide fault-recovery solutions with suitable levels of task completeness. Through this, the impact of the faulty vehicle on the whole team is determined and treated in an automated way.

## 2. PRELIMINARIES AND FAULT-FREE PROBLEM DESCRIPTION

This section defines the normal cooperative motion planning scheme at a signal-free and lane-free intersection without any faults. The concerned scheme is formulated as a nonlinear optimal control problem. This fault-free formulation is a preparation for the faulty case introduced in Section 3.

### 2.1 CAV Kinematics

Suppose there are $\mathrm{N}_{\mathrm{V}}$ CAVs. A kinematic bicycle model is adopted to describe the driving principle of each CAV (Moon et al., 2017; Li et al., 2016). The kinematics of vehicle $i$ $\left(i=1, \ldots, \mathrm{~N}_{\mathrm{V}}\right)$ is formulated as

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{~d} t}\left[\begin{array}{c}
x_{i}(t) \\
y_{i}(t) \\
\theta_{i}(t) \\
v_{i}(t) \\
\phi_{i}(t)
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}
v_{i}(t) \cdot \cos \theta_{i}(t) \\
v_{i}(t) \cdot \sin \theta_{i}(t) \\
v_{i}(t) \cdot \tan \phi_{i}(t) / \mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{w}} \\
a_{i}(t) \\
\omega_{i}(t)
\end{array}\right], t \in\left[0, t_{\mathrm{f}}\right]
$$

where $t_{\mathrm{f}}$ stands for the task completion time (not fixed), $\left(x_{i}(t), y_{i}(t)\right)$ locates the midpoint along the rear-wheel axis of CAV $i, v_{i}(t)$ and $a_{i}(t)$ respectively denote the velocity and acceleration of that point, $\phi_{i}(t)$ represents the steering angle, $\omega_{i}(t)$ refers to the angular velocity of $\phi_{i}(t), \theta_{i}(t)$ stands for the orientation angle of the vehicle, and $\mathrm{L}_{\mathrm{w}}$ denotes the distance between the front and rear wheel axes (Fig. 1).

Algebraic constraints include

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \left|a_{i}(t)\right| \leq \mathrm{a}_{\max } \\
& 0 \leq v_{i}(t) \leq \mathrm{v}_{\max } \\
& \left|\phi_{i}(t)\right| \leq \Phi_{\max } \\
& \left|\omega_{i}(t)\right| \leq \Omega_{\max }, t \in\left[0, t_{\mathrm{f}}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

Herein, $\mathrm{a}_{\text {max }}, \mathrm{v}_{\text {max }}, \Phi_{\text {max }}$, and $\Omega_{\text {max }}$ denote the upper bounds on $\left|a_{i}(t)\right|, v_{i}(t),\left|\phi_{i}(t)\right|$, and $\left|\omega_{i}(t)\right|$, respectively. Note that $\mathrm{a}_{\text {max }}$ and $\Omega_{\text {max }}$ are related to passenger comfort, but not the mechanical limitations of the vehicle.

Without consideration of exterior restriction, Eqs. (1) and (2) define how each CAV moves in the free space.

### 2.2 Intersection Scenario

Apart from the aforementioned kinematic constraints, scenario related constraints also have influences on the driving behaviours of the vehicles. The scenario related constraints consist of the collision avoidance constraints and region specification constraints. In more details, the CAVs should not collide with each other, or collide to the road barriers. Also, each CAV should run within a specified region at the intersection. These constraints are formulated as follows.

Suppose that each CAV is rectangular. The analytical description of the rectangle-to-rectangle overlap avoidance condition is intricate. Thus instead, a circumcircle is adopted to cover the rectangular region of each vehicle. As depicted in Fig. 1, the circle centre $\left(x c_{i}, y c_{i}\right)$ locates at the geometric centre of vehicle $i$, and then the radius $\mathrm{R}_{i}$ is uniformly determined. This simplification has practical meanings if the redundant half-moon regions are regarded as safety oriented margins. With such simplification, collisions among the vehicles are avoided if

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \left(x c_{i}(t)-x c_{j}(t)\right)^{2}+\left(y c_{i}(t)-y c_{j}(t)\right)^{2} \geq\left(\mathrm{R}_{i}+\mathrm{R}_{j}\right)^{2} \\
& \forall i, j=1, \ldots, \mathrm{~N}_{\mathrm{V}}, i \neq j, t \in\left[0, t_{\mathrm{f}}\right] .
\end{aligned}
$$

Besides that, the circular CAVs should not collide with the rectangular street blocks. Collision avoidance between a circle and a rectangle is almost the same as restricting the circle centre out of an expended rectangle (Fig. 2). Then we can adopt the triangle area based criterion in Li and Shao (2015) to formulate the vehicle-to-block collision avoidance constrains.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/e8a2763f-8185-48e4-8f5c-3db21cec4ba7-3.jpg?height=338&width=840&top_left_y=472&top_left_x=159)
Fig. 2. Schematics on the vehicle-to-block overlap avoidance constraints. Note that if the circle centre is out of the rectangle region on the right, then it is sufficient that the circle does not collide with the rectangle on the left either.

Each CAV enters the intersection from one of the four directions and exits in one of the three ways (namely turning left, going straight, and turning right). Then all of the CAVs can be classified into 12 categories, namely A1, A2, A3, B1, B2,..., D3 as shown in Fig. 3(a). A1, B1, C1, and D1 are index sets recording the index of each left-turn CAV; A2, B2, C2, and D2 represent the CAVs which plan to go straight; and A3, B3, C3, and D3 represent the right-turn ones.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/e8a2763f-8185-48e4-8f5c-3db21cec4ba7-3.jpg?height=508&width=868&top_left_y=1348&top_left_x=144)
Fig. 3. Schematics on the intersection scenario: (a) notations of CAV categories; and (b) accessible zones of left-turn and right-turn vehicles.

The accessible zone of each CAV type is defined as follows. For the convenience of presentation, an $x-y$ coordinate is established such that the $x$ and $y$ axes respectively stand for the central lines of the intersection (Fig. 3(b)). For the right-turn vehicles, the admissible zones are outlined by the $x$ and $y$ axes, together with the block barriers. An example is highlighted in Fig. 3(b) for the vehicles in B3. For the through vehicles, the tube-like admissible zones are outlined by one axis and the road barrier. For example, the CAVs in C2 run are restricted to run between $y=0$ and $y=\mathrm{RW}$. Regarding the left-turn vehicles, Fig. 3(b) utilizes A1 as an example to illustrate our way to define their accessible zones. It is notable that the A1 accessible zone completely covers the D3 accessible zone. This idea enables the left-turn vehicles to drive with more flexibility (see the sinuous left-turn path in Fig. 3(b)). It appears crazy from a conventional traffic viewpoint, but a naive version of this idea has been reported in Goldblatt et al. (1994), which efficiently tackles the heavy left-turn traffic flow.

Requesting a circular CAV to stay to one side of a specified line is simple, because the involved lines in the $x-y$ coordinate are either vertical or horizontal. For example, "CAV $i$ stays to the left side of $x=0$ " is identical to $x c_{i}(t)+\mathrm{R}_{i} \leq 0$.

### 2.3 Cooperative Motion Planning Task

The preceding subsections have defined the kinematics and scenario related constraints. There are also task related constraints and an optimization objective, which are introduced as follows.

Since $t=0$, all the vehicles are taken over by the centralized intersection management system. For vehicle $i,\left[x_{i}(0), y_{i}(0)\right]$ is obtained by the sensors. The rest of the configuration variables are particularly required to satisfy

$$
\left[v_{i}(0), a_{i}(0), \phi_{i}(0), \omega_{i}(0)\right]=\left[\mathrm{v}_{0}, 0,0,0\right], i=1,2, \ldots, \mathrm{~N}_{\mathrm{V}},
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \theta_{i}(0)=0, i \in \mathrm{~A} 1 \bigcup \mathrm{~A} 2 \bigcup \mathrm{~A} 3 ; \\
& \theta_{i}(0)=\pi / 2, i \in \mathrm{~B} 1 \bigcup \mathrm{~B} 2 \bigcup \mathrm{~B} 3 ; \\
& \theta_{i}(0)=\pi, i \in \mathrm{C} 1 \bigcup \mathrm{C} 2 \bigcup \mathrm{C} 3 ; \\
& \theta_{i}(0)=-\pi / 2, i \in \mathrm{D} 1 \bigcup \mathrm{D} 2 \bigcup \mathrm{D} 3 .
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mathrm{v}_{0}$ is a user-specified velocity constant. Eq. (4) can avoid the extreme cases in which two vehicles will definitely collide when $t>0$, although no collision happens at $t=0$. Let us consider a simple case: at $t=0$, two vehicles are driving forward in a line; the former vehicle's acceleration at $t=0$ is 0 , and the latter vehicle's acceleration is positive; both vehicles will collide right after $t=0$ if their gap at $t=0$ is sufficiently small. The entire motion process will begin with a rational state with Eq. (4). Due to the same reason, the terminal configuration of each CAV is required as

$$
\left[v_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right), a_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right), \phi_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right), \omega_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)\right]=\left[\mathrm{v}_{0}, 0,0,0\right], i=1,2, \ldots, \mathrm{~N}_{\mathrm{V}},
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \theta_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)=\pi / 2, i \in \mathrm{~A} 1 \cup \mathrm{~B} 2 \bigcup \mathrm{C} 3 ; \\
& \theta_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)=\pi, i \in \mathrm{~B} 1 \bigcup \mathrm{C} 2 \bigcup \mathrm{D} 3 ; \\
& \theta_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)=-\pi / 2, i \in \mathrm{~A} 3 \bigcup \mathrm{C} 1 \bigcup \mathrm{D} 2 ; \\
& \theta_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)=0, i \in \mathrm{~A} 2 \bigcup \mathrm{~B} 3 \bigcup \mathrm{D} 1 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Besides that, each vehicle is required to enter a specified region at the terminal moment $t_{\mathrm{f}}$. For example, the CAVs in group A1UB2UC3 should stay to the right side of $x=0$ and stay above $y=\mathrm{RW}$ (see the red region in Fig. 4):

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
x c_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)-\mathrm{R}_{i} \geq 0 \\
y c_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)-\mathrm{R}_{i} \geq \mathrm{RW}
\end{array}, i \in \mathrm{~A} 1 \cup \mathrm{~B} 2 \cup \mathrm{C} 3 .\right.
$$

Satisfaction to such terminal conditions means a perfect end of the left-turn process for the vehicles. In the remaining of this paper, we denote the terminal region and terminal orientation related conditions as the "goal" of a CAV.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/e8a2763f-8185-48e4-8f5c-3db21cec4ba7-4.jpg?height=577&width=872&top_left_y=215&top_left_x=142)
Fig. 4. Schematics on the terminal regions of the vehicles.

This study considers a time-distance objective defined in Eq. (6), which penalizes large completion time, and encourages the CAVs to go farther in their terminal orientations:
minimize :

$$
\begin{aligned}
t_{\mathrm{f}}-\lambda \cdot & \left(\sum_{i \in \mathrm{Al} \cup \mathrm{~B} 2 \cup \mathrm{C} 3} y_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)+\sum_{i \in \mathrm{~A} 2 \cup \mathrm{~B} 3 \cup \mathrm{D} 1} x_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)\right. \\
& \left.-\sum_{i \in \mathrm{~A} 3 \cup \mathrm{C} 1 \cup \mathrm{D} 2} y_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)-\sum_{i \in \mathrm{~B} 1 \cup \mathrm{C} 2 \cup \mathrm{D} 3} x_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}\right)\right) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Expecting the vehicles to go farther and to complete the process in less time are conflicting. The rationale of designing such an optimization objective is given as follows. As there are multiple vehicles in the scenario, $t_{\mathrm{f}}$ is largely determined by the last CAV that can reach its goal. The other vehicles which are able to reach their goals earlier would not be clearly guided without the second item in Eq. (6). Therefore, although conflicts exist in Eq. (6), that would not be a problem if the weight parameter $\lambda$ is set sufficiently small.
As a summary of the whole section, the introduced constraints and optimization objective, when put together, form an optimal control problem.

## 3. FAULT-TOLERANT COOPERATIVE MOTION PLANNING ALGORITHM

This section proposes a fault-tolerant cooperative motion planner to tackle the actuator faults which occur unexpectedly on the way of the nominal motions. Subsection 3.1 introduces the fault mechanism. Subsection 3.2 provides an overview of the entire FTCC method. Subsection 3.3 introduces the online motion replanning framework. At last, Subsection 3.4 presents the applied online computation method, which provides faultrecovery trajectories efficiently.

### 3.1 CAV Fault Definition

This study considers partial loss of effectiveness of the CAV steering actuator as the failure type. Suppose the $j$ th vehicle becomes faulty at $t=t_{\xi}$, the related equation in Eq. (1) is replaced by

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} \phi_{j}(t)}{\mathrm{d} t}=\gamma_{j} \cdot \omega_{j}(t), t \geq t_{\xi},
$$

where $\gamma_{j} \in[0,1)$ represents the controller effectiveness, and $t_{\xi}$ denotes the fault occurrence instant. $\gamma_{j}=0$ implies an outage, such as the steering angle is stuck and cannot move any longer; and $\gamma_{j} \in[0,1)$ means the steering angle actuator is partially degraded.

### 3.2 Overall Fault-Tolerant Cooperative Control Process

Assumption 1: Each CAV is able to detect the occurred fault and diagnose the loss of effectiveness accurately.

Assumption 2: The instant state of each CAV is always available accurately.

When fault occurs at $t=t_{\xi}$, suppose that the fault detection and diagnosis procedures finish at $t=t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}$. When an accurate $\gamma_{j}$ is obtained at $t=t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}$, online motion replanning begins. Suppose that the online motion replanning process finishes within a given finite-length period $\Delta t_{2}$. Therefore, the faulty period lasts from $t_{\xi}$ to $t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}+\Delta t_{2}$.

During the entire faulty period, the nominal control commands are still sent to the faulty and healthy vehicles. Compared with the healthy vehicles, the faulty vehicle follows the newly diagnosed kinematic principle defined in Eq. (7). Therefore, the configurations of all the CAVs at the moment $t=t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}+\Delta t_{2}$ can be calculated via simulation. Such configurations are taken as the initial states in the motion replanning problems introduced later. When the replanned trajectories are available at or earlier than $t=t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}+\Delta t_{2}$, they are implemented as the fault-recovery commands for all the CAVs since $t=t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}+\Delta t_{2}$. ### 3.3 Fault-Recovery Motion Replanning Framework

The fault-recovery motion replanning process begins after $t=t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}$ and ends at some time no later than $t=t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}+\Delta t_{2}$. Several problems are solved online in parallel.

As the faulty CAV's steering actuator has been degraded, it is natural to investigate whether the faulty vehicle is still able to complete its original goal. If it is not, we wonder whether the rest healthy vehicles can finish their original goals. If they cannot either, the bottom line is to ensure safety. The rest of this subsection concretely defines the multi-level motion replanning problems.

The first problem, namely $P_{1}$, holds Hypothesis 1 that each faulty/healthy vehicle can achieve its original goal. In more details, all the vehicles begin from where they should be at $t=t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}+\Delta t_{2}$ (obtained via simulation); the terminal conditions are completely the same as those introduced in

Subsection 2.3; and the kinematics of the faulty vehicle is modelled according to Eq. (7). If the optimal solution to $P_{1}$ is obtained, then Hypothesis 1 is accepted; and vice versa.

The second problem $P_{2}$ holds Hypothesis 2 that only each healthy vehicle can achieve its original goal. $P_{2}$ is the same as $P_{1}$ except that the faulty vehicle is not required to reach its goal finally. Instead, it is required to stop immediately:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \omega_{j}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\right)=a_{j}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\right)=v_{j}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\right)=0 \\
& \left|x_{j}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\right) \pm \mathrm{R}_{j}\right| \leq \mathrm{RW} \\
& \left|y_{j}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\right) \pm \mathrm{R}_{j}\right| \leq \mathrm{RW}
\end{aligned},
$$

where $t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}$ denotes the fault-recovery process completion time, and $j$ represents the faulty vehicle. If the optimal solution to $P_{2}$ is obtained, while the solution to $P_{1}$ is not achievable, then Hypothesis 2 is accepted. The solution to $P_{2}$ is an alternative choice if $P_{1}$ turns out to be insoluble online. Herein, the event " $P_{1}$ is insoluble online" happens because i) $P_{1}$ is too hard for online computation within the requested time $\Delta t_{2}$; or ii) no possibility ever exists for the faulty CAV to achieve its original goal.

The third problem $P_{3}$ holds Hypothesis 3 that none of the vehicles can achieve the original goal. $P_{3}$ is the same as $P_{1}$ except that all of the CAVs only need to stop immediately:

$$
\omega_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\right)=a_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\right)=v_{i}\left(t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\right)=0, i=1, \ldots, \mathrm{~N}_{\mathrm{V}} .
$$

If the optimal solution to $P_{3}$ is obtained, while the solution to neither $P_{1}$ nor $P_{2}$ is achievable, then Hypothesis 3 is accepted.

In $P_{2}$ or $P_{3}$, the CAVs are actually in an emergent status, thus the acceleration profiles of the braking vehicle(s) can be set smaller than $-\mathrm{a}_{\text {max }}$. Concretely, for vehicle $k$ which aims to stop immediately, the following constraint is used to replace Eq. (2a) in the replanning problem.

$$
\mathrm{a}_{\text {brake }} \leq a_{k}(t) \leq \mathrm{a}_{\max }, t \in\left[t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}+\Delta t_{2}, t_{\mathrm{f}}^{\prime}\right] .
$$

The aforementioned three problems are solved in parallel. The priority order of them is set as $P_{1}>P_{2}>P_{3}$. If more than one problems are successfully solved before $t=t_{\xi}+\Delta t_{1}+\Delta t_{2}$, then the solution to the highest-priority problem is output as the fault-recovery strategy.

As a summary, this subsection has introduced a parallel computation architecture, which enables to prepare a series of solutions which complete the fault-recovery scheme in different levels.

### 3.4 Motion Replanning Algorithm

This subsection briefly introduces how to numerically solve the aforementioned three problems. Basically, an orthogonal collocation direct transcription (OCDT) method is adopted to convert the concerned infinite-dimensional problem to a nonlinear programming (NLP) problem, which is solved by the interior-point method (IPM) thereafter. The progressively constrained dynamic optimization (PCDO) strategy (Li et al., 2017b) is adopted to facilitate the solving process in using IPM to handle NLPs. Finding fault-recovery solutions in real-time remains to be an open problem. For the time being, we use the solution of the fault-free case as the initial guess in solving the fault-recovery motion replanning problems.

## 5. CONCLUSIONS

This paper has presented a cooperative motion planner as part of an FTCC method for multiple CAVs at a lane-free and signal-free intersection. The main contribution lies in the proposal of a parallel computation framework, which enables to complete the original goals to the largest extent when faults have degraded the vehicle team's capability in part.

As our future works, more fault types in addition to the partial loss of effectiveness will be investigated. Particularly, i) faults in both robots and the communication links, and ii) intricate time-variant faults which are difficult to diagnose, will be considered. More importantly, how to solve the fault-recovery replanning problems in real time will be seriously investigated, as the replanning problems in Section 4 cannot be accomplished within $\Delta t_{1}+\Delta t_{2}$, for the time being.
