## I. Introduction

Autonomous vehicles (AVs) have the potential to eliminate car crashes, alleviate traffic congestion and reduce fuel consumption. Such vehicles will open the door to highly safe, efficient and sustainable transportation systems in the future. In December 2014, Google unveiled its first completed self-driving car prototype, which is fully functional. Notably, Google's self-driving cars have driven more than 700,000 miles on California public roads over the past five years [1]. In addition, other famous manufacturers including Audi, BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Nissan, Tesla and Volvo, have announced their intentions to have commercially viable autonomous driving capabilities in multiple vehicle models by 2020 [2]. What's more, several forward-looking U.S. states, including California, Florida, Nevada, Michigan and Washington D.C., are proceeding with AV-enabling legislation to address the barriers to AV adoption and promotion [3]. Assuming an additional five years for driving the price down and marketing promotion, AVs may be available on the mass market by 2025. AVs are inevitable, and one of the accompanying issues is how to manage navigation for AVs across intersections safely and efficiently in a largescale AV market penetration environment.

Over the past few years, some government-supported projects have explored new intersection traffic management strategies in connected vehicle environments, such as Cybercars-2, Intersafe-2, Autonomous Intersection Management, IntelliDrive and Cooperative Intersection Collision Avoidance Systems [4]-[7]. Multi-agent-based autonomous intersection control is a hot topic in the field of autonomous intersection management. Stone [8] proposed a reservation-based intersection control mechanism to coordinate the movement of AVs through intersections. Matteo [9] extended Stone's work by considering how to improve the vehicle crossing speed through coordination at reservation-based intersections and how cooperative actions over an entire network could help further minimize the network travel time. Jin [10] used a dynamic feedback multi-agent system, in which both vehicle agents and the intersection management agent could ex- change information in real time. Tlig [11] proposed a synchronization-based intersection control mechanism to allow AV agents to cross without stopping.

Apart from multi-agent systems, some other methods have been developed for intersection management. Alonso [12] presented a cooperative maneuver among three dualmode cars, and used two methods of resolution to address the problem of several vehicles approaching an intersection. Wu [13] considered the autonomous intersection as a combinatorial optimization problem and proposed an ant colony system to solve the control problem for a large number of vehicles and lanes. Onieva [14] presented a case study in which an AV must cooperate with a supposedly manually driven vehicle to conduct a cross-roads maneuver without risk. Makarem [15] proposed an alternative control method for coordinating AVs at intersections. The simulation results showed that, compared with adaptive traffic lights and roundabout technologies, the proposed method could reduce energy consumption and pollution emissions. Ahmane [16] presented a new model based on timed petri nets with multipliers for controlling the traffic at isolated intersections. The proposed approach was tested in both a computer simulation environment and a real intersection.

The USDOT recently initiated the development and deployment of connected vehicle technologies, formerly known as IntelliDrive technologies. Intersection control and management is one promising area of connected vehicle technology-based dynamic mobility applications. More and more researchers have been pursuing various control methods that were not dependent on any traffic light and stop signal. Lee and Park [17], [18] developed predictive trajectory-based optimal safe gap adjustment logic and proposed a cooperative vehicle intersection control system. In the proposed approach, an optimization model was developed to eliminate the potential overlaps of vehicular trajectories coming from all conflicting approaches at the intersection. Kamal [19] proposed a vehicle-intersection coordination scheme that does not use any traffic lights under a connected vehicle environment. AVs approaching the intersection from all sections were globally coordinated by an intersection coordination unit to allow them to cross the intersection safely and smoothly. Following Stone's concept of reservation-based autonomous control of urban traffic, Li [20] developed a VISSIM external driver model to simulate autonomous intersections in a connected vehicle environment.

In summary, with the development of automated driving technologies and connected vehicle technologies, considerable research has been conducted on cooperative intersection control. However, the majority of the existing control methods passively observed or predicted the trajectories of the approaching AVs rather than strategically guiding the AVs to adjust their approaching trajectories. Based on previous works, this paper presents a novel coordinated intersection control method in a connected vehicle environment and improves the autonomous intersection management strategy in the following aspects:
(1) a novel intersection channelization for mixed traffic flow, including AVs and human-driven vehicles (HDVs); (2) communication failure is taken into account in the method; (3) buffer area for time-space adjustment for approaching AVs; and (4) different control logics and structures for failure control and eliminating invalid solutions. Furthermore, a simulation test-bed is developed, and a typical road network in Guangzhou, China, is used to evaluate the proposed method.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section II proposes the methodology, including the logical structure, models and algorithms. Section III presents the simulation results. Section IV shows the sensitivity analysis of the proposed method under various traffic conditions. Conclusions and future work are presented in Section V.

## II. Methodology

The buffer-assignment mechanism for vehicle-intersection coordination is constructed based on the following assumptions:

1) At each intersection, a connected vehicle center (CVC) and several roadside units are established to coordinate the movement of all vehicles. The CVC can observe the trajectories of all vehicles through traffic detection devices, such as radar or LiDAR-based sensors. The signal timing scheme (for HDVs) is also controlled by the CVC.
2) Each approaching AV attempts to connect to the CVC and transmits its destination, position, acceleration and speed to the CVC periodically. The measurement errors of the states of the AVs are small enough to be ignored. Each AV receives and follows exactly the corresponding instructions of the CVC.
3) The communication protocol between the roadside units and AVs is IEEE 802.11p, which is a standard for wireless access in the vehicular environment [21], [22]. Communication failures may occur.
4) Only automobiles, including AVs and HDVs, are considered in this study.

## A. Buffer-Assignment Mechanism

The fundamental objective of the buffer-assignment mechanism is to strategically guide AVs to pass through an intersection with unimpeded movement, but not at the expense of the safety. The most notable feature of the proposed method is the highly effective vehicle-to-vehicle and vehicle-to-infrastructure coordination.

The essential problem of the buffer-assignment mechanism is strategically allocating time and space resources of some conflict sections (CSs), which may lead to car crashes or traffic jams. From the perspective of geometry, the trajectory of a vehicle at an intersection can be considered

## B. Time Occupancy Model of Conflict Sections

Intersection traffic control can be considered to be a multi traffic flow queuing problem, in which each CS is exclusively used at any time. The optimization of this kind of problem can be described as minimizing the total travel time of all vehicles in a given period of time $(T)$.

The exact number of arriving vehicles cannot be predicted in a long period of time because of the randomness and time variation of traffic flow. Therefore, we divide a given long period of time into several sufficiently small discrete intervals: $T=T_{1}+T_{2}+\cdots+T_{M}$. Then, the mathematical expression of the multi traffic flow queuing problem is described as:

$$
\min Y=\sum_{m=1}^{M} \sum_{k=1}^{N_{m}} t\left(V e h_{k}\right)
$$

where $Y$ represents the total travel time of all vehicles, $N_{m}$ represents the number of vehicles passing the intersections in the $m$ th time interval, and $t\left(V e h_{k}\right)$ is the $k$ th vehicle's travel time traversing the intersection.

To avoid addressing the uncertainty of future traffic demands, the optimized target is to minimize the travel time based on the current traffic demand. (1) can be transformed into:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\min Y= & \min \left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{1}} t\left(V e h_{k}\right)\right)+\min \left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{2}} t\left(V e h_{k}\right)\right)+\cdots \\
& +\min \left(\sum_{k=1}^{N_{M}} t\left(V e h_{k}\right)\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

To describe the conflict phenomena in the CA, a time occupancy structure is defined as $\operatorname{TO}($ CSID, VID $)=$ $\left[\right.$ CSID $, t_{b}, t_{e}$, VID $]$. In this equation, CSID is the ID of the CS, $t_{b}$ is the beginning time of the occupancy, $t_{e}$ is the ending time of the occupancy, and VID is the ID of the vehicle that will travel through this CS. The set of the time occupancy of a CS is denoted as TO(CSID).

How to obtain a feasible crossing span is the core issue, which has been mentioned but not clearly described in previous studies [8], [20]. This study proposes a very convenient method for obtaining a feasible crossing span for an individual AV.

1) Continuity constraint:
$f\left(V e h_{k}, t\right)$ is piecewise continuous differentiable in $\left[t_{0}, t_{1}\right]$, which means that the speed is gradually changing and the acceleration range is limited.
2) Integral constraint:

$$
L=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}} f\left(V e h_{k}, t\right) d t
$$

3) Boundary constraint:

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
f\left(V e h_{k}, t_{0}\right)=v_{0} \\
f\left(V e h_{k}, t_{1}\right)=v_{1}
\end{array}\right.
$$

4) Magnitude constraint:

$$
f\left(V e h_{k}, t\right) \in\left[0, v_{\max }\right]
$$

5) Differential constraint:

$$
f^{\prime}\left(V e h_{k}, t\right) \in\left[a_{\min }, a_{\max }\right]
$$

6) Longitudinal safety distance constraint:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \int_{0}^{x} g\left(V e h_{k-1}, t\right) d t-\int_{0}^{x} f\left(V e h_{k}, t\right) d t \geq S \\
& S=\frac{g^{2}\left(V e h_{k-1}, t\right)-f^{2}\left(V e h_{k}, t\right)}{-2 a_{\min }}+f\left(V e h_{k}, t\right) \sigma+\operatorname{Len}_{k-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $x \in\left[t_{0}, t_{1}\right] . S$ is the basic safety distance. $\sigma$ is the sum of lost time, including reaction time, mechanism delay time, and so forth. Len $_{k-1}$ is the length of the $k$ - 1 th vehicle. $g\left(V e h_{k-1}, t\right)$ is the speed function of the front vehicle. $f$ and $g$ may be different, which means that each vehicle has a distinct speed profile (movement steps). Hence, it is a formidable challenge for the CVC to coordinate multiple AVs in real time.

The time-space constraint of the vehicle is demonstrated as (6), which means that the vehicle will travel a distance exactly equal to $L$ during the time interval $t_{0} \rightarrow t_{1}$. Therefore, obtaining the movement steps of an AV in the BA is equivalent to solving an integral equation, which has several restricted conditions. It is impossible to guarantee that each AV obtains a suitable speed profile in the BA because the speed profile function of an AV has boundary constraint (7), magnitude constraint (8), differential constraint (9), and longitudinal safety distance constraint (10) and (11). An assignment-failure handling process is advanced to improve the fault tolerance of the buffer-assignment mechanism.
First, the CVC will attempt to obtain the crossing chances and a suitable speed profile for an approaching AV at every time interval before it succeeds in passing through the intersection. Indeed, note that AVs will move ahead by themselves even though they have not received a suitable speed profile. The extreme situation is that an AV must stop at the second stop line (as in Fig. 1) and wait for a crossing chance. Then, the CVC will attempt to assign a special crossing span and transmit a single-segment speed profile for the stopped AV. For the sake of safety, all CSs in its route will reserve the occupancy of the entire period according to the maximum crossing time for a HDV in a signal control environment. In this way, a HDV is not very different from an AV for the buffer-assignment mechanism; the former one simply takes up more occupancy time of the CSs. Moreover, the CVC does not assign crossing span to any AVs that are behind a HDV in the BA.

As discussed above, the entire buffer-assignment based coordinated control logic of the CVC contains several parts. These parts work together to construct a complete control model, which can coordinate the movements of all approaching vehicles, including AVs and HDVs, and ensure that each vehicle passes through the intersection safely and orderly.

## IV. Sensitivity Analysis

In real traffic conditions, the proportion of HDVs and the security time gap may have considerable influence on the efficiency and mobility of the traffic system. Therefore, some additional simulation experiments are conducted to evaluate the influence of the proportion of HDVs and the security time gap.

The study site is a cross intersection that only has 2 through lanes in each entrance. The traffic volume of each traffic input is 500 veh/h. As a comparison, signal control method is employed. The signal control scheme is 2 phases with a 50 s fixed cycle, and the travel delay is 25.5 s.

1) Effect of the proportion of HDVs

The proportion of HDVs varies from 0\% to the point where the average travel delay is larger than that in the signal control method based on Webster's algorithm. The simulation results are shown in Fig. 8(a). The performance of the proposed method will be better than that of signal control method if the proportion of HDVs is less than 21\%.
2) Effect of security time gap

The security time gap varies from 1 to 15 simulation seconds. The simulation results are shown in Fig. 8(b). The proposed method dramatically outperforms signal control method even if the security time gap is 15 simulation seconds.

## V. Conclusions and Future Work

To improve the mobility and sustainability of the emerging autonomous intersection management strategy, this study proposes a buffer-assignment based coordinated control method with connected vehicle technology. This paper addresses novel algorithms to assign the crossing span and employs a three-segment linear speed profile to control the trajectories of vehicles in the BA. Furthermore, the assignment failure handling process and crossing rule for HDVs are advanced to enhance the practicability and reliability of the buffer-assignment mechanism.

An actual network in Guangzhou China is employed to evaluate the performance of the buffer-assignment based coordinated control method. The simulation experiments reveal that the proposed method can dramatically improve the efficiency and sustainability of autonomous intersections. The sensitivity analysis shows that the benefits of the buffer-assignment mechanism are considerable even in a strict security condition. Note that the proposed method still dramatically outperforms the signal control method in a relatively low AV market penetration (approximately 80\%). In conclusion, the buffer-assignment based coordinated control method has promising potential for the development of an advanced autonomous intersection management strategy.

The vehicle movement steps provided by the existing model are very rigorous in the BA and CA. A future study will explore some redundant control strategies, by which the motion planning of the vehicle must be revised repeatedly as it moves in the BA and CA to ensure safety. Moreover, the occurrence of incidents should also be considered in real-world implementations, such as car crashes and mechanical failures. Finally, field tests in more complex real-world road networks are necessary to further evaluate the performance and identify possible problems and issues of the buffer-assignment mechanism.
