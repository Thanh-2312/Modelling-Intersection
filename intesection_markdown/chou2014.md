# Evaluation of Triangabout as Alternative for Intersection with Nonthrough Arterial Movement 

Chih-Sheng Chou and Andrew P. Nichols

At the majority of signalized intersections along an arterial, the mainline movement passes straight through the intersection. At four-leg intersections where the major arterial movement is not a through movement (i.e., an L-shaped corridor vertex), the intersection can sometimes experience more congestion than adjacent intersections along the corridor because the two minor approaches, which are adjacent conflicting movements, must be served in a split-phase fashion. A new, unconventional intersection design, referred to as a "triangabout," is proposed to improve mobility and enhance safety for such intersections. The two approaches that are the mainline arterial form two legs of a triangle and a new diagonal section of roadway connecting those two legs is the hypotenuse. Flow within the triangabout is one-way counterclockwise, similar to a roundabout. The three intersections at the vertices of the triangle are signalized to provide a dedicated right-of-way. A case study is performed for the intersection of Van Voorhis Road and Chestnut Ridge Road in Morgantown, West Virginia, along the WV-705 corridor. Simulation results from VISSIM show that the triangabout design can reduce travel delay by 50\% compared with the existing configuration. Also, the number of conflict points compared with a conventional intersection is reduced by 34\%. There are a number of qualitative benefits of this design compared with conventional intersections at an L-shaped corridor vertex.

Arterial corridor alignments in many metropolitan areas are not specifically planned to accommodate future growth and are often defined by the growth that occurs along specific routes, which are then upgraded to handle the demand. Although it is common and preferred for an arterial to pass straight through an intersection, with the minor approaches on opposite sides of the arterial, this movement is not always the case. Thus, there are arterials on which the mainline traffic flow and arterial corridor form a turning movement at an intersection, hereafter referred to as a nonthrough arterial movement (NTAM) intersection (this term was created for use in this paper because no other industry standard terminology could be identified). Two examples of NTAM intersections can be observed along the

[^0]WV-705 Corridor in Morgantown, West Virginia, as shown in Figure 1.

The traffic operations at the NTAM intersection along the arterial can present operational and geometric challenges that do not exist at other intersections along the corridor. For example, the mainline movement will be a left turn in one direction and a right turn in the opposite direction. Although these movements can run concurrently, the assignment of coordinated phases is unconventional, concurrent pedestrian crossing is a challenge, and the two minor approaches at the intersection must operate in a split-phase manner, which will increase lost time.

Conventional mitigation alternatives are evaluated for the study intersection discussed here, including additional lanes, modified signal timings, and a roundabout. However, none of these options were able to fully meet the operational needs as well as satisfy other qualitative objectives of the project. These qualitative objectives include pedestrian safety, access to adjacent businesses, and general right-of-way impacts. More attention has recently been placed on unconventional arterial intersection designs (UAIDs) by the FHWA Every Day Counts program (1). Examples of UAIDs identified by FHWA focus on those that displace left turns or are variations of U-turns. Previous implementations and studies of these designs show that they are useful with unique intersections, particularly when heavy left-turn traffic is present (2-4). The design principles for UAIDs emphasize traffic movement along the arterial while reducing signal phases and conflict points (5). None of the existing UAIDs were suitable for the problems encountered at the study intersections. Ultimately, a new intersection design was derived by combining features of both a roundabout and a quadrant intersection, referred to in this paper as a "triangabout."

The primary objective of the study was to identify feasible intersection design and operational alternatives that would reduce vehicular delay and improve vehicle and pedestrian safety while minimizing right-of-way impacts. The objective of this paper is to convey details about an innovative alternative that was identified within the study to meet these objectives. A studied intersection is presented with its existing traffic condition and possible mitigations. Details of the triangabout design, including geometric layout and signal timing phase plan, are presented after that. Moreover, procedures to evaluate the system by using simulation are discussed. Systematic experimental designs were completed for this study, and quantitative analyses were conducted and synthesized. For simplicity, traffic movements of eastbound left turn, eastbound through, and eastbound right turn are abbreviated EBLT, EBTH, and EBRT, respectively. Similar abbreviation schemes are applied to westbound (WB), northbound (NB), and southbound (SB) directions as well.

## STUDY INTERSECTION

## Existing Conditions

Intersection 2 in Figure $1 a$ (enlarged view in Figure $1 b$ ) will be the focus of the analysis presented here. This intersection is formed by Van Voorhis Road (northbound-southbound), Chestnut Ridge Road (westbound), and Burroughs Street (eastbound). The mainline arterial movement is the WBLT and NBRT. The eastbound and southbound approaches are considered to be the minor approaches. Peak hour data collection (Figure $2 a$ ) revealed that the left-turn demand was 65\% of the overall westbound volume and 72\% of the northbound traffic was for the right-turn movement. The peak hour level of service (LOS) based on control delay is shown in Figure $2 b$ for these same conditions, where $C, D, E$, and $F$ represent average control delay per vehicle in the range of 20 to 35 s, 35 to 55 s, 55 to 80 s, and more than 80 s, respectively. The current ring structure for the intersection is shown in Figure $1 c$. The WBLT movement has dual left-turn lanes, which are served by coordinated Phase 6. The NBRT movement has a single lane, which is essentially active continuously unless the exclusive pedestrian Phase 9 is served, since it turns into an exclusive lane exiting the intersection eastbound. Having both of the major movements active during the same phase (Phase 6) rather than having them be conflicting movements is advantageous.

Disadvantages of this configuration for the NBRT movement include poor lane utilization and increased conflicts south of the intersection (due to vehicles merging into the right-turn lane) and poor platooning at the adjacent signal to the east. The configuration of this intersection is not conducive to pedestrians crossing the westbound approach, which would ordinarily run concurrently with the northbound phase, but is configured as an exclusive phase because of the lack of compliance with the pedestrian signal by the NBRT traffic. Because of the size of the intersection, the pedestrian walk and clearance times are a combined 32 s, which is approximately 15\% of the peak hour cycle length. ## Mitigation Analysis

Various mitigation alternatives were identified and analyzed to alleviate congestion at this intersection while improving vehicular and pedestrian safety. In 2010 a comprehensive arterial improvement plan was initiated that resulted in the deployment of adaptive signal control along the WV-705 corridor (19 signals). In previous research that evaluated the performance of adaptive control along this corridor, the signal system was actually divided into two separate systems; this intersection operated as an isolated intersection in between the systems because it could not operate at a cycle length that was optimal for the rest of the system (6). Therefore, it was clear that the capacity of this intersection needed to be increased so that it was compatible with the rest of the system.

Due to right-of-way constraints in the northeast and northwest quadrants of the intersections, it was determined at the outset of the project that widening could only be considered on the south side of the intersection. The major impact of this decision was the inability to increase the number of lanes on the southbound approach. Additional lanes could be considered on the other three approaches, although minimizing the offset of through lanes was still a consideration.

## Conventional Signalized Intersection Alternatives

Various combinations of additional lanes were considered along with corresponding modifications to the signal phasing. For conciseness, the rationale behind the design of the preferred conventional signalized alternative is not discussed here, but its geometric design and signal phasing are illustrated in Figure 3. This preferred alternative was selected on the basis of multiple analysis iterations and is considered optimal within the project constraints. The alternative is subsequently referred to in this paper as "Add 2 Lanes" since an additional eastbound lane and northbound lane are being added to the existing conventional signalized intersection. Also, the pedestrian movements are concurrent in this configuration.

## Roundabout Alternative

Another alternative considered and evaluated was a multilane roundabout. Considering the right-of-way constraints within this project, the only location to feasibly construct the roundabout was to the southeast of the current intersection location. Although the resulting layout was not ideal, it was considered to be workable pending the results of the operational analysis. The operational analysis was performed using SIDRA Intersection and VISSIM for both two-lane and three-lane roundabout configurations with varying entering lane combinations (7).

The results of the analysis were favorable for the northbound and westbound primary movements; however, the minor approaches, most notably the eastbound approach, were estimated to experience significant queuing. The ramifications on the eastbound approach were caused by the available gaps' being used up by the vehicles on the southbound approach in the heavy WBLT movement; this set-up essentially left no gaps for the eastbound approach. On the basis of the operational analysis, the less-than-ideal geometry, and the likelihood that high-intensity activated crosswalk beacons would likely be required to accommodate the level of pedestrian activity, it was decided that the roundabout was not a workable alternative.

## UAIDs

The UAIDs investigated in this study were median U-turn, jughandle, and quadrant configurations. Among these UAIDs, the quadrant intersection design was most applicable to this situation. The quadrant design is intended to divert left-turn movements away from the main intersection by creating a new two-way roadway in one quadrant of the intersection that connects two adjacent intersection legs. ## New UAID

After some brainstorming regarding the performance of these alternatives and the benefits regarding some of the UAIDs, a new alternative design was identified that provides safety benefits similar to a roundabout through reduced conflict points and operates like a roundabout with counterclockwise flow but provides signalized right-of-way for entering vehicles. A diagonal road is constructed across one of the quadrants, which will serve to divert the minor movements at the intersection away from the main intersection where the heavy left-turn movement will occur. Since the resulting shape is a triangle and the vehicle flow is counterclockwise, similar to a roundabout, the name triangabout was adopted to describe it.

## TRIANGABOUT INTERSECTION DESIGN

## Geometric Layout and Signal Phasing

The proposed triangabout design for the study intersection is illustrated in Figure $4 a$. This layout utilizes currently available right-of-way in the southeast quadrant of the intersection. In Figure $4 a, A$ denotes the location of the original intersection, and $B$ and $C$ are the two new signalized intersections created by the one-way quadrant road. The flow along the three legs of the triangabout is one way in a counterclockwise

FIGURE 4 Triangabout intersection alternative: ( $a$ ) layout and ( $b$ ) signalization.

manner. All left-turn movements at the three signalized intersections are dual lanes with protected-only signalization. Pedestrian access is provided to all four quadrants of the intersection with pedestrian signalization and crosswalks as shown in Figure 4. In order to maintain the flow of traffic circulating within the triangabout, dedicated pedestrian right-of-way is only provided across the external approaches, which allows pedestrian movements to run concurrently with other signalized phases rather than exclusive phases.

Since the three intersections are in close proximity, the signals must operate in a coordinated fashion to facilitate continuous movements through the intersections. Although this operation can be accomplished with three separate traffic signal controllers, it would be more reliable to run all three intersections with a single controller to avoid congestion that might occur when a controller goes into transition (e.g., changing time-of-day plans or preemption). The proposed signal phasing for the three intersections running on one controller is shown in Figure 4b, where the first, second, and third rings show the plans for Intersections C, A, and B, respectively.

The one-way conversion of the two legs AB and AC allows the capacity on those links to be increased. This setup results in the ability to configure the westbound approach to C and northbound approach to B as three entering lanes. The original NBRT (mainline) traffic should only be stopped at one intersection at most. The traffic will be stopped at B when the SBLT movement is active or when a pedestrian activates the pedestrian signal. The pedestrian phase is served concurrently with the SBLT movement to minimize vehicle delays. The NBRT vehicles then proceed through C without stopping because it is a continuous movement without conflicting vehicles or pedestrians. Therefore, the original NBRT movement should not experience a significant difference in delay and actually may be improved because of the additional lane capacity.

The original WBLT (mainline) traffic might get stopped at C, but should proceed through A if the signals are coordinated correctly. Unless a pedestrian is crossing at B, which stops the SBTH movement, those vehicles should continue through unimpeded. Therefore, the triangabout should provide as good, if not better, performance for this major movement.

At the main intersection A, only three approaches are using this intersection, which can operate as a three-phase signal with overlaps; this operation will greatly reduce the overall delay. Intersections B and C can both operate as two-phase signals with overlaps, so lost time will be minimal. The two pedestrian movements at A will run concurrently with other vehicle movements. The WBRT at A will flow continuously as an overlap, except when a pedestrian crosses the southbound approach. Another benefit of this alternative is that widening the eastbound approach is not required. Once the eastbound movement is green, all traffic will turn left into four lanes. The eastbound vehicles that ordinarily go straight through are diverted to B to make a left-turn movement. The eastbound vehicles that ordinarily turn left are affected the most because they are diverted to make left turns at B and C but will generally make a free-flow WBRT back at A. The southbound vehicles that ordinarily turn right or go straight through will be relatively unaffected. The southbound vehicles that ordinarily turn left will be diverted to B to make a left turn but will proceed through C unimpeded. If the signal coordination is effective, vehicles queued at A should not have to stop at B to make a left-turn movement. The original NBLT and NBTH movements are also affected because they must divert to C.

The triangabout will not reduce the delay for every movement at the intersection; however, it should favor the mainline movements and can be timed to benefit other major movements. The benefits that are derived at UAIDs by diverting the left-turning traffic are experienced with the triangabout by diverting through movements because of the NTAM. Similarly, the safety benefits of a roundabout are experienced because of the reduced conflict angles.

## Advantages and Disadvantages of Triangabout

The triangabout system benefits main traffic streams significantly by increasing capacity for WBLT and NBRT movements. It simplifies phase design at Intersection A so that green time can be allocated for a dramatic main traffic stream increase. Phases omitted include all NB movements, EBTH and EBLT, and SBLT. In addition, the exclusive pedestrian phase is removed. Both B and C intersections are running with two-phase design so that green times allocated for the main traffic stream are sufficient for coordination with adjacent intersections. Consequently, the system enhances arterial functionality for the entire corridor. Meanwhile, safety is also improved by reducing the total number of conflict points. After traffic lanes between Intersections A, B, and C are converted into a one-way system, right-angle crossing conflicts, the most severe type, are all eliminated. However, it is possible that rear-end collision rates could increase with the triangabout design because of the addition of two signalized intersections. In addition, the system does not need much land to build additional lanes. It makes the solution possible for areas with a similar traffic pattern and limited land.

In contrast, the routing scheme of a triangabout induces longer distances for minor traffic movements. The most complicated movement is the EBLT, where traffic is diverted through all triangabout intersections to complete the maneuver. Although the main traffic stream along the arterial is well maintained, intersection performance might be degraded due to diverted minor traffic streams if such demand is high. It is possible that the EBLT movement could be served directly, but that additional phase would increase delay for the WBTH and WBRT movements. That scheme is not feasible for this location because of the volume distribution and because the EBLT storage is not adequate, which would cause left-turning vehicles to impede the flow of the EBRT vehicles. This alternative could be explored in other applications of this design.

The design itself is considered to be unconventional because there is no known similar installation. It might also take time for road users to become familiar with the routing scheme, and it could initially result in an increase in crashes until drivers become familiar with the setup. Nevertheless, "newness is not a sufficient reason to ignore an otherwise superior alternative" (2).

## QUANTIFYING BENEFITS OF PREFERRED ALTERNATIVES

## Techniques for Measuring Intersection Performance

This study applied simulation to quantify the performance of the triangabout compared with both the existing operation and the operation of the conventional signal with two additional lanes (i.e., Add 2 Lanes) because such methods have been commonly suggested or used to evaluate UAID designs in prior studies (5, 8, 9). VISSIM, a traffic microsimulation tool, was used for this study to measure and compare the performance of the alternatives, including delay, travel time, and emissions (10).

VISSIM applies car-following and lane-changing models to replicate traffic operation in real time. In VISSIM numerous network elements, such as Link, Vehicle Inputs, Route Decision, Controller, Signal Head, Detector, and others, must be applied to build an arterial simulation network. Once it is built, users can easily modify a network element (e.g., add or reduce number of lanes on a link) or vehicle inputs (e.g., different volume levels) to conduct sensitivity analyses. VISSIM also allows users to customize various outputs and performance metrics. To evaluate intersection performance, the element node can be placed in such a way that its edges cross each intersection approach and form a polygon. Various performance metrics, including average delay and emissions, can be derived for directional traffic flows (e.g., NBRT and WBLT) between the polygon boundaries. Moreover, another simulation element, travel time measurement, can be employed to measure travel time between two locations. Both the node and travel time measurement elements are applied to quantify alternative performance.

To evaluate the general safety of an intersection, conflict points resulting from crossing, diverging, or merging vehicle paths can be identified and compared among alternatives. Such a conflict diagram is developed by manually drawing all possible vehicle paths through an intersection and classifying the points where two paths intersect. Theoretically, the conflict points should be eliminated through assigning movements to different signal phases. In fact, many crashes occur at an intersection when phases change to conflicting directions or when vehicles violate right-of-way assignments. Thus, conflict point analysis for an intersection should be based on both signalized and unsignalized scenarios. Recently, Pan et al. proposed a method to determine conflict points for intersections in a systematic way (11). Their method considered intersection geometry, signal control, and traffic movement and computed virtual lanes for each traffic movement. Three calculation models for various conflict points were provided in their study, and they were adopted here.

## Experimental Design

A fully calibrated VISSIM model representing the WV-705 corridor in Morgantown, West Virginia (Figure $5 a$ ), was used as the base model for this analysis (6). Because this study focuses on the NTAM intersection circled in Figure $5 a$ (enlarged view in Figure $5 b$ ), a node element is placed around the intersection. Any variations in intersection geometry between alternatives must be contained within the node for valid comparison. In addition to the existing configuration, the Add 2 Lanes and triangabout (Figure 5, $c$ and $d$, respectively) designs are modeled as intersections along the arterial. Since the triangabout alternative contains more than one signalized intersection, the operations could not be quantified per intersection and combined to compare with the other two alternatives. Therefore, the node analysis that treats the three intersections as a single system was necessary.

Moreover, travel time measurement elements are placed to measure travel time in both directions for a segment that includes

Pedestrian demand was modeled in all scenarios, with the same demand but different crossing locations and pedestrian phase timing parameters for each alternative. Although they are included in the model to determine the impacts on vehicle delay, pedestrian delay was not calculated.

Although this analysis focused on improvements to a single intersection, the analysis was conducted for the entire corridor in order to capture the effect of vehicle platooning and to observe the quality of signal progression. Two volume scenarios were evaluated, which represented the existing conditions (as shown in Figure $2 a$ for one of the 19 intersections) and future conditions in 20 years assuming 1.5\% annual growth. Each of the six scenarios (three alternatives times two volumes) was simulated from 5:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m. and repeated five times with different random seeds; the result was 30 simulations. The processing time for each simulation run was approximately 2.5 h. Performance measures for each scenario were derived by averaging the five simulation runs. Standard deviations were also computed and inspected to make sure the results were within reasonable ranges.

## Results

The a.m. peak period at the study intersection is from 7:45 to 8:45 a.m. and the p.m. peak is from 4:30 to 5:30 p.m. The performance measures, average delay, emissions, fuel consumption, and vehicle counts were summarized only for the peak hours. As stated previously, these quantities were measured across the boundaries entering and exiting the node. Vehicle counts from the simulation are compared with the field data collection to ensure that those conditions are reasonably replicated. Analysis of the results revealed that the volumes for each alternative were within 1\% of the volumes observed in the field.

## Safety

Both signalized and unsignalized control scenarios were evaluated for the conflict point analysis, as previously described. The results of the conflict types identified with Pan's method for the three networks are summarized in Table 2. The values shown for the triangabout are the sum of the three intersections. In addition, the values shown in parentheses for the triangabout are calculated from the conflict diagrams shown in Figure 8, which were manually produced because Pan's method did not seem to be applicable to unique intersection configurations. Under signalized control, many conflicts are minimized or eliminated because conflicting movements are assigned to different phases. Consequently, safety is improved while mobility is reduced. Results of the unsignalized scenario can reflect the possible crashes that could occur during a phase transition or when drivers violate the assigned right-of-way. As shown in Table 2, the triangabout system reduces conflict points by 34\% compared with the existing network. The Add 2 Lanes alternative, however, increases the conflict number by 13\%. The benefits of the triangabout are similar to those of a roundabout where the more severe crossing type conflicts are eliminated because of the unidirectional flow of traffic.

## Benefit-Cost Analysis

With the estimated benefits, equivalent monetary values can be computed for the purpose of computing benefit-cost ratios. This study included measures of travel delay, emissions, and fuel consumption for benefit estimates. According to the field data collection at this intersection, the a.m. and p.m. peak volumes respectively shared 8\% and 11\% of the daily total. In addition, assuming 5 weekdays and 52 weeks in a year, annual benefit projections are derived for both alternatives when the performance from the existing network is set as a basis. Results show that the triangabout contributes more than US\$ 2 million per year, whereas the Add 2 Lanes alternative shows a slightly lower number. Estimated construction costs (including right-of-way acquisition) for these two alternatives are \$700,000 for the triangabout and \$750,000 for the Add 2 Lanes network; these values result in benefit-cost ratios of 2.88 and 2.66, respectively, based only on the first year of operation. ## Analysis of Alternative Robustness

Results from simulating the future 20-year horizon volume scenario indicated that the triangabout performs better than the Add 2 Lanes alternative. Specifically, the projected average delay of the triangabout design will increase from 26 to 55 s per vehicle in the a.m. peak hour and from 33 to 79 s per vehicle in the p.m. peak hour. The corresponding values for the Add 2 Lanes alternative are 29 to 58 s per vehicle and 32 to 88 s per vehicle. Although the results are similar, the triangabout design can provide as good or better performance in the future years.

## CONCLUSION

The proposed triangabout design can significantly improve mobility and safety of the study intersection in Morgantown; the design is an uncommon configuration in which the mainline movement is not a through movement and operates as concurrent left and right turns. Results from quantitative analysis produced with VISSIM indicated that the triangabout can save more than 50\% of travel delay and reduce 34\% of the conflict points at this intersection. This alternative not only enhances the arterial functionality along the corridor but also benefits most of the minor movements at this intersection. The operational benefits for the overall intersection between the triangabout and an improved conventional signalized alternative were not drastic. However, the triangabout alternative greatly improves motorist safety at the intersection and has other qualitative benefits, including improved pedestrian safety, improved access to adjacent developments, and reduced right-of-way impacts. Therefore, the triangabout is deemed to be a preferable alternative.

Because the triangabout diverts minor traffic movements, the intersection delay may further degrade if that minor traffic demand increases. Thus, future research will include a sensitivity analysis to investigate this effect. Furthermore, the length of the internal legs of the triangabout was dictated by the available right-of-way, but future analysis will investigate the sensitivity of these distances. The traffic signal control for the triangabout is quite complex, especially if operated with a single controller. Performance is heavily dependent on having the optimal coordination settings.
