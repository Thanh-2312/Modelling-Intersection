# Controlled Optimization of Phases at an Intersection 

SUVRAJEET SEN AND K. LARRY HEAD<br>SIE Dept. of University of Arizona, Tucson, Arizona 85721


#### Abstract

This paper presents a general purpose algorithm for real-time traffic control at an intersection. Our methodology, based on dynamic programming, allows optimization of a variety of performance indices such as delay, stops and queue lengths. Furthermore, optimal phase sequencing is a direct by-product of this new approach. These features make the new methodology a powerful tool for intersection control. We demonstrate the usefulness of the approach by a simulation experiment in which our intersection control algorithm is interfaced with a well established simulation package called TRAF-NETSIM. Our study compares the controlled optimization of phases methodology with fully-actuated as well as semi-actuated control. We show that consistent reductions in delay may be possible by adopting the new algorithm.


Recent interest in the application of advanced technologies for surveillance, communication and control have renewed interest in the development of real-time traffic-adaptive signal control systems. There have been two notable real-time systems developed and deployed, SCOOT (see Hunt, et al. (1981), and ROBERTSON (1986)) and SCATS (see Sims (1979) and Luk (1984)). The SCOOT system automates an off-line methodology based on the use of the TRANSYT traffic signal optimization model (FHWA (1988)) by utilizing on-line surveillance information to incrementally adjust the signal timing. SCATS is a hierarchical control system that forms groups of intersections, all of which use similar signal timings. These groups are formed based on similarities in traffic conditions at the intersections in a group. Both systems have been deployed in a number of cities and have generally been found to improve traffic control. Other systems, including TRAC (LEES (1989)) and UTOPIA (Mauro and Di Taranto (1990)), have also been implemented in a few cities.

Most of these systems view the control problem in a "macro" setting in which signal control parameters (e.g., cycle time, splits, and offsets) are optimized based on a macroscopic traffic flow model. Such models are appropriate for centralized control. However, with greater proliferation of computer technology, and the deployment of Advanced Traffic Management Systems, decentralized real-time systems are under consideration for development, testing, and deployment. Using real-time traffic data, such decentralized systems are expected to control each intersection and provide coordination between multiple intersections. This paper is devoted to the development of a module that provides intersection control within a larger system called RHODES (Head, Mirchandani and Sheppard (1992)).
In developing a real-time control model for an intersection, Gartner (1983) was the first to recognize the need to move away from parametric models which optimize parameters such as cycle time, splits, and offsets. Instead, Gartner (1983) proposed a non-parametric model in which the decision to switch between phases is based on actual arrival data at the intersection, thus making the model more relevant for real-time traffic-adaptive signal control. The model stated by Gartner (1983) is a discrete-time optimal control formulation of the single intersection real-time traffic-adaptive control problem. However, this formulation does not lend itself very well to dynamic programming (DP)-based methods. In an effort to adopt a practical solution method for this formulation Gartner suggested the use of a restricted search heuristic that enumerates a few alternative feasible solutions for a two phase intersection. This method, known as OPAC, has since been field-tested and has been shown to reduce delays in a number of studies (Gartner, Tarnoff and Andrews (1992)). More recently, OPAC has
been extended to accommodate arterial networks (Farradyne Systems, Inc. (1993)).

Our model is motivated by the need to address many of the same issues that have directed the development of OPAC. Thus, we are interested in the development of phase switching decisions based on arrival data at an intersection. Furthermore, we are interested in developing a model that can be used under a variety of traffic conditions. And finally, our goal is to develop a computational scheme that is efficient enough to be implemented in realtime. Unlike OPAC, our algorithmic process is based entirely on DP. The contribution here lies in a reorientation (of the model) which allows us to solve rather general intersection control problems using only one state variable for optimization. This results is an efficient and general procedure that yields an optimal solution to the problems addressed in Gartner (1983). In fact, the optimization method also allows phase sequencing, without which the impact of real-time optimization may be very limited. Finally, the generality of DP translates into a very flexible control algorithm since it is applicable to a variety of performance measures including delays, queues and stops.
The outline of this paper is as follows. We begin with a discussion of the basic intersection signal timing problem and identify some of the critical issues that the methodology must address. We then formulate the intersection control problem as an optimization problem and show that the problem structure is such that DP is an appropriate solution methodology. The DP formulation is presented and followed by a discussion of the formulation of several possible objective functions. We also present a simple example that demonstrates the methodology. Finally, the results of a simulation study of a single intersection are presented.

## 1. PROBLEM FORMULATION

### 1.1. The Intersection Traffic Control Problem

At a signalized intersection, a traffic signal assigns the right-of-way to approaching vehicles in such a way as to allow safe passage through the intersection. Generally, it is desired to make this right-of-way assignment to minimize (optimize) stops, delay, or some other measure of performance. Consider an intersection and the set of possible vehicle movements as shown in Figure 1A. Note, that for our purposes, right turn movements are considered as part of the through movement. Combinations of non-conflicting movements that allow safe passage through the intersection are called phases. Figure $1 B$ shows one possible set of phases, denoted

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/0a83e11d-f02f-4375-8495-66ce45b56cb9-03.jpg?height=597&width=571&top_left_y=226&top_left_x=1263)
Fig. 1. Intersection movements, possible phases, and one possible sequence of phases and phase timings. (A) Typical intersection and set of movements. $(B)$ One possible set of phases. $(C)$ One possible sequence of phases and their associated timings.

$P$. Each phase $\phi \in P$ corresponds to a set of nonconflicting movements. Of course, other phases are possible including combinations of movements 1 and 2, 3 and 4, 5 and 6, and 7 and 8.
Any sequence of phases, and their associated phase durations is considered a signal timing plan. Figure 1C depicts one possible signal timing plan. A traditional fixed-time traffic signal control algorithm allocates a predetermined amount of time to each phase in a fixed phase sequence. Fixed-time signal timing plans are generally developed based on historically recorded, or observed, volumes and not on the actual real-time traffic. Traffic actuated signal control utilizes traffic detectors to observe real-time vehicle arrivals to adjust the phase durations (timing) between prespecified minimum and maximum phase times. Note that these methods neither optimize nor evaluate any performance measure in real-time.
In addition to ensuring that no more than one phase is allocated the right of way at any point in time, a feasible timing plan must satisfy two safety requirements: a) whenever a phase is allocated nonzero green time it must exceed a prescribed minimum, and b) when a signal switches from one phase to another, it must allocate an effective clearance interval during which no movement through the intersection is permitted. We assume that this effective clearance interval contains a portion of an "amber" interval and an "all-red" interval.

One exception to property (b) above occurs when phase overlaps are allowed. A phase overlap can occur when a pair of phases that contain at least one common movement occur in sequence. For example, using the movement descriptions depicted in Figure

1A, a phase consisting of movements 1 and 2, and a phase consisting of movements 2 and 6, occurring in sequence would allow movement 2 to "overlap" and no clearance interval would be necessary. The controlled optimization of phases (COP) model presented in the following section can be modified to allow phase overlapping by allowing the duration of the clearance interval to depend on combinations of movements in adjacent phases. In order to keep the following discussion as simple as possible, we do not address phase overlaps in our development.

The development of a real-time optimization method introduces the need for traffic prediction, at least for short-term traffic flow prediction. Note that without the ability to make arrival predictions, the system will not be proactive, thus eliminating sorne of the advantages of real-time optimization. Hence, real-time traffic-adaptive systems must introduce a prediction module. We do not directly address the prediction problem in this article, but concentrate on the optimization algorithm for utilizing short-term traffic flow predictions for intersection control. The simulation results that are presented in Section 5 have utilized a short-term traffic flow prediction model (Head (1995)) and we present a brief summary in Appendix A.

Given the phase of the signal at time 0 and a predicted demand over the time-interval $[0, T]$, the intersection control problem involves a choice of phase sequences and timings so as to optimize a particular performance index (e.g., delay or stops or queue lengths), while satisfying safety requirements (a) and (b) above.

### 1.2. The COP Formulation for Intersection Traffic Control

We now proceed to the DP formulation used in COP. We begin with a brief summary of DP terminology and refer the reader to DREYFUS and LAW (1977) for a more thorough treatment. The DP methodology is based on breaking a decision problem into manageable decision stages and computing an appropriate measure of effectiveness in a recursive manner. The recursion can be performed either in a forward fashion, starting from the initial decision stage, or in a backward fashion, starting from the final decision stage. In our development, we will only consider a forward recursion. In this setting, a value function for any stage represents an accumulated measure of effectiveness for the current and all previous stages. In specifying a DP formulation we must define the appropriate state variables, decision stages, and the recursion necessary to compute the value functions. Alternative definitions of these quantities lead to alternative realizations of a DP methodology. More traditional formulations are provided in Appendix B for the sake of completeness. We shall make some comparative remarks subsequently.

Notation. We introduce the following notation.

$P \equiv$ Set of phases. The cardinality of this set will be denoted $|P|$. Individual phases are indexed by $\phi$.
$T \equiv$ Total number discrete time-steps. Each period of length $\Delta$ is indexed by $t \in[1, T]$.
$\gamma \equiv$ Minimum green time (integer number of timesteps).
$r \equiv$ Effective clearance interval (integer number of time-steps). We assume that $r \leqslant \gamma$.

Our recursion is based on using phases as stages in the DP calculation. Starting with an initial phase $(\ell)$, COP treats each phase in a cycle as a stage, and optimizes over as many cycles as necessary to obtain an optimum. In our development, the term cycle is used mainly for expository purposes and we emphasize that COP allows us to skip phases whenever it is advantageous to do so. At any rate, the DP approach in COP defines stages by assigning phase $\ell$ of cycle 1 to stage 1, phase $(\ell+1) \operatorname{modulo}(|P|)$ to stage 2 , etc. The exact number of stages used in the DP is a by-product of the computations and need not be specified at the outset. We introduce additional notation to describe the methodology.

| $j$ | $\equiv$ Index for stages of the DP. |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\ell$ | $\equiv$ Index in $P$ that denotes the initial phase. |
| $x_{j}$ | ⇒ Control variable denoting the amount of green-time allocated to stage $j$. |
| $s_{j}$ | ⇒ State variable denoting the total number of time-steps that have been allocated after stage $j$ has been completed. |

## 2. CALCULATION OF PERFORMANCE INDICES

The performance indices we consider in this section are

- Total number of stops
- Maximum queue length
- Total delay

Traffic engineers may prefer using one performance index over another or a combination, depending upon the prevailing traffic conditions. For exam-
ple, when a system is not saturated, the number of stops may be an appropriate criterion, but as the ratio of volume to capacity, or saturation, increases (beyond 0.8 , say) total delay becomes the preferred criterion (Mr. Dennis Sheppard, Assistant Traffic Engineer, City of Tucson, AZ). Since our goal is to develop a flexible model, we allow any one of the three criteria mentioned above.

In traditional approaches (see (B.1-B.4) in Appen$\operatorname{dix} \mathrm{B})$, modeling queue build-up and dissipation constitutes one of the more important tasks. While our DP recursion does not assume measured queue lengths, these quantities must be calculated, or estimated, for the evaluation of some performance indices. Fortunately, our methodology treats queue lengths as a function of the state variable defined in the previous section and the observed/predicted vehicle arrivals to the intersection. Consequently, queue lengths are calculated for accounting purposes and their traditional role as state variables (for optimization) is abandoned. It turns out that the separability required in (4) is maintained when the performance index is the number of stops. However, this property may not always be satisfied when modeling total delay. In the ensuing discussion, we shall first summarize the calculation of queue lengths and then present the computations necessary for the evaluation of performance indices.
We introduce further notation.

$k \equiv$ Index for vehicles arriving at the intersection.
$a_{k} \equiv$ Predicted arrival time of vehicle $k$.
$p_{k} \equiv$ Phase requested by vehicle $k$.
$\phi(j) \equiv$ The index in $P$ associated with stage $j$.
$L_{\phi, j} \equiv$ A function of $s_{j}$ and $x_{j}$ denoting temporary queue length (see definition below).
$Q_{\sigma, j} \equiv \mathrm{~A}$ function of $s_{j}$ denoting permanent queue length (see definition below).
$A_{\phi} \equiv$ A function of scalars, $a, b$ denoting the number of vehicles arriving in the timeinterval $(a, b)$ and requesting phase $\phi$. If $a=b$, we put $A_{\phi}(a, b)=0$.
$M_{\phi} \equiv \mathrm{A}$ function of the green-time $x$. This quantity denotes the maximum number of vehicles that can be discharged by queue $\phi$ during a time-interval of length $x$.

### 2.1. Calculation of Queue Lengths

The calculation of performance indices will be carried out by decomposing the time-interval [0, $T$ ] into sub-intervals $\left[s_{j-1}, s_{j}\right), j=1,2, \ldots$. Recall that with $s_{j}$ and $x_{j}$ given, $s_{j-1}$ is obtained via (2). Hence the time-interval $\left[s_{j-1}, s_{j}\right)$ may be looked upon as a function of $s_{j}$ and $x_{j}$.

We will assume that initial queue lengths, denoted $Q_{\phi, 0}$ for all $\phi \in P$, are either available or are continuously estimated. In general for stage $j$, we assume that the quantities $Q_{\sigma, j-1}\left(s_{j-1}\right)$ are available for all values of the variable $s_{j-1}$. In the spirit of our recursive calculations, we now discuss how we calculate the value $Q_{\sigma, j}\left(s_{j}\right)$ for any given value of $s_{j}$. The strategy is as follows.

1. Given $s_{j}$ calculate temporary queue lengths $L_{\sigma, j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)$ for each $\phi \in P$ and $x_{j} \in X_{j}\left(s_{j}\right)$.
2. Based on the temporary queue lengths $L_{\sigma, j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)$ we can calculate the performance index $f_{j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)$, which in turn is used in the minimization in step 1 of the Forward Recursion.
3. Identify $x_{j}^{*}\left(s_{j}\right)$ according to the minimization in step 1 of the Forward Recursion. Then the temporary queue length $L_{\sigma_{, j}}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}^{*}\left(s_{j}\right)\right)$ is recorded as the permanent queue length $Q_{\sigma, j}\left(s_{j}\right)$.

We have presented the above strategy in a manner that suggests that $L_{\sigma, j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)$ should be calculated for all $x_{j}$ before conducting the minimization. This is done only for expositional rather than computational reasons. Within a computer implementation, these steps are performed in a streamlined manner and values of $L_{\sigma, j}$ need not be retained for calculations in other stages. Hence we refer to these values as temporary. On the other hand, the values referred to as permanent queue lengths must be retained for calculation of future stages. We now present the formulas for temporary queue lengths. If $\phi \neq \phi(j)$, we let

$$
L_{\sigma, j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)=Q_{\sigma, j-1}\left(s_{j-1}\right)+A_{\phi}\left(s_{j-1}, s_{j}\right) .
$$

On the other hand, if $\phi=\phi(j)$, we calculate the following

$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{\sigma, j} & \left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right) \\
= & \operatorname{Max}\left\{0, Q_{\sigma, j-1}\left(s_{j-1}\right)+A_{\phi}\left(s_{j-1}, s_{j-1}+x_{j}\right)-M_{\phi}\left(x_{j}\right)\right\} \\
& +A_{\phi}\left(s_{j-1}+x_{j}, s_{j}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

In the above calculations, note that arrivals during the clearance interval, $A_{\phi}\left(s_{j-1}+x_{j}, s_{j}\right)$, are entered in the queue, regardless of the phase last served.

It should be noted that jurisdictional operational policies, such as disallowing phase skipping in the presence of non-zero queue lengths, can be accommodated in this formulation. This can be accomplished by not allowing $x_{j}=0$ when $L_{c, j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)>0$.

We now proceed to the calculation of several performance indices.

### 2.2. Maximum Queue Length

Given the calculation of queue lengths in the previous subsection, the maximum queue length is simply the following performance measure.

$$
f_{j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)=\operatorname{Max}_{\phi \in P} L_{\sigma, j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)
$$

Note also that the operator ∘ used in (4) (and in the forward recursion) is the "Max" operator for this performance index.

### 2.3. Number of Stops

In calculating this performance measure, we count those vehicles that arrive in the time-interval $\left[s_{j-1}, s_{j}\right)$ and are stopped. Let

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{\sigma, j} \equiv & \text { A function of } s_{j} \text { and } x_{j} \text { denoting temporarily } \\
& \text { stopped vehicles. As with temporary queue } \\
& \text { lengths, this value need not be retained for } \\
& \text { calculations in other stages. }
\end{aligned}
$$

The number of stops within the time-interval $\left[s_{j-1}\right.$, $s_{j}$ ) is given by

$$
f_{j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)=\sum_{\phi \in P} S_{\sigma, j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)
$$

where quantities $S_{\sigma, j}$ are calculated as follows. If $\phi \neq \phi(j)$, we have

$$
S_{\sigma, j}=A_{\phi}\left(s_{j-1}, s_{j}\right) .
$$

On the other hand, if $\phi=\phi(j)$, consider the following formula.

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{\sigma, j}= & \operatorname{Max}\left\{0, A_{\phi}\left(s_{j-1}, s_{j-1}+x_{j}\right)\right. \\
& \left.\quad-\operatorname{Max}\left\{0, M_{\phi}\left(x_{j}\right)-Q_{\sigma, j-1}\left(s_{j-1}\right)\right\}\right\} \\
& +A_{\phi}\left(s_{j-1}+x_{j}, s_{j}\right) .
\end{aligned}
$$

To justify the above formula, note that the inner "Max" makes it possible to first discharge the vehicles that are queued at the beginning of the timeinterval (i.e., $s_{j-1}$ ), and then if further capacity is available, arriving cars are discharged. If any arriving cars remain (i.e., the outer "Max" is positive), they are included in the count of stopped vehicles. Finally the last term in the formula denotes the number of cars arriving in the clearance interval.

### 2.4. Delay

This is, perhaps, the most commonly reported performance index in the transportation literature. The total delay $f_{j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)$ will consist of several components. It is convenient to classify the vehicles according to whether they arrive in the time-interval [ $s_{j-1}$, $s_{j}$ ) or they are in queue at the beginning of the time-interval. In addition, it is convenient to define the following functions.

$$
\begin{aligned}
& D_{\phi} \equiv \text { A function representing total delay for vehi- } \\
& \text { cles requesting phase } \phi \text {. } \\
& B_{\phi} \equiv \begin{array}{l}
\text { A function of scalars } a, b \text { denoting the quan- } \\
\text { tity }
\end{array} \\
& \qquad B_{\phi}(a, b)=\sum_{k: p_{k}=\phi, a \leqslant a_{k}<b}\left(b-a_{k}\right) .
\end{aligned}
$$

$T_{\phi} \equiv$ This function is used in calculating minimum delay for vehicles arriving in the time-interval $[a, b)$ and not leaving until after $b$.
$T_{\phi} \equiv \mathrm{A}$ function of the number of vehicles discharged $(d)$. This quantity denotes the total delay in discharging $d$ vehicles.
$t_{\phi} \equiv$ This function of $s_{j}$ and $x_{j}$ is only defined for $\phi=\phi(j)$. This quantity denotes the arrival time of the earliest vehicle that is required to stop while phase $\phi(j)$ has the right of way. When no arriving vehicles are stopped we put $t_{\phi}=s_{j-1}+x_{j}$, where $\phi=\phi(j)$.
Given $s_{j}, x_{j}$, the total delay associated with timeinterval $\left[s_{j-1}, s_{j}\right)$ is calculated by adding the delays associated with each phase $\phi \in P$, that is,

$$
f_{j}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)=\sum_{\phi \in P} D_{\phi}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right) .
$$

If $\phi \neq \phi(j)$, the delay $D_{\phi}$ consists of delay associated with those vehicles that are in queue at the beginning of the time-interval plus delay associated with arriving vehicles. This quantity is calculated in (6).

$$
D_{\phi}\left(s_{j}, x_{j}\right)=Q_{\sigma, j-1}\left(s_{j-1}\right)\left(s_{j}-s_{j-1}\right)+B_{\phi}\left(s_{j-1}, s_{j}\right)
$$

On the other hand, for $\phi=\phi(j)$, the delay $D_{\phi}$ consists of delay associated with vehicles that are discharged, plus vehicles in queue that are not discharged in the current green phase plus the delay associated with arriving vehicles that are not allowed to pass in the current green phase. These three terms constitute the three components added in (7) below. Thus for $\phi=\phi(j)$ we have

$$

## 4. THE SIMULATION FRAMEWORK AND EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

In order to test the effectiveness of the COP formulation we developed a simulation-based framework for experimentation. The framework includes interfacing the COP algorithm with a prediction methodology and a traffic network simulation that can generate realistic vehicle detector data. The prediction methodology is described in Appendix A (see also Head (1995)), and the traffic simulation used in this study was TRAF-NETSIM (FHWA (1992)) which is a microscopic simulation model. Figure 3 depicts the framework showing the interface between these models.

Traffic parameters, including the phase sequence, effective clearance interval, and minimum green time are provided to COP and the prediction model. In addition, the prediction model requires information about the network geometry, historical turning percentages, and speed-density relationships. The traffic simulation model provides event-by-event detector data to the prediction model. The predicted arrivals are then used by the COP algorithm to generate the signal controls that are in turn passed to both the prediction model and the traffic simulation.
In order to ensure as realistic conditions as possible a small network based on a section of the Tucson, Arizona network was modeled. Figure 4 shows the layout of the network. Intersection 483 (Campbell Avenue and Broadway) was selected for implementation of the COP algorithm.

## 5. CONCLUSIONS

In this paper, we have described a DP algorithm for the optimal control of a single intersection. The methodology derives its power from the generality of DP which allows us to optimize a variety of performance measures including delay, queues and stops. Furthermore, COP accommodates phase sequencing, which is one of the more critical requirements for real-time optimization.

## APPENDIX A <br> SUMMARY OF THE SHORT-TERM PREDICTION MODEL

The COP method requires knowledge of the future traffic arrival process in order to optimize the future traffic signal plan. To provide this required information we have developed a data driven, short-term, traffic flow prediction model.

The model, called PREDICT, utilizes the timespace relationship that is inherent in traffic flow to provide the necessary predictions. PREDICT observes the traffic flow at all of the intersections that are upstream to the controlled intersection. Each time a vehicle crosses an upstream detector, the expected travel time is computed based on the upstream traffic signal plan, upstream queue lengths, and link travel times (based on link free-flow speed and the level of congestion). Figure A1 depicts 4 possible cases that can make up the travel time.

Once the travel time is estimated, the probability that the vehicle will follow a route into the controlled intersection is used to compute the expected number of arrivals at the controlled intersection. This probability is based on the turning percentages at the upstream intersections. These turning percentages must somehow be estimated using, for example, an intersection capacity analysis study. We do not currently know of any real time method for estimating these percentages, however our simulation study has shown that the results are not sensitive to small variations in these parameters.

Another important issue in predicting traffic flow is the effect of sources and sinks on the approaches to the controlled intersection. In some cases when
the volumes generated by these sources and sinks are significant, it may be difficult to provide accurate predictions. In these cases, a predictive traffic control method, such as COP, may not be the appropriate form of control. However, we feel that in cases where the flow can be predicted with some degree of accuracy, a traffic adaptive method such as COP can provide considerable advantages over fixed-time and traffic actuated control.

## APPENDIX B <br> TRADITIONAL FORMULATIONS

Traditionally, the intersection control problem has been stated as a discrete-time optimal control problem. In order to provide a self-contained presentation, we include two formulations in this appendix. The first one is a simplified formulation in which the minimum green-time constraint is ignored. The second formulation provides a more complete integer programming representation. For the sake of simplicity in presentation, both formulations assume that vehicles can be served by exactly one phase. We use the following notation in the mathematical formulation.

Notation.

| $P$ | ⇒ Set of phases. Individual phases are in- dexed by $\phi$. |
| :--- | :--- |
| $T$ | $\equiv$ Total number discrete time-intervals. Each period of length $\Delta$ is indexed by $t \in[1, T]$. |
| $\gamma$ | ⇒ Minimum green time (integer number of time-steps). |
| $r$ | ⇒ Clearance interval (integer number of time-steps). |
| $A_{\phi}(t)$ | $\equiv$ Predicted arrivals requesting phase $\phi$ in time-interval $t$. |
| $d_{\phi}(q)$ | $\equiv$ A function that predicts the number of vehicles discharged from a queue of length $q$ during one time-interval of green for phase $\phi$. |
| $n_{\phi}(t)$ | ⇒ A state variable specifying the number of vehicles in the queue for phase $\phi$ in time-interval $t$. The matrix $N$ (with rows $\left.n_{\phi}(\cdot)\right)$ will denote the entire collection of state variables. |
| $u_{\phi}(t)$ | ⇒ A control variable whose value is 1 if phase $\phi$ is green and 0 otherwise. The matrix $U$ (with rows $\left.u_{\phi}(\cdot)\right)$ will denote the entire collection of control variables. |
| $F(N, U) \equiv$ | The performance index to be optimized. |

The simplest statement of the optimal control problem (without satisfying the minimum green time requirement) may be stated as follows.

$$
n_{\phi}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}{l}
\operatorname{Min} F(N, U) \\
n_{\phi}(t-1)+A_{\phi}(t) \quad \text { if } \quad u_{\phi}(t)=0 \\
-d_{\phi}\left(n_{\phi}(t-1)+A_{\phi}(t)\right) \\
\quad \text { if } \quad u_{\phi}(t)=\text { '1 } t
\end{array}\right.
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \sum_{\phi \in P} u_{\phi}(t) \leqslant 1 \quad \forall \mathrm{t} \\
& u_{\phi}(t) \in\{0,1\} \quad \forall \phi, t
\end{aligned}
$$

In this formulation we use $\forall \phi, t$ to denote $\forall \phi \in P$ and $t \in[1, T]$. (B.2) provides the dynamical equations for queue build-up and dissipation, and (B.3) ensures that at most one phase has the right of way at any time-interval. Note that the above formulation does not impose the safety constraints involving minimum green time and the clearance interval. Since these constraints involve the use of some combinatorial modeling techniques, we postpone their inclusion and comment on this simpler version. Incidentally, model (B.1-B.4) is the same as the one in Gartner (1983).

Provided the performance index $F$ is separable in the DP sense, the above control problem can, in principle, be solved using DP. Without going into the details of the recursion, we note that the stages of the recursion correspond to the intervals of time and the state variables in each stage correspond to $\left\{n_{\phi}(t)\right\}_{\phi \in P}$. It is important to notice that the number of state variables in the above formulation corresponds to the number of phases in $P$. For realistic traffic control systems which operate 8 phases, the above formulation leads to a DP recursion using 8 state variables. It is well known that such recursions (with multi-dimensional state variables) are not particularly efficient due to "the curse of dimensionality" (see Dreyfus and Law (1977)). For this reason, the traffic engineering literature has abandoned DP as a real-time problem-solving tool for intersection control (see also discussion in Gartner (1983)).

One of the other disadvantages of using DP for (B.1-B.4) results from difficulties associated with the inclusion of the safety constraints. For example, the minimum green time constraint requires the following

$$
\begin{aligned}
& u_{\phi}(t)=1, u_{\phi}(t-1)=0 \\
& \quad \Rightarrow u_{\phi}(t+1)=\cdots=u_{\phi}(t+\gamma)=1
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that the above requirement links together control variables associated with several time-intervals
(stages). This coupling destroys the stagewise separability required by DP. Hence one has to adopt a different algorithmic framework in order to incorporate the safety constraints.

In the following we present an integer programming formulation of the intersection control problem. Let $M>0$ denote a large positive integer. With the rest of the notation as introduced earlier, consider the following integer program (IP).

$$
\begin{array}{ccc} 
& \operatorname{Min} F(N, U) & (\mathrm{B} .5 \\
\text { s.t. } & n_{\phi}(t)-n_{\phi}(t-1)-M u_{\phi}(t) \leqslant A_{\phi}(t) & \forall \phi, \mathrm{t} \\
& & (\mathrm{~B} .6 \\
n_{\phi}(t)-n_{\phi}(t-1)+M u_{\phi}(t) \geqslant A_{\phi}(t) & \forall \phi, \mathrm{t} \\
n_{\phi}(t)-n_{\phi}(t-1)+d_{\phi}\left(n_{\phi}(t-1)+A_{\phi}(t)\right) & \\
-M\left(1-u_{\phi}(t)\right) \leqslant A_{\phi}(t) & \forall \phi, \mathrm{t}
\end{array}
$$
