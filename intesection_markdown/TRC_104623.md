#### Abstract

Autonomous intersection management (AIM) has been widely investigated over the past two decades as a solution for signal-free intersections in a fully connected and autonomous environment. However, existing AIM approaches lack consideration for crossing pedestrians, especially in a way that enables pedestrians to cross streets safely while optimizing upcoming vehicles at the intersection to maintain traffic continuity. To address this issue, this paper proposes an Automated Pedestrian Shuttle (APS) service system to transport pedestrians across the street. Using a coupled space-time network modeling method, an optimization model, which incorporates pedestrian ride strategies and aims to minimize the total crossing time, is first established for the APS operation scheme. A Shuttle-by-Shuttle-Planning (SBSP) algorithm is developed for model solving to determine the APS operation route and the departure time from each station along the route. Then, a timing schedule optimization model is proposed to adjust the departure time of APS at each station and the time of main-lane vehicles entering an intersection to avoid vehicle conflicts. To ensure that the main-lane vehicles can enter the intersection on time, a trajectory optimization model is proposed to provide a specific driving strategy for each main-lane vehicle. A rolling horizon strategy is adopted to bridge the different optimization time windows. Finally, the effectiveness of the proposed APS service system is demonstrated through numerical simulation experiments. The results show that (1) the APS service can effectively help pedestrians cross the street with minor delays to main-lane vehicles; (2) The dynamic-route scheme is adaptable to the needs of randomly arriving pedestrians due to its demand-responsive nature; (3) The service efficiency of the system is related to the number of APSs, and the more APSs, the higher the service efficiency under the premise of unsaturation; (4) The designed SBSP algorithm significantly reduces the model-solving time compared to the commercial solver, making the APS service system potentially applicable for field implementation.


Keywords: Autonomous intersection management, Automated pedestrian shuttle, Pedestrian crossing, Connected and autonomous vehicles, Space-time network

## 1. Introduction

Connected and autonomous vehicles (CAVs) refer to the combination of connected vehicles (CVs) and autonomous vehicles (AVs). Thus, they not only establish communication with other vehicles and roadside infrastructure through a diverse range of communication technologies but also operate independently of human drivers (Shiwakoti et al., 2020). The rise of CAV technologies has yielded numerous advantages for the transportation system, encompassing enhanced traffic safety (Nascimento et al., 2020; Yao et al., 2020a), improved traffic efficiency (Ding et al., 2020b; Talebpour and Mahmassani, 2016), and environmental advancements (Kopelias et al., 2020; Taiebat et al., 2018).

In urban traffic networks, the emergence of CAVs presents innovative solutions for intersection management. By collecting vehicle data through vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) and vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I) devices, the allocation of traffic signal phases can be dynamically adjusted in real-time, which helps mitigate traffic delays (Feng et al., 2015). Besides, CAVs can optimize their trajectories by leveraging traffic signal timing plans, enabling them to pass through intersections in an eco-driving manner without stops (Wei et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2017). Consequently, integrating vehicle trajectory control and traffic signals optimization becomes a viable option, offering enhanced system efficiency and promoting environmental sustainability (Feng et al., 2018; B. Xu et al., 2019). As technology advances, the penetration rate of CAVs is expected to increase. Once the penetration rate reaches 100\%, there will be a complete absence of human-driven vehicles (HVs) on the road. At this time, the need for traditional traffic signal-based intersection control will diminish (Wuthishuwong et al., 2015), giving rise to an autonomous intersection management (AIM) paradigm.

AIM is a multiagent-based centralized management strategy that uses a central controller or manager to unify the dispatch of vehicles within communication range. It can further improve traffic efficiency as compared to optimized signal control. The methodologies of AIM can be classified into two categories: reservation-based and optimization-based. In reservation-based methods, vehicles request a specific time and space from the central controller, which accepts or rejects the reservation based on predefined rules (Dresner and Stone, 2004). Dresner and Stone (2008) proposed the 'first-come, first-served' (FCFS) policy as a classical rule. According to this policy, the vehicle that arrives at the intersection first has the priority to reserve the intersection space resource, while a later arriving vehicle will be rejected if it tries to reserve an occupied space. Later on, auction-based (Carlino et al., 2013) and batch-based (Tachet et al., 2016) policies have been suggested as enhanced approaches to the FCFS policy. Reservation-based methods have gained popularity among researchers due to their efficiency and simplicity in computation (Fajardo et al., 2011; He et al., 2018; Li et al., 2013). However, such methods do not optimize vehicle sequencing through intersections, thus not ensuring system optimality (Yu et al., 2019). Moreover, it has been demonstrated that traffic signal control methods may outperform reservation-based methods under asymmetric conditions and high traffic demand (Levin et al., 2016; Yu et al., 2019). The optimization-based method replaces the 'reservation' mechanism with an 'assignment' mechanism, whereby the central controller directly provides the vehicle with an optimized travel strategy (Levin and Rey, 2017). Optimization-based methods typically strive to maximize the intersection throughput (Mirheli et al., 2018), minimize traffic delays (Wu et al., 2022b), reduce fuel consumption (Yao et al., 2023a), and other related objectives (Kamal et al., 2015). These methods can be formulated in various manners. For example, Yao et al. (2020b) introduced a two-stage model, where the first stage assigns intersection entry times for each vehicle to minimize traffic delays, and the second stage generates vehicle trajectories based on the output of the first stage with the consideration of eco-driving. In related studies, the two-stage model can be transformed into a bi-level model and solved iteratively (Zhao et al., 2021). Besides, constructing a single-layer model is one of the options for a wide range of scholars (Choi et al., 2019; Lee and Park, 2012; Levin and Rey, 2017; Li et al., 2019; Lu et al., 2021). However, optimization-based methods generally rely on mixed integer programming (MIP) models or
nonlinear models, necessitating significant computational resources and are challenging to implement in real-time scenarios (Ding et al., 2020a). Consequently, some studies have sought to strike a balance between computation time and results by identifying suboptimal solutions (Jiang et al., 2023; H. Xu et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020).

It is worth noting that most existing AIM methods overlook the presence of pedestrians. They assume that vehicles are the only road users and primarily focus on avoiding vehicle collisions and mitigating vehicle stops at intersections to enhance traffic efficiency (El Hamdani et al., 2020a). Yet, it is crucial to acknowledge that urban road intersections are significant areas with potential safety concerns for pedestrians, and incorporating pedestrian crossing behaviors into intersection management methodologies is essential.

Although constructing pedestrian overbridges can be a straightforward solution, their high cost and the insufficient crossing demand at various intersections often render such an approach impractical. Consequently, researchers prefer alternative strategies to address this issue within the two-dimensional plane of the intersection. Several recent studies have incorporated pedestrian crossing concerns into the AIM framework. Given that pedestrians cannot be controlled by the central controller, some studies have proposed reserving traffic signals exclusively for pedestrians. Niels et al. (2019) presented the integration of traffic signals for pedestrians into an FCFS-based AIM method and established two policies: fixed cycles for pedestrian crossings and the pedestrian priority policy. The former grants pedestrians the right-of-way (ROW) for the entire intersection area during the pedestrian green phase, which ensures a certain level of service. However, it lacks flexibility and can lead to inefficiencies, particularly in the absence of pedestrian demand. The latter policy builds an actuated control system. When a pedestrian initiates a crossing request by pressing the crossing button or standing in the induction area, the pedestrian signal adjusts its state based on whether the intergreen phase and the minimum green phase for vehicle passage are satisfied. Chen et al. (2020) developed a joint optimization model named AIM-ped to consider both pedestrian and vehicle ROW allocations. Specifically, the pedestrian ROW is established by activating the crosswalk, and this activation prohibits vehicles from moving if their paths intersect with the activated crosswalk. However, it is noteworthy that the AIM-ped may reduce throughput at the intersection due to the obstruction caused by conflicts between vehicle paths and crosswalks. As pedestrian demand increases, intersections using this control mode will progressively resemble conventional traffic light systems. To sustain the efficiency of the AIM system, El Hamdani et al. (2020b) enable the pedestrian crossing in the middle of each road, which decouples the road crossing from intersections. However, this does not change the result that the signal controls may reduce traffic capacity, even turning continuous traffic flow into interrupted traffic flow. Wu et al. (2022a) undertook an innovative approach to address the pedestrian crossing challenge at signal-free intersections by introducing an automated pedestrian shuttle (APS). APS is a connected and autonomous minibus that operates within and traverses the intersection. Pedestrians board APSs similar to buses and cross the street alongside the vehicle. This novel utilization of individual vehicles as transportation units circumvents the decrease in intersection capacity because AIM models maintain tight vehicle gaps to minimize delays. Furthermore, it averts safety concerns arising from pedestrians arriving during the green ped-signal and being unable to complete their crossing. While this first attempt presents a unique solution, there are some limitations. For instance, they did not incorporate APS dwelling time as a decision variable. By controlling the dwelling time, time wastage can be reduced and cooperative pickups by multiple APSs can be achieved, potentially leading to increased benefits.

In this study, we focus on providing safe and efficient pedestrian crossings at autonomous intersections while maintaining continuous and efficient traffic flow operation. First, we redesigned autonomous intersections to incorporate dedicated APS lanes. Then, an APS route optimization model is constructed based on two coupled space-time networks, and the decision variables include the order
of each APS visiting different stations and the dwelling time at each station. To ensure safety, collaborative collision avoidance strategies are proposed, including a timing schedule optimization model and a trajectory optimization model. A rolling horizon strategy is then introduced to bridge different optimization time windows. Finally, various numerical simulation experiments are conducted to verify the effectiveness of the developed APS service system.

This paper presents the following contributions.
(1) A framework for incorporating pedestrians into AIM considerations is proposed, which not only provides safe crossing services for pedestrians but also optimizes the movements of upcoming vehicles to ensure traffic continuity. This framework addresses the limitation of classic AIM studies that overlook pedestrians as traffic participants and improves the efficiency of vehicle movements at intersections of recent pedestrian-inclusive studies.
(2) A demand-responsive APS route optimization model considering pedestrian ride strategies is proposed. The model aims to minimize pedestrian time costs by optimizing the route of each APS and determining the appropriate dwelling time at each stop along the route. Besides, the operation scheme obtained by the model can deal with the uncertainty of pedestrians.
(3) An SBSP algorithm is designed to solve the APS route optimization model. Due to the complexity of the proposed model, commercial solvers face difficulties in solving it. The SBSP algorithm finds sub-optimal solutions in a decomposition and iteration manner, making it applicable for field implementation.
(4) Based on a set of illustrative examples, the efficiency of the proposed framework in various scenarios and the effectiveness of the designed algorithm are systemically evaluated. The results demonstrate that the APS service can effectively help pedestrians cross the street with minor delays to upcoming vehicles.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 introduces autonomous intersections with dedicated APS lanes. Section 3 proposes the APS route optimization model. Section 4 presents the collaborative collision avoidance strategy, including timing schedule and trajectory optimization. Section 5 introduces the rolling horizon strategy. Section 6 details various numerical experiments. Finally, Section 7 concludes this work.

## 2. Problem statement

This study aims to develop an APS service system to help pedestrians cross the street at autonomous intersections. This section provides a brief description of the studied intersection. Section 2.1 introduces the configuration of the intersection. Section 2.2 describes the lanes, including dedicated APS lanes and entry lanes in each approach. Section 2.3 outlines vehicle paths and conflict regions. Section 2.4 prepares the time cost within the intersection needed by the models in the following sections. For the convenience of readers, the key notations are summarized in Appendix F.

### 2.1. Intersection configuration

Fig. 1 illustrates an autonomous intersection with APS service. The intersection comprises an intersection area, entry lanes in four approaches, and dedicated APS lanes. The intersection area is a square with width $\mathcal{D}$, where vehicle conflicts may occur. Each approach has two entry lanes and two exit lanes, all having an equal width of $w$. The entry lanes are designed to all-direction turn lanes, enabling vehicles to turn in any direction (Zhengbing He, 2020). Four street corners, denoted by $K, K=$ \{A, B, C, D\}, provide waiting zones for pedestrians to cross the street. Each corner features four APS stations, represented by $K 1$ to $K 4$. The length of an APS station is $L_{S}$, which should exceed the length of an APS $l^{A}$ to ensure proper parking inside the station. For modeling purposes, the following assumptions are made.

Assumption 1: The APS does not differentiate front and back and can use either side as the forward direction. Besides, it always keeps one end on the station stop line when dwelling.

Assumption 2: Pedestrians are directed by prompts, such as variable message signs (VMS), guiding them toward the appropriate shuttle for a safe crossing of the intersection.

Assumption 3: All vehicles are fully automated and connected (Mitrovic et al., 2020). All of them can share real-time status information through V2V devices. Besides, the central controller can obtain all vehicles' real-time information through V2I devices (Yao et al., 2023b). Communication delays and packet loss are not considered (Chen et al., 2022). All vehicles are highly controllable and free of control errors (Zohdy and Rakha, 2016).

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/dfdd684f-3cd0-4a3a-b76d-a50f2b52ba25-05.jpg?height=736&width=1169&top_left_y=755&top_left_x=438)
Fig. 1. The layout of the autonomous intersection with APS service.

The autonomous intersection system comprises three primary components, as depicted in Fig. 2.
Pedestrians: Vulnerable participants and non-intelligent agents. Pedestrians arrive randomly at a street corner to await and cross the street using an APS. When they arrive, they are grouped based on their destinations. One conventional approach of grouping is to have different queuing positions at street corners for pedestrians heading to different destinations, so there are a total of three queuing points. Another possible approach is using smartphones or push buttons to send destination information to the central controller, which virtually categorizes the pedestrians. Once an APS arrives, the station is activated and an information dissemination device (e.g., VMS) located at the street corner informs pedestrians about the arriving APS. Depending on the APS operation scheme, the broadcasted information may include the full route of the APS or only the next station the APS is traveling to. According to different management strategies and levels of supervision, pedestrians are either required to follow specific boarding instructions or allowed to board and alight the APS according to their wishes. If a pedestrian fails to board the APS due to either the APS being at full capacity or it not being the desired APS for that pedestrian, he/she will wait at the corner for the next APS.

APS Service: APS services can be considered minibusses operating within autonomous intersections, which function as a dynamic pedestrian crossing system. It can adaptively update its operation scheme using real-time pedestrian demand data (or historical/forecast demand data) collected through devices like cameras, smartphones, or push buttons. During the scheme development process, it is crucial to prevent conflicts between different APSs and between APSs and vehicles on the main lanes. Therefore, the operation scheme will undergo collaborative collision avoidance for fine-tuning after APS route optimization.

Main-lane Vehicles: Main-lane vehicles are significant participants at intersections and initiate information exchange with the central controller upon entering the optimization area. By integrating all relevant information, the central controller determines entry times for vehicles to avoid conflicts effectively. Subsequently, specific driving strategies for main-lane vehicles are formulated based on these considerations.

Fig. 3. Operation flow of the intersection system.

The operation flow of the intersection system is detailed in Fig. 3. A time unit (e.g., 1 s) is initially defined. In each time unit, pedestrians or main-lane vehicles may enter the system. As APS route optimization and collaborative collision avoidance strategy formulation are performed at specific intervals, it is necessary to determine whether the two procedures should occur in the current unit. Firstly, whether APS route optimization is to be carried out is determined. If so, it is conducted, and the collaborative collision avoidance strategy (including timing schedule optimization and trajectory optimization) is immediately formulated. Once the collaborative collision avoidance strategy is formulated, the APS operation scheme and the driving strategy for main-lane vehicles are updated. If APS route optimization is not required, it is then determined if the collaborative collision avoidance
strategy should be separately formulated. If so, only the driving strategies of main-lane vehicles are updated. If there is no present need for both APS route optimization or a new collaborative collision avoidance strategy, all vehicles continue to travel using the current scheme until the start of a new time unit.

2.2.1 Dedicated APS lane

The dedicated APS lane is designed as a two-loop structure, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Stations $K 1$ and $K 2$ (where $K \in\{\mathrm{~A}, \mathrm{~B}, \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{D}\}$ ) are situated on the outer loop, while stations $K 3$ and $K 4$ are located on the inner loop. It is reasonable for APS to travel from the current station to any other station. However, to simplify the model and to reduce conflicts between APSs during operation, it is assumed that the APS is not allowed to drive across dedicated lanes. Further, APS is restricted from moving between stations at the same corner because such movement is mostly meaningless.

2.2.2 Entry lane at each approach

The entry lane consists of two parts: the optimization area and the buffer area. As vehicles can turn in any direction from any entry lane, it is assumed that vehicles will not change lanes. When the vehicles
enter the optimization area, the central controller starts exchanging information with them and optimizes the vehicles in the optimization area at a certain interval to develop a collaborative collision avoidance strategy. The collaborative collision avoidance strategy eliminates conflicts among vehicles (both main-lane vehicles and APSs) inside the intersection area and generates a driving strategy for the main-lane vehicle. Once a main-lane vehicle enters the buffer area, its driving strategy no longer changes. However, if the vehicle is still in the optimization area when a new optimization starts, it will again participate in the optimization and have its driving strategy updated. The length of the optimization area must be sufficient to ensure that each vehicle undergoes optimization at least once. Besides, the buffer area ensures vehicles have enough time to accelerate and decelerate to carry out the driving strategy. In our previous research (Jiang et al., 2023), the length requirements of both the optimization and buffer areas were derived. Another thing to note is that, before the vehicles are optimized, they drive by themselves. For modeling purposes, it can be assumed that they obey a specific car-following model, such as the Cooperative Adaptive Cruise Control (CACC) model, or simulate them using a Cellular Automata (CA) model.

### 2.3. Vehicle paths and conflict regions

It is assumed that the path of a vehicle within an intersection is predetermined based on its entry lane and intended turn, as shown in Fig. 5. For each entry lane and type of turning, there is a corresponding exit lane that is uniquely determined. Likewise, the path from one station to another is fixed for the APS. The vehicle path consists of a centerline, an inner boundary, and an outer boundary, as shown by the gray dashed line, the red solid line, and the blue solid line, respectively. The distance between the inner and outer boundaries is equal to the lane width.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/dfdd684f-3cd0-4a3a-b76d-a50f2b52ba25-08.jpg?height=585&width=1653&top_left_y=1388&top_left_x=204)
Fig. 5. Vehicle path inside the intersection.

The overlap of vehicle paths is defined as the conflict region. To avoid collisions, each conflict region can only accommodate one vehicle at any given time. For vehicle $i$, the minimum and maximum distances between the intersection stop line and the boundaries of the conflict region with vehicle $j$ are denoted as $\underline{u}_{i, j}$ and $\bar{u}_{i, j}$, respectively. The process to obtain $\underline{u}_{i, j}\left(\underline{u}_{j, i}\right)$ and $\bar{u}_{i, j}\left(\bar{u}_{j, i}\right)$ for vehicle pairs $i$ and $j$ are demonstrated as follows.

Step 1: If vehicle $i$ and vehicle $j$ have the same path, $\underline{u}_{i, j}=\underline{u}_{j, i}=0, \bar{u}_{i, j}=\bar{u}_{j, i}=d_{\text {min }}$. Otherwise, go to Step 2.

Step 2: Initialize an empty set named Key Point.
Step 3: Calculate the crossover points between the path boundaries of vehicle $i$ and vehicle $j$. Add them to the Key Point. If there is any turning part in both paths of vehicle $i$ and vehicle $j$, go to Step 4. Otherwise, go to Step 5.

Step 4: Make tangents from the center of the path turning part of vehicle $i$ (vehicle $j$ ) to the inner
and outer boundaries of the turning part of vehicle $j$ (vehicle $i$ ). Add the tangent points that are between the two path boundaries of vehicle $i$ (vehicle $j$ ) to the Key Point.

Step 5: If the Key Point is empty, it means that vehicle $i$ and vehicle $j$ do not conflict. In this case, it is defined that $\underline{u}_{i, j}=\underline{u}_{j, i}=M$ and $\bar{u}_{i, j}=\bar{u}_{j, i}=-M$. Otherwise, go to Step 6.

Step 6: Project each point in the Key Point onto the path centerline of vehicle $i$ (vehicle $j$ ). Calculate the distance from each projected point along the centerline to the intersection stop line. The minimum distance is noted as $\underline{u}_{i, j}\left(\underline{u}_{j, i}\right)$ and the maximum distance is noted as $\bar{u}_{i, j}\left(\bar{u}_{j, i}\right)$.

In Fig. 6, the path boundaries of vehicle $i$ and vehicle $j$ are shown in blue and green, respectively. The red area is the conflict region. The yellow dots indicate the points in the set Key Point, and the orange dots indicate the projection points. In Fig. 6 (a), the key points contain the tangent points described in Step 4, while only the crossover points exist in Fig. 6 (b).

2.4. Time cost within the intersection

In this section, we calculate the travel time for vehicles moving between different locations within the intersection, including (1) the travel time for APSs between stations, (2) the travel time for APSs from the intersection stop line to the boundaries of the conflict region, and (3) the travel time for main-lane vehicles from the intersection stop line to the boundaries of the conflict region. Travel time (1) is used as an input for APS route optimization (see Sections 3.1 and 3.2), while travel times (2) and (3) are inputs for timing schedule optimization (see Section 4.1).
2.4.1 Travel time for APSs between stations

The journey of an APS from one station to another directly connected station is considered a complete journey of a virtual APS (the concept of virtual APS is introduced in Section 4.1.1). To facilitate the modeling process, it is necessary to establish several assumptions.

Assumption 4: Generally, the APS travels between stations in three stages: acceleration, cruise, and deceleration. However, if the travel distance is short, it may be two stages without cruising.

Assumption 5: The maximum acceleration and deceleration of the APS are the same. The APS travels at maximum acceleration (deceleration) during the acceleration (deceleration) stage. At the cruise stage, the ASP travels at a uniform speed.

Without loss of generality, we introduce an algorithm to calculate the time $\Omega(L, d)$ required for the APS to travel a distance of $d$ from the station, as shown in Algorithm 1. In the algorithm, line 3 calculates the distance traveled by the APS from stationary to cruising. However, in the case of a short path, the APS may not have sufficient distance to accelerate to the cruising speed. Therefore, lines 4-5 give the actual acceleration distance and acceleration time of the APS. Since the travel is mirrored from
start to finish, the deceleration distance and deceleration time of the APS are the same as the acceleration phase. Lines 6-7, 8-9, and 10-11 give the time required for the APS to travel from the station stop line to the position when it is in the acceleration phase, the cruise phase (if any), and the deceleration phase, respectively.

```
Algorithm 1: Calculate the time for an APS from the station to a location along the path
    Input: path length \(L\) of the APS from the depart station to the target station, travel distance \(d\) from the
    station stop line to an on-path position, the maximum acceleration (deceleration) \(a_{\max }^{A}\) of the APS, the
    cruising speed \(v_{\text {int }}^{A}\) of the APS
    Output: travel time \(\Omega(L, d)\) from the station stop line to the on-path position
    \(x_{a d}^{A}=\frac{\left(v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}\right)^{2}}{2 a_{\max }^{A}}\)
    \(x^{a c c}=\min \left[x_{a d}^{A}, \frac{L}{2}\right]\)
    \(t^{a c c}=\sqrt{\frac{2 x^{a c c}}{a_{\text {max }}^{A}}}\)
    if \(d \leq x^{a c c}\) then
        \(\Omega(L, d)=\sqrt{\frac{2 d}{a_{\text {max }}^{A}}}\)
    elif \(x^{a c c}<d \leq L-x^{a c c}\) then
        \(\Omega(L, d)=t^{a c c}+\frac{d-x_{a d}^{A}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}\)
    else
        \(\Omega(L, d)=2 t^{a c c}+\max \left[\frac{L-2 x^{a c c}}{v_{\text {int }}^{A}}, 0\right]-\sqrt{\frac{2(L-d)}{a_{\text {max }}^{A}}}\)
    end if
```

Let $L_{i, j}$ denote the travel distances between stations $i$ and $j$, which also refers to the length of an APS path (see Table 1). According to Algorithm 1, the required travel time is

$$
\mathcal{J}_{i, j}=\Omega\left(L_{i, j}, L_{i, j}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}{c}
2 \frac{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}{a_{\max }^{A}}+\frac{L_{i, j}-\frac{\left(v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}\right)^{2}}{a_{\max }^{A}}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}, \quad x_{a d}^{A} \leq \frac{L_{i, j}}{2}, \\
2 \sqrt{\frac{L_{i, j}}{a_{\max }^{A}}}, \quad \text { otherwise }
\end{array} \quad \forall i, j \in I .\right.
$$

Note that, for the same station and stations that are not directly connected, such as D4 and A2, we consider the path length to be $+\infty$. Therefore, the time cost is $+\infty$ as well.
2.4.2 Travel time for APSs from the intersection stop line to the boundaries of the conflict region

Considering vehicle length, the distances traveled by APS $i$ from the intersection stop line to enter and completely leave the conflict region with vehicle $j$ are $\underline{d}_{i, j}=\underline{u}_{i, j}$ and $\bar{d}_{i, j}=\bar{u}_{i, j}+l_{i}$, respectively. Further, the corresponding travel times are $\underline{t}_{i, j}$ and $\bar{t}_{i, j}$. Denote the depart and arrival stations of APS $i$ as $n_{i}^{d}$ and $n_{i}^{a}$, respectively, the path length is represented as $L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}$.

In the case of the APS operated in the inner-loop dedicated lane, the station stop line and the intersection stop line are located at different positions. The distances from the station to the boundaries of the conflict region are $\underline{d}_{i, j}+L_{S}$ and $\bar{d}_{i, j}+L_{S}$, respectively. Consequently, the travel time from the intersection stop line to the conflict region can be calculated by subtracting the travel time from the station stop line to the intersection stop line from the travel time from the station stop line to the conflict region.

$$
\underline{t}_{i, j}=\Omega\left(L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}, \underline{d}_{i, j}+L_{S}\right)-\Omega\left(L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}, L_{S}\right), \quad \forall i \in V^{A}, i \text { is operated in the inner loop, } j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M}
$$

$$
\bar{t}_{i, j}=\Omega\left(L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}, \bar{d}_{i, j}+L_{S}\right)-\Omega\left(L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}, L_{S}\right), \quad \forall i \in V^{A}, i \text { is operated in the inner loop, } j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M} .
$$

For simplicity, it is assumed that the acceleration and deceleration processes of the APS operated in the inner-loop dedicated lane are to be completed outside the intersection area. Therefore, Eqs. (2)-(3) can be expressed as

$$
\begin{gathered}
\underline{t}_{i, j}=\frac{\underline{d}_{i, j}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}=\frac{\underline{u}_{i, j}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}, \quad \forall i \in V^{A}, i \text { is operated in the inner loop, } j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M}, \\
\bar{t}_{i, j}=\frac{\bar{d}_{i, j}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}=\frac{\bar{u}_{i, j}+l_{i}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}, \quad \forall i \in V^{A}, i \text { is operated in the inner loop, } j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M} .
\end{gathered}
$$

For the APS operated in the outer-loop dedicated lane, its station stop line overlaps the intersection stop line. Therefore, its time to the conflict zone is

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\underline{t}_{i, j}=\Omega\left(L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}, \underline{d}_{i, j}\right), & \forall i \in V^{A}, i \text { is operated in the outer loop, } j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M}, \\
\bar{t}_{i, j}=\Omega\left(L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}, \bar{d}_{i, j}\right), & \forall i \in V^{A}, i \text { is operated in the outer loop, } j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M} .
\end{array}
$$

According to Algorithm 1,

$$
\underline{t}_{i, j}=\left\{\begin{array}{cl}
\sqrt{\frac{2 \underline{d}_{i, j}}{a_{\max }^{A}}}, & \text { if } \underline{d}_{i, j} \leq x_{i}^{a c c} \\
t_{i}^{a c c}+\frac{\underline{d}_{i, j}-x_{a d}^{A}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}, & \text { if } x_{i}^{a c c}<\underline{d}_{i, j} \leq L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}-x_{i}^{a c c} \\
2 t_{i}^{a c c}+\max \left[\frac{L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}-2 x_{i}^{a c c}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}, 0\right]-\sqrt{\frac{2\left(L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}-\underline{d}_{i, j}\right)}{a_{\max }^{A}}}, & \text { if } L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}-x_{i}^{a c c}<\underline{d}_{i, j}
\end{array},\right.
$$

$\forall i \in V^{A}, i$ is operated in the outer loop, $j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M}$,

$$
\bar{t}_{i, j}=\left\{\begin{array}{cl}
\sqrt{\frac{2 \bar{d}_{i, j}}{a_{\max }^{A}}}, & \text { if } \bar{d}_{i, j} \leq x_{i}^{a c c} \\
t_{i}^{a c c}+\frac{\bar{d}_{i, j}-x_{a d}^{A}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}, & \text { if } x_{i}^{a c c}<\bar{d}_{i, j} \leq L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}-x_{i}^{a c c} \\
2 t_{i}^{a c c}+\max \left[\frac{L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}-2 x_{i}^{a c c}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{A}}, 0\right]-\sqrt{\frac{2\left(L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}-\bar{d}_{i, j}\right)}{a_{\max }^{A}},} & \text { if } L_{n_{i}^{d}, n_{i}^{a}}-x_{i}^{a c c}<\bar{d}_{i, j}
\end{array},\right.
$$

$\forall i \in V^{A}, i$ is operated in the outer loop, $j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M}$.
2.4.3 Travel time for main-lane vehicles from the intersection stop line to the boundaries of the conflict region

The main-lane vehicle enters the intersection area and travels at a uniform speed of $v_{\text {int }}^{M}$. Therefore,

$$
\underline{t}_{i, j}=\frac{\underline{d}_{i, j}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{M}}=\frac{\underline{u}_{i, j}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{M}}, \quad \forall i \in V^{M}, j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M}
$$

$$
\bar{t}_{i, j}=\frac{\bar{d}_{i, j}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{M}}=\frac{\bar{u}_{i, j}+l_{i}}{v_{\mathrm{int}}^{M}}, \quad \forall i \in V^{M}, j \in V^{A} \cup V^{M} .
$$

## 3. Determine the operation scheme of the APS service

In this section, we present the APS operation scheme, which can dynamically adapt its driving strategy in response to pedestrian crossing demand. Section 3.1 abstracts the operation scheme into the space-time network. Section 3.2 establishes a model for optimizing the operational scheme of APS considering the varying pedestrian crossing demand. Furthermore, Section 3.3 offers an analysis of the proposed model, and Section 3.4 provides a corresponding solution method.

### 3.1. Construction of the space-time network

To model the route of APS, we present two coupled space-time networks: the APS space-time network $G^{A}(\mathcal{N}, \mathcal{A})$ and the pedestrian space-time network $G^{P}(\mathcal{N}, \mathcal{E})$. In detail, the APS space-time network shows the operation scheme of APS vehicles, which presents the location of any APS $v \in \mathcal{V}$ at each timestamp $t \in T$. The pedestrian space-time network represents the origins and destinations of pedestrians along with their corresponding timestamps. It is a pedestrian transport solution tailored to the APS operation scheme to minimize pedestrian time consumption. It should be emphasized that it is inaccurate to state that pedestrians have a particular station as their destination. For pedestrians, their destinations are street corners, and which specific station they will disembark from depends on where the APS comes to a halt.

Fig. 7. Illustration of the space-time network.

As shown in Fig. 7 (a), an APS dwells at station D2 when the service starts. It begins heading to station C1 at time $t=3$ and arrives when $t=4$. After several times stop, it continues traveling to station A2 and arrives at time $t=8$. After another dwelling period, it moves to station B1, and finally, it returns to station D2.

As for the pedestrians, as illustrated in Fig. 7 (b), 7 of them are already on board at the beginning. Then, 8 pedestrians get on, and 7 pedestrians get off the APS when it dwells at station D2. When the APS arrives at station C1, 5 pedestrians board, and 8 pedestrians alight. After a few moments, the APS picks up 3 pedestrians and makes 4 pedestrians finish crossing when it arrives at station A2. Finally, when the APS arrives at station B1, all the loaded pedestrians finish their journey, but 1 newcomer gets on the APS and heads to station D2.
(1) APS space-time network

In the APS space-time network, the coupled space-time status that an APS station $i \in I$ at timestamp $t \in T$ is represented by node $(i, t) \in \mathcal{N}$. Besides, two types of virtual nodes, named source nodes $\left(s^{v}, t^{s}\right), v \in \mathcal{V}$ and sink nodes $\left(e^{v}, t^{e}\right), v \in \mathcal{V}$ are contained in the set $\mathcal{N}$ to make the network has the ability to handle open boundary condition problems. Each $\operatorname{arc}\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A}$ represents the movement of an APS from one station to another between an earlier timestamp and a later one (Yin et al., 2022). A set of connected arcs from a source node to a sink node forms a route, and each route is owned by a particular APS to represent its operation scheme. Specifically, the arcs can be categorized into three types: travel arcs, dwell arcs, and virtual arcs, as illustrated in Fig. 7 (a).

- Travel arc

Travel arcs indicate the movement of the APS between two different stations. A flow on one of these arcs represents an APS (or a number of pedestrians) move from station $i$ at timestamp $t$ to station $j$ at timestamp $t^{\prime}$, where $t^{\prime}=t+\mathcal{T}_{i, j}$ and $\mathcal{T}_{i, j}$ denotes the time cost of an APS traveling between station $i$ and station $j$. Note that travel arcs only exist between directly connected stations, since $\mathcal{T}_{i, j}=+\infty$ for non-directly connected stations and the same stations.

$$
\mathcal{A}^{T}=\left\{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \mid i, j \in I, t, t^{\prime} \in T, t^{\prime}=t+\mathcal{T}_{i, j}\right\} .
$$

- Dwell arc

Dwell arcs indicate the APS stopping at a station to pick up or drop off pedestrians. A flow on one of these arcs represents an APS (or a number of pedestrians) dwelling at station $i$ from timestamp $t$ to timestamp $t^{\prime}=t+1$.

$$
\mathcal{A}^{D}=\left\{\left(i, i, t, t^{\prime}\right) \mid i \in I, t, t^{\prime} \in T, t^{\prime}=t+1\right\} .
$$

- Virtual arc

Virtual arcs connect the real nodes and virtual nodes (source nodes and sink nodes) so that multiple arcs form a completed route. Starting from a source node, a virtual arc can connect to any real node. However, a virtual arc flowing into a sink node can only start from the nodes with the last timestamp.

$$
\mathcal{A}^{V}=\left\{\left(s^{v}, i, t^{s}, t\right) \mid v \in \mathcal{V}, i \in I, t \in T\right\} \cup\left\{\left(i, e^{v}, t^{s}+|T|, t^{e}\right) \mid v \in \mathcal{V}, i \in I\right\} .
$$

(2) Pedestrian space-time network

In the pedestrian space-time network, the node set $\mathcal{N}$ is the same as in the APS space-time network. The arc $\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}$ represents the transport of pedestrians from one station to another station. Unlike the arcs in the APS space-time network, the arcs here do not represent the route of pedestrians. The two ends of the arc indicate the space-time status of the pedestrian boarding and alighting. There are two categories of arcs: pedestrian transport arcs and unfinished pedestrian transport arcs, as demonstrated in Fig. 7 (b).

- Pedestrian transport arc

Pedestrian transport arcs indicate pedestrians who depart from station $i$ at timestamp $t$ and arrive at station $j$ at timestamp $t^{\prime}$. The connected nodes have to be stations that belong to the same loop of dedicated lanes but are not positioned at the same corner.

$$
\varepsilon^{T}=\left\{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \mid i, j \in I, \chi(i)=\chi(j), \phi(i) \neq \phi(j), t, t^{\prime} \in T, t^{\prime} \geq t+1\right\} .
$$

- Unfinished pedestrian transport arc

If the initial load of an APS is not zero, it means there are unfinished pedestrian transports in previous time windows. In the study time window, they can be considered as pedestrians boarding from $t^{s}$ at a virtual depart station $s^{v}$ and alighting at their target corner at any possible timestamp.

$$
\varepsilon^{U}=\left\{\left(s^{v}, i, t^{s}, t\right) \mid v \in \mathcal{V}, i \in I, t \in T, \eta_{\phi(i)}^{v} \neq 0\right\} .
$$

3.2. The route optimization model of multiple APS vehicles
(1) Decision variables

Before presenting the APS route optimization model, we introduce the decision variables involved, as shown in Table 2.

(2) Model formulation
Objective function:

$$
\min \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left((\varphi-1) t+t^{\prime}-\varphi|T|-\varphi t^{s}\right)+\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{U}} \lambda_{s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left(t^{\prime}-t^{s}-1\right) .
$$

Subject to,
Flow balance constraint:

$$
\sum_{\left(j, i, t^{\prime}, t\right) \in \mathcal{A}} x_{j, i, t^{\prime}, t}^{v}-\sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A}} x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}=\left\{\begin{array}{rl}
-1, & i=s^{v}, t=t^{s} \\
1, & i=e^{v}, t=t^{e}, \\
0, & i \in I, t \in T
\end{array} \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V} .\right.
$$

Station occupancy constraints:

$$
\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A}} x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \leq 1, \quad \forall i \in I, t \in T .
$$

Path occupancy constraint:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}+\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} x_{\tilde{u}, \tilde{y}, \tilde{t}, \tilde{t}^{\prime}}^{v} & \leq 1, \\
\forall\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right),\left(\tilde{\imath}, \tilde{\jmath}, \tilde{t}, \tilde{t}^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A}^{T}, \chi(i)=\chi(\tilde{\imath}),\{\phi(i), \phi(j)\} & =\{\phi(\tilde{\imath}), \phi(\tilde{\jmath})\},\left[t, t^{\prime}\right) \cap\left[\tilde{t}, \tilde{t}^{\prime}\right) \neq \varnothing .
\end{aligned}
$$

Vacant station constraint:

$$
\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{0 \leq k<t_{\text {buffer }}} x_{j, j, t^{\prime}-k-1, t^{\prime}-k}^{v} \leq\left(1-x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}\right) \cdot t_{\text {buffer }} \cdot|\mathcal{V}|, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V},\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A}^{T} .
$$

Maximum dwell time constraint:

$$
\sum_{0 \leq k \leq\left\lceil\frac{\sum_{\mathcal{C}}}{\min \left\{\alpha^{\text {max }}, \beta^{\text {max }}\right\}}\right\rceil+t_{\text {lost }} x_{i, i, t+k, t+k+1}^{v} \leq\left\lceil\frac{\mathcal{C}}{\min \left\{\alpha^{\text {max }}, \beta^{\text {max }}\right\}}\right\rceil+t_{\text {lost }}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}, i \in I, t \in T .}
$$

Initial status constraints:

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_{s^{v}, n_{\text {init }}^{v}, t^{s}, t_{\text {init }}^{v}}^{v}=1, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}, \\
x_{s^{v}, n_{\text {init }}^{v}, t_{\text {init }}^{v}+k, t_{\text {init }}^{v}+k+1}^{v}=1, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}, 0 \leq k<t_{\mathrm{opt}}, \\
y_{s^{v}, n_{\text {init }}^{v}, t^{s}, t_{\text {init }}^{v}}^{v}=y_{\text {init }}^{v}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V} .
\end{gathered}
$$

Transport and demand constraint:

$$
\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime} \mid \phi(i)=m, \phi(j)=n\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \leq \mathcal{D}_{m n}, \quad \forall m, n \in K, m \neq n .
$$

Boarding and alighting constraints:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\beta_{i, t}^{v}= & \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \varepsilon^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}, i \in I, t \in T, \\
\alpha_{i, t}^{v}= & \sum_{\left(j, i, t^{\prime}, t\right) \in \varepsilon^{T} \cup \varepsilon^{U}} \lambda_{j, i, t^{\prime}, t}^{v}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}, i \in I, t \in T, \\
& \beta_{i, t}^{v} \leq \beta^{\max }, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}, i \in I, t \in T, \\
& \alpha_{i, t}^{v} \leq \alpha^{\max }, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}, i \in I, t \in T .
\end{aligned}
$$

Pedestrian transport and APS route coupling constraints:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \leq x_{a^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot \min \left\{\alpha^{\max }, \beta^{\max }\right\}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V},\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}, t^{\prime}+t_{\mathrm{lost}} \in T, a^{\prime} \in \psi\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right), \\
\lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}=0, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V},\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}, t^{\prime}+t_{\mathrm{lost}} \notin T .
\end{gathered}
$$

Unfinished travel constraint:

$$
\sum_{\left(s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime} \mid \phi(j)=m\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{U}} \lambda_{s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime}}^{v}=\eta_{m}^{v}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}, \eta_{m}^{v} \neq 0 .
$$

APS load constraints:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\sum_{\left(j, i, t^{\prime}, t\right) \in \mathcal{A}} y_{j, i, t^{\prime}, t}^{v}-\sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A}} y_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}=\alpha_{i, t}^{v}-\beta_{i, t}^{v}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V}, i \in I, t \in T, \\
y_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \leq x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot \mathcal{C}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V},\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A} .
\end{gathered}
$$

Decision variables:

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \in\{0,1\}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V},\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A}, \\
0 \leq y_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \leq \mathcal{C}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V},\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A}, \\
0 \leq \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \leq \min \left\{\alpha^{\max }, \beta^{\max }\right\}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V},\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E} .
\end{gathered}
$$

The objective function in Eq. (17) aims to minimize the overall time cost of pedestrians, including both waiting and travel times. The detailed derivation process is provided in Appendix A. Constraint (18) corresponds to the flow balance relationship, which ensures that each APS occupies and only occupies one unique space-time path. Constraint (19) ensures that at any given timestamp and station, there can be at most one APS arriving, dwelling, or departing. Constraint (20) restricts two APSs from the same dedicated lane loop with the same corner pairs from occupying a road section simultaneously. Constraint (21) states that if an APS is scheduled to arrive at station $j$ at timestamp $t^{\prime}$, then station $j$ must be vacant (without any APS dwelling) for a period of $t_{\text {buffer }}$ prior to $t^{\prime}$. Constraint (22) indicates that the maximum time an APS can stay at a station is determined by the time required to transition from empty to full load (or vice versa), along with time lost (e.g., the time for opening and closing the door). Given the semi-open system nature of the APS, the initial status of each APS is predetermined. Therefore, constraints (23) and (25) ensure that APS $v$ is at the station $n_{\text {init }}^{v}$ at timestamp $t_{\text {init }}^{v}$, with a load of $y_{\text {init }}^{v}$. Constraint (24) requires the APS to stay at $n_{\text {init }}^{v}$ for at least $t_{\text {opt }}$ time intervals, and this time can be used to complete the APS route optimization. Constraint (26) specifies that the number of pedestrians transported by the APS service should meet the demand for pedestrian crossing, although due to capacity limitations, some demands may not be fulfilled. Constraint (27) and constraint (28) specify the number of pedestrians boarding and alighting the APS from station $i$ at timestamp $t$, respectively. Additionally, constraints (29) and (30) limit the boarding and alighting rates. Constraints (31) and (32) ensure that the pedestrian transports from station $i$ to station $j$ exist only when an APS dwells at both stations. The constrained set $\psi(\cdot)$ is specified in Appendix B. Note that the dwelling process includes the time for pedestrians to board and alight, as well as time lost. Otherwise, it is considered as a transit of the APS through that station. Constraint (33) describes that all pedestrians already inside the vehicle at the beginning of the study time window will arrive at their destinations. Constraint (34) elucidates the change in the number of pedestrians inside an APS as it passes through a station. Constraint (35) specifies the maximum number of pedestrians allowed inside an APS. Constraints (36)-(38) specify the domain of decision variables.

### 3.3. Analysis of the proposed model

The APS route optimization model is an integer linear programming (ILP) model based on coupled time-space networks. In this section, we first examine the rationale behind the constraint formulation. Subsequently, we analyze the complexity of the model.

## (1) Constraint Analysis

The route of the APS can be determined by constraints (18)-(24), yet it lacks a purposeful objective because the path variable $x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}$ is absent in the objective function. By applying constraint (23) and constraint (18), each APS can find a space-time path starting from its initial state and ending at the sink node. However, these paths between APSs are not guaranteed conflict-free at this stage. Conflicts for APSs can arise in three ways: (a) multiple APSs appearing at the same station, (b) two APSs traveling on the road in opposite directions causing a head-on collision, and (c) two APSs intersecting on different paths leading to a lateral collision. To address the first type of conflict, constraint (19) is imposed. For the second type, constraint (20) is introduced. As for the third type of conflict, since it can be relatively
easily resolved (e.g., by one of the vehicles slightly delaying its movement), it is not explicitly addressed in the APS route optimization model but rather reserved for cooperative collision avoidance with main-lane vehicles. Constraints (21) and (22) are not essential for route determination, but they narrow down the solution space. Consider a scenario where an APS has just picked up a group of pedestrians from a station, and immediately another APS arrives at the same station. In such cases, very few pedestrians will board the second APS, wasting capacity. Therefore, constraint (21) (and constraint (20) as well) ensures that no two APSs visit the same station consecutively within a short period. Additionally, when an APS stops at a station, any stop time beyond the boarding or alighting period will lead to unnecessary delay. Solutions introducing such extra delay time are typically not desirable, and by imposing constraint (22), these solutions can be preemptively excluded. However, it is important to note that this additional delay may sometimes be to avoid collisions between APSs, and therefore constraint (22) may raise the lower bound of the model in some cases. From a modeling perspective, constraint (24) is unnecessary and its existence may preclude optimal solutions. Nevertheless, in practical scenarios, the constraint is crucial because it ensures that the APS does not depart toward a new station in the next $t_{\text {opt }}$ time intervals, which provides time for the model to be solved.

To purposefully plan APS routes, it is necessary to integrate pedestrian flow onto APS space-time paths. It means that each arc in the APS space-time network not only represents the travel route of the APS but also indicates the number of pedestrians present in the APS. Constraints (25) and (34) can be seen as a semi-open pedestrian flow evolution relationship, and the progress direction is determined by constraint (35), which can be divided into two separate constraints.

$$
\begin{gathered}
y_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \leq x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot M, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V},\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A} . \\
y_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \leq \mathcal{C}, \quad \forall v \in \mathcal{V},\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{A} .
\end{gathered}
$$

In constraint (39), $M$ represents a large positive number. When $x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}=1, y_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}$ may assume any unconstrained value. However, in conjunction with constraint (40), the value of $y_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}$ must not surpass the capacity limitation of the APS. Conversely, when $x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}=0, y_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}$ must be precisely 0. Through this, the constraint ensures that pedestrian flow is only added to the selected space-time arc, thereby coupling pedestrian flow with the APS space-time path. Constraints (27)-(32) play a crucial role in integrating the APS space-time network with the pedestrian space-time network. They strongly dictate that the starting and ending nodes of pedestrians must align with the space-time path of the APS, and this alignment must occur during the non-time-lost periods of the dwelling phase. Like constraint (35), constraint (31) can be subdivided. Finally, constraints (26) and (33) set limits on the number of newly generated pedestrians within the study time window and the number of pedestrians from the previous time window. Without these two constraints, the objective function would lose its intended significance.

## Remark 1

The flow on the pedestrian space-time network represents the ride strategy of the pedestrians as it specifies the time when the pedestrians board and alight the APS. Since the objective function of the APS route optimization model is related to this ride strategy, it can consider the behavior of pedestrians when planning the route. As demonstrated in Section 6.2.1, adhering to this strategy achieves a high crossing efficiency.

However, strict adherence to this ride strategy is challenging, particularly in the absence of supervision. Pedestrian behavior exhibits uncertainty, as they may not follow the optimized ride strategy. Instead, they make decisions regarding boarding the APS based on their needs and the APS route information. Therefore, the optimized strategy can be regarded as a desired strategy, which deviates from the actual ride strategy of pedestrians. Since pedestrians tend to pursue short crossing times spontaneously, the deviation between the desired and actual ride strategy is within a controllable range. Combined with the rolling optimization framework (see Section 5), the model can handle the uncertainty of pedestrian arrival and ridership similarly to Model Predictive Control (MPC).

The experiments conducted in Section 6 further validate the efficiency of the proposed model under uncertain conditions.

In situations where pedestrians cannot adhere to the desired ride strategy, we can further mitigate the deviation caused by uncertainty by providing limited information to pedestrians. ### 3.4. Solution algorithm

Due to the complexity of the APS route optimization model, finding an optimal solution would be challenging. Therefore, we propose a heuristic approach to obtain a sub-optimal solution quickly.

Inspired by Team Theory (Marshak, 1974), we consider the APS route planning problem as a teamwork problem, where the APSs work together towards the common goal of minimizing pedestrian crossing time. The person-by-person approach, widely used in Team Theory, involves fixing the strategies of all team members except one and then determining the optimal strategy for that member based on the strategies of others (Malikopoulos, 2022). This process is repeated for each team member until no further improvement in team performance can be achieved by unilaterally changing strategies. Based on this concept, we introduce the shuttle-by-shuttle planning (SBSP) algorithm, as described in Algorithm 3. This algorithm begins by randomly generating a set of feasible solutions to ensure feasibility in subsequent iterations. A sequence of APSs is then established, and the first APS in the sequence is selected for planning. The operation schemes (the decision variable $x_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}$ ) of all other APSs are fixed, and the APS route optimization model is solved for the selected APS. This simplifies the problem since only one APS needs to be planned. The process is then iterated sequentially until the optimal objective function value remains constant for $|\mathcal{V}|$ iterations (or the process reaches the maximum iteration times). At this point, it implies that no further improvement in the objective function value can be achieved by changing the operation scheme of any APS. The current scheme becomes the obtained solution. Note that the initial solution generated does not consider solution quality, so each APS's operation scheme should differ from the initial one during the iterative process. According to the algorithm, the first round of iteration does not trigger the termination condition. Thus, during the first $|\mathcal{V}|$ times iterations (the first round), constraint (26) is excluded to speed up the solving progress. However, starting from the second round of iterations, constraint (26) must be added to the proposed model; otherwise, the meaning of the objective function would change.

## 4. Collaborative collision avoidance strategy

This section elucidates the methodology for preventing collisions among vehicles. By employing predetermined travel patterns for APSs and main-lane vehicles, collisions can be avoided by scheduling the entry time for each vehicle. Section 4.1 presents an optimization model to determine the optimal time
for each vehicle to enter the intersection. Subsequently, in Section 4.2, viable driving strategies are proposed for main-lane vehicles to align their entry times with the desired schedule accurately.
4.1. Timing schedule optimization for APSs and main-lane vehicles

Once the routes for the APS are determined, they need to work together with the main-lane vehicles to avoid collisions. In this study, we employ a central controller to design a collision-free passing strategy for both main-lane vehicles and APSs. It is assumed that the main-lane vehicles travel at the same uniform speed in the intersection and that the APSs travel within the intersection area according to the route determined in Section 3.2. The timing schedule optimization aims to determine the entry time into the intersection area for main-lane vehicles and the departure time from each station for APSs. Note that the delay caused by collision avoidance for the APS is expressed as additional dwell times. During the additional dwell times, the doors of the APSs remain closed, and they are ready to depart without picking up or dropping off any pedestrians.

### 4.1.1 Virtual APS

To facilitate modeling, we propose the concept of virtual APS (V-APS). The route of an APS can be considered as the relay of multiple virtual APSs. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the APS commences its journey from station D2 and progresses to station C1, then station A2, station B1, and ultimately returns to station D2. It can be split as follows: V-APS 1 travels from station D2 to station C1, then handovers work with V-APS 2. V-APS 2 completes the task from station C1 to station A2. V-APS 3 carries out the mission from station A2 to station B1. Finally, V-APS 4 shoulders the last leg of the journey from station B1 back to station D2. With the concept of virtual APS, the decision variables of the timing schedule model become the entry time of each main-lane vehicle into the intersection area and the departure time of each V-APS from the respective station. Here, the delay of the APS due to collision avoidance is expressed as the departure delay of each virtual APS.

The timing schedule optimization model is a mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model for collaborative collision avoidance among main-lane vehicles and APSs. The objective function (41) seeks to minimize the overall delay of main-lane vehicles and APSs. Specifically, Eqs. (42) and (43) specify the weights of different vehicles. The weight assigned to main-lane vehicles is determined based on the number of occupants in each vehicle. An average number $\ell^{M}=\left(\sum_{i \in V^{M}} \ell_{i}\right) /\left|V^{M}\right|$ is used for simplification in numerical experiments. For APSs, the weight is the sum of the ego V-APS occupancy and its subsequent V-APS occupancy, because if the ego V-APS is delayed, all its subsequent V-APSs will be delayed as well. Constraint (44) guarantees that the actual departure time of an APS from a station is no earlier than the planned time determined by the APS route optimization. Besides, it should not be earlier than when the optimization starts. Constraint (45) specifies that the time interval between the departure of consecutive V-APSs should be greater than the combined travel duration between the corresponding two stations and the dwell time of the following V-APS. Constraint (46) establishes a clear correlation between the departure time of the V-APS and its entry time into the intersection. Specifically, if the V-APS is located in the inner-loop dedicated lane, it accelerates to attain its maximum speed upon departing from the station. Conversely, if the V-APS is situated in the outer-loop dedicated lane, its station is aligned with the intersection stop line, and the departure time coincides with the entry into the intersection. Constraint (47) specifies that the earliest time of entry into the intersection for the main-lane vehicle is when the current optimization starts plus the minimum travel time from the current position to the stop line. Constraints (48) and (49) limit the time difference between two vehicles from different lanes entering the intersection. When $\gamma_{i j}=0$, vehicle $i$ leaves the conflict region first. On the contrary, when $\gamma_{i j}=1$, vehicle $j$ passes the conflict region first. Constraint (50) bounds the time interval between two main-lane vehicles from the same lane entering the intersection. Since lane changing is prohibited, vehicles follow the 'first-in, first-out' principle. Constraint (51) indicates that in the case of V-APSs that are not part of the same APS, if the target station of V-APS $i$ and the departure station of V-APS $j$ are the same, and V-APS $j$ departs before V-APS $i$ arrives, V-APS $j$ must leave the conflict region before V-APS $i$ can enter it. Constraints (52)-(55) specify the domain of decision variables.

### 4.2. Trajectory optimization for main-lane vehicles on an entry lane

After the timing schedule optimization, APSs depart from the station at the optimized departure times, while main-lane vehicles enter the intersection at the optimized entry times. However, the timing schedule optimization does not provide a solution for the trajectory of the main-lane vehicle from the start of the optimization until it enters the intersection. This is where trajectory optimization comes into play. It aims to develop a driving strategy for each main-lane vehicle to ensure timely entry into the intersection. Typically, trajectory optimization is formulated as an optimal control model, to reduce pollution emissions or improve driving comfort (Malikopoulos et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2021). In this study, we focus on minimizing fuel consumption and transforming the optimal control model into a linear programming (LP) model using the infinitesimal method (Li and Li, 2019; Yao et al., 2023a, 2020b). z_{i}[\tau] \geq-a_{i}[\tau], \quad \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\mathrm{init}}, \tau_{i}^{+}\right] .
\end{gathered}
$$

Subject to,
Kinematic constraints:

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_{i}[\tau]=x_{i}[\tau-1]+v_{i}[\tau-1] \delta+\frac{1}{2} a_{i}[\tau-1] \delta^{2}, \quad \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\text {init }}+1, \tau_{i}^{+}\right] \\
v_{i}[\tau]=v_{i}[\tau-1]+a_{i}[\tau-1] \delta, \quad \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\text {init }}+1, \tau_{i}^{+}\right]
\end{gathered}
$$

Speed and acceleration range constraints:

$$
\begin{gathered}
0 \leq v_{i}[\tau] \leq v_{\mathrm{road}}^{\max }, \quad \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\mathrm{init}}, \tau_{i}^{+}\right], \\
b_{\max }^{M} \leq a_{i}[\tau] \leq a_{\max }^{M}, \quad \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\mathrm{init}}, \tau_{i}^{+}\right] .
\end{gathered}
$$

State constraints:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_{i}\left[\tau^{\text {init }}\right]=x_{i}^{\text {init }}, & \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \\
v_{i}\left[\tau^{\text {init }}\right]=v_{i}^{\text {init }}, & \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \\
a_{i}\left[\tau^{\text {init }}\right]=a_{i}^{\text {init }}, & \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \\
x_{i}\left[\tau_{i}^{+}\right]=L_{C}, & \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \\
v_{i}\left[\tau_{i}^{+}\right]=v_{\text {int }}^{M}, & \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \\
a_{i}\left[\tau_{i}^{+}\right]=0, & \forall i \in V^{M, h} .
\end{aligned}
$$

Safety constraint:

$$
x_{i-1}[\tau]-x_{i}[\tau] \geq l_{i-1}+d_{\min }, \quad \forall i, i-1 \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\text {init }}, \tau_{i-1}^{+}\right] .
$$

Decision variables:

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
a_{i}[\tau] \in \mathbb{R}, & \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\text {init }}, \tau_{i}^{+}\right] \\
z_{i}[\tau] \in \mathbb{R}^{*}, & \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\text {init }}, \tau_{i}^{+}\right] \\
v_{i}[\tau] \in \mathbb{R}^{*}, & \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\text {init }}, \tau_{i}^{+}\right] \\
x_{i}[\tau] \in \mathbb{R}^{*}, & \forall i \in V^{M, h}, \tau \in\left[\tau^{\text {init }}, \tau_{i}^{+}\right] .
\end{array}
$$

The trajectory optimization model is formulated as a linear programming (LP) problem. Eq. (56)
represents the objective function, which aims to minimize the fuel consumption of main-lane vehicles on a specific entry lane. The original form of this function is defined as the sum of the absolute values of the acceleration of each main-lane vehicle at different time intervals before it enters the intersection (i.e., $\sum_{i \in V^{M, h}} \sum_{\tau \in\left[\tau^{\text {init }}, \tau_{i}^{+}\right]}\left|a_{i}[\tau]\right| \delta$ ). By minimizing the acceleration, we can effectively reduce fuel consumption (Yu et al., 2018). Constraints (57) and (58) linearize the absolute value of the acceleration, leading to the final form of Eq. (56). Constraints (59) and (60) represent the dynamic equations that each main-lane vehicle must satisfy during its journey. Constraints (61) and (62) impose speed and acceleration limits on the vehicle. Constraints (63)-(65) provide the initial state of the vehicle at the beginning of the optimization process, while constraints (66)-(68) define the state upon entering the intersection. Constraint (69) ensures a safe distance between adjacent main-lane vehicles. Since all vehicles are connected and autonomous, they can respond to emergencies simultaneously, allowing for a fixed safety distance between them. Lastly, constraints (70)-(73) specify the feasible range of decision variables.

It is worth noting that the model is independent for each entry lane, and the trajectory optimizations for all lanes are performed in parallel.

## 5. Rolling horizon strategy

The rolling horizon strategy, widely explored in previous research (Jiang et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2023a), is utilized in this study to ensure the continuous operation of the system. To optimize APS routes and facilitate collaborative collision avoidance strategies, a coupled rolling horizon framework is proposed. Specifically, APS route optimization is conducted at fixed time intervals, which are no longer in duration than the APS route optimization time window. However, the intervals for formulating the collaborative collision avoidance strategy are not fixed, because activating APS route optimization triggers the formulation of the collaborative collision avoidance strategy.

It should be noted that the design of the rolling horizon framework is affected by the solution times of the APS route optimization model and the cooperative collision avoidance model. Therefore, we make two assumptions before introducing the framework.

Assumption 6: Assuming a powerful computing device for dealing with the APS route optimization model, and the SBSP algorithm is capable of solving the model in $t_{\text {opt }}$.

Assumption 7: Since the methods to allow collaborative collision avoidance to be applied in realtime have been given in some studies (Xu et al., 2020), it is assumed that the solution time of the cooperative collision avoidance model in this paper meets the requirement of real-time implementation.

Fig. 9 illustrates a framework based on the two assumptions, which enables real-time APS route optimization. As shown in the figure, after finishing APS route optimization, collaborative avoidance begins. For each instance of collaborative avoidance strategy formulation, the subsequent collaborative avoidance strategy is expected to commence after a preset time interval (which may differ from the fixed time interval of APS route optimization). However, in case an APS route optimization occurs during this process, the collaborative avoidance strategy is formulated immediately after the APS route optimization is done, and the anticipated time for the subsequent collaborative avoidance strategy formulation is updated to be after a preset time interval.

- Assignment scheme: APS dynamically adjusts the traveling route according to pedestrian demand. In addition, the station equipment (e.g., tripod turnstile) strictly controls the boarding time of pedestrians with different ODs according to the results of the APS route optimization model. Pedestrians are aware of the route of the APS and may board it with a probability if the subsequent stops contain their destination. ### 6.5. Discussion of computation times

The APS route optimization model in this study is formulated as an integer linear programming (ILP) model. Commercial solvers such as Gurobi and CPLEX are commonly employed to solve ILP models. However, these solvers may have slow computational efficiency when dealing with large-scale
problems. Hence, to enhance computational efficiency, a decomposition iteration algorithm named SBSP is proposed in this paper.

### 6.5.1 Comparison between SBSP and Gurobi

We first compare SBSP with Gurobi to verify the computational efficiency of SBSP. We design two experiments. The first one is to solve both methods up to the termination condition, where the termination condition for SBSP is shown in Algorithm 3, and the termination condition for Gurobi is the solving time up to 1000 s . The second experiment is to solve SBSP and Gurobi for the same amount of time (i.e., the time needed to terminate the iteration for SBSP).

We generated a series of randomized scenarios, in which the number of pedestrians between each pair of ODs is the same. Four APSs are deployed in all scenarios, with two randomly positioned in the inner dedicated lane and two in the outer dedicated lane. The yaxis, presented in logarithmic form, indicates the CPU time associated with different scenarios. As observed from the figure, Gurobi always terminates at the time limit, indicating that it is hard to converge. ### 6.5.2 Potential for schemes to be applied in real-time

Although SBSP outperforms Gurobi with limited times, its solution time is far beyond $|T|$ (i.e., 60 s), hindering its real-time applicability. This limitation can affect the effectiveness of the APS service. In this section, we analyze the feasibility and application methods of different APS schemes in practice based on their CPU time. As mentioned in Section 5 and Appendix C, two thresholds are considered for the computation time, $t_{\text {opt }}$ and $|T|$. If the APS route optimization can be completed within $t_{\text {opt }}$, the selected scheme can be applied in real-time. If the APS route optimization can be completed within $|T|$, the selected solution can be implemented online using predicted data. However, if the optimization time exceeds these two thresholds, the selected scheme cannot be applied online and can only be completed offline.

Table 11 presents the average time required for a single APS route optimization, with the standard deviation shown in parentheses. The table also provides the total crossing times for pedestrians, enabling readers to compare the efficiency of different schemes. Note that the CPU time of the dynamic-route scheme is smaller than the statistics in the previous section because the demands are not always equal among ODs in each optimization in the simulation.

According to the table, on our personal laptop, the dynamic-route scheme can only be executed offline. However, by reducing the number of directly connected stations (as shown in Appendix E), the dynamic-route scheme can be executed online, albeit not in real-time, relying on short-term pedestrian demand forecasts. Similarly, the fixed-route scheme can be executed online, but achieving real-time execution is still challenging with current computing power. One potential solution is to increase $t_{\text {opt }}$, but this may compromise the flexibility of the APS service to some extent. Another solution is to enhance the computing power of the devices. We believe that the computing power of central controllers should be significantly higher than that of personal devices, enabling the fixed-route scheme to be applied in real-time, while the dynamic-route scheme has the potential to be implemented in real-time with the support of high-computing-power devices.

To summarize, the APS route optimization model is a large-scale integer programming model, posing challenges in acquiring optimal solutions within a limited time using Gurobi. SBSP, being a decomposition iteration algorithm, can find suboptimal solutions and generate better solutions than Gurobi in the same CPU time. Nevertheless, the current computation time of SBSP may not satisfy the real-time application requirements. However, this does not imply that implementing the APS service is unachievable. Three potential solutions are available: (1) utilizing more powerful computing devices, (2) employing dynamic-route schemes exclusively during specific periods (e.g., peak hours) while employing fixed-route schemes or scheduled schemes (fixed routes and dwelling times without optimization) during other times, and (3) selecting a dedicated lane layout with fewer directly connected stations, sacrificing some flexibility for computational efficiency.

### 6.6. Comparison with a signalized intersection

In previous sections, we verified the effectiveness of different APS schemes in various scenarios, explored the impact of the number of APS vehicles on service efficiency, and proposed strategies for possible application bottlenecks (e.g., computation times). In this section, we illustrate in which conditions the APS service is more applicable by comparing it with the signal control method. The baseline signal control method, derived from Niels et al. (2019), comprises two phases: the pedestrian green phase and the vehicle green phase. During the pedestrian green phase, the intersection is exclusively reserved for pedestrians, allowing them to traverse individual legs or diagonally across the intersection. During the vehicle green phase, the intersection performs a standard AIM scheme (Yao et al., 2023a), wherein the central controller decides the sequence of vehicle movements. If a vehicle cannot pass through the intersection at the current green phase, it adopts an eco-driving strategy and waits for the next green phase (Feng et al., 2018).

In the signal control method, the length of the pedestrian green phase is determined according to National Cooperative Highway Research Program et al. (2011), as expressed in Eq. (74). Referring to their study and Niels et al. (2019), the walk interval is set to be 7 s and the walking speed to be 1.2 m/s. In Fig. 1, the diagonal crossing distance is the longest at about 24.42 m, resulting in a minimum green time of 27.35 s. Considering that exclusive pedestrian phasing may be followed by an extended time, the length of the pedestrian green phase is fixed at 30 s . The length of the vehicle green phase ranges from 30 s to 120 s at 15 s intervals. Regarding the APS service (dynamic-route scheme), guided by the findings in Section 6.4, the services with 4, 6, and 8 vehicles are selected for comparison.

$$
\text { Minimum Green Time }=\text { Walk Interval }+\frac{\text { Crosswalk Length }}{\text { Walking Speed }} .
$$

## 7. Conclusions and Future Work

One of the key features of AIM is the uninterrupted flow of vehicles through intersections, which enhances efficiency. However, existing AIM studies that consider pedestrians mainly incorporate pedestrian signals, doing so may interrupt vehicle flow, which somewhat contradicts the principle of

AIM. To fill the research gap, this paper designs an APS service system for pedestrians to cross the street safely at autonomous intersections while ensuring the efficiency and continuity of arterial traffic. Specifically, a coupled space-time network modeling approach is employed to develop an optimization model of the APS operation scheme, which considers the pedestrian ride strategy and aims to minimize the total pedestrian crossing time. The constructed model is solved using an SBSP algorithm based on Team Theory. Once the initial APS operation scheme is obtained, the main-lane vehicles are considered, and a MILP model is used to coordinate all vehicles for collision avoidance. The APS operation scheme is then fine-tuned and becomes the final scheme. A trajectory optimization model is also developed to formulate specific driving strategies for all main-lane vehicles to ensure they travel on schedule and safely according to the collision-free strategy. Finally, a rolling horizon strategy is introduced to bridge different optimization time windows. Through numerical experiments, the following conclusions are found.

1. Within an autonomous intersection, the vehicle management system and APS service system function as interacting modules to meet the needs of main-lane vehicles and pedestrians, respectively. In this study, both modules demonstrate commendable efficiency. The vehicle management system ensures smooth traffic flow at the intersection, while the APS service system effectively facilitates pedestrian street-crossing. The efficiency of main-lane vehicle movements is primarily influenced by the demand for main-lane vehicles, but the efficiency of APS service is influenced by both pedestrian demand and vehicle demand.
2. In the APS service system, the assignment scheme provides the most efficient service, followed by the dynamic-route scheme. Due to their ability to respond to pedestrian crossing demand and adapt their operation schemes accordingly, they are superior to fixed schemes. However, the assignment scheme requires pedestrians to be able to get on and off the APS in a precise manner, which is difficult to achieve, especially in unsupervised situations. Furthermore, strictly enforcing the optimized ride strategy fails to account for the stochastic nature of pedestrians. For example, it does not accommodate the scenario where pedestrians may not be present at the street corner during the time of optimization but arrive before the APS's arrival. The dynamic-route scheme effectively addresses the uncertainties associated with the arrival and boarding patterns of pedestrians. Therefore, it is the best solution.
3. The efficiency of fixed-route schemes varies significantly depending on the chosen operating routes. If a fixed-route scheme is to be implemented, it is crucial to customize the routes based on different demand scenarios.
4. The number of available APSs involves a trade-off between pedestrian crossing efficiency and vehicle access efficiency. The efficiency of the APS service increases with the number of APS vehicles, with four being the minimum requirement to constitute an APS service system.
5. The APS route optimization model is a complex and computationally demanding task. The proposed SBSP algorithm can find a suboptimal solution in a decomposition and iteration manner, which creates a balance between computational efficiency and solution quality.
6. APS service is one of the optional management methods available to traffic managers to consider pedestrians. While it proves suitable for the majority of intersections, the decision should be tailored to the particular requirements of the manager. If ensuring efficient uninterrupted traffic flow on arterials is a priority, the APS service emerges as the optimal choice among the available alternatives in most cases.

This paper provides a solution for future signal-free intersections that take pedestrians into account. As a preliminary exploration of APS, some limitations exist in the proposed system. For instance, the proposed APS service system does not consider the energy loss of the APS. Unlike general public transportation, the APS is always within the intersection, and addressing its energy needs is the subject of future research. In addition, the proposed APS service system is highly customized with many settings,
including the topology of directly connected stations, the time interval for responding to pedestrian demand, and the length of the optimization time window. Since this study is a preliminary exploration of the APS service, the analysis of these related setup parameters will be conducted in future research. Subsequent studies will also conduct the APS service at intersections of different scales.

## Appendix A

The total time consumption of pedestrians can be viewed as two components: waiting time and travel time. We calculate the waiting time and travel time of pedestrians by dividing them into three categories based on their location at the beginning of the optimization and their availability to be served in the current time window.

Category 1: Pedestrians are located at the station when the optimization starts and can receive APS service during the current time window. In this case, pedestrians board the APS from time $t$ and reach the target street corner and get off at time $t^{\prime}$. Their total waiting time is

$$
\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left(t-t^{s}-1\right) .
$$

Correspondingly, their total travel time is

$$
\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left(t^{\prime}-t\right) .
$$

Category 2: Pedestrians are already in the vehicle when the optimization starts and can receive the APS service during the current time window. In this case, pedestrians have no waiting time, they arrive at the target station and get off at time $t^{\prime}$. Therefore, their total travel time is

$$
\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{U}} \lambda_{s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left(t^{\prime}-t^{s}-1\right) .
$$

Category 3: Pedestrians are unable to receive the APS service during the current time window, regardless of where they are when the optimization starts, and they have a total waiting time of

$$
\left(\sum_{m} \sum_{n} \mathcal{D}_{m n}-\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}\right) \cdot(|T|-1) .
$$

Considering that pedestrians are in a different mood when waiting at the station and traveling, we multiply the waiting time by a coefficient $\varphi$. Therefore, the total time cost can be regarded as

$$
\begin{array}{r}
\varphi\left(\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left(t-t^{s}-1\right)+\left(\sum_{m} \sum_{n} \mathcal{D}_{m n}-\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v}\right) \cdot(|T|-1)\right) \\
+\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left(t^{\prime}-t\right)+\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{U}} \lambda_{s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left(t^{\prime}-t^{s}-1\right) .
\end{array}
$$

After uniting like terms and rounding off the constant term, the objective function in Eq. (17) can be obtained.

$$
\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}} \lambda_{i, j, t, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left((\varphi-1) t+t^{\prime}-\varphi|T|-\varphi t^{s}\right)+\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \sum_{\left(s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{U}} \lambda_{s^{v}, j, t^{s}, t^{\prime}}^{v} \cdot\left(t^{\prime}-t^{s}-1\right) .
$$

## Appendix B

Given a pedestrian transport arc $\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{T}$, the constrained set $\psi\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right)$ consists of the following dwell arcs.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
(i, i, t+k, t+k+1), & 0 \leq k \leq t_{\text {lost }} \\
\left(j, j, t^{\prime}+k, t^{\prime}+k+1\right), & 0 \leq k \leq t_{\text {lost }}
\end{array}
$$

Given an unfinished pedestrian transport arc $\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right) \in \mathcal{E}^{U}$, the constrained set $\psi\left(i, j, t, t^{\prime}\right)$ consists of the following dwell arcs.

$$
\left(j, j, t^{\prime}+k, t^{\prime}+k+1\right), \quad 0 \leq k \leq t_{\text {lost }} .
$$

## Appendix C

When the APS route optimization cannot be completed in time $t_{\text {opt }}$, the real-time optimization cannot be implemented. Instead, the online but not real-time optimization strategy or the offline optimization strategy can be chosen.
(1) Online but not real-time APS route optimization

If the computation time for an APS optimization falls between $t_{\text {opt }}$ and $|T|$, the selected scheme cannot be applied in real-time but can be implemented online using predicted data. The modified framework is illustrated in Fig. 23. The change is that APS route optimization will be performed using short-term prediction data ahead of a fixed time interval, which must be greater than the model-solving time. Hence, the shorter the model solving time, the shorter the fixed time interval can be set, allowing for a longer overlap between adjacent time windows. Once the APS route optimization model is solved, the solution will be temporarily stored in the central controller. After the fixed time interval, the preoptimized APS operation scheme will be fine-tuned based on the main-lane vehicles (i.e., collaborative collision avoidance), and then it will be sent down to each APS for implementation. At this time, this downcast APS scheme has been determined, and the next APS route optimization model can be solved based on this scheme as well as the new prediction data. Therefore, this framework can be applied online, but the APS route optimization must rely on short-term prediction data in advance, rather than real-time collected data.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/dfdd684f-3cd0-4a3a-b76d-a50f2b52ba25-48.jpg?height=815&width=1564&top_left_y=1258&top_left_x=250)
Fig. 23. Coupled rolling horizon framework: online but not real-time.

(2) Offline APS route optimization

When the computation time for APS route optimization is greater than $|T|$, online optimization is infeasible, and only offline optimization can be performed. This means that the optimization needs to be carried out much longer in advance using the forecast data in the far future. Since the computation time for a single time window is larger than the implementation time, this solution can only cater to specific periods and cannot work 24/7. As depicted in Fig. 24, the pre-optimized APS scheme is implemented without fine-tuning at every fixed time interval. Consequently, the collaborative collision avoidance model only changes the driving strategy of the main-lane vehicles.

## Appendix D

Through the test, we identified two fixed-route schemes in a system with 4 APSs.

- Fixed-route scheme \# 1: Two APSs are positioned in the outer dedicated lane, while the remaining two APSs are placed in the inner dedicated lane. Within each dedicated lane, one APS travels in a counterclockwise direction, while the other APS travels clockwise. Specifically, APS 1 follows the route A1-C2-D1-B2-A1, APS 2 follows the route A2-B1-D2-C1-A2, APS 3 follows the route A3-C4-D3-B4-A3, and APS 4 follows the route A4-B3-D4-C3-A4.
- Fixed-route scheme \# 2: Two APSs are situated in the outer dedicated lane, while another two APSs are positioned in the inner dedicated lane. Within the outer dedicated lane, one APM traverses the path in a clockwise direction, while the other APS follows a counterclockwise route. In the inner dedicated lane, both APSs adhere to an identical route. Specifically, APS 1 navigates the circuit A1-C2-D1-B2-A1, while APS 2 undertakes the course A2-B1-D2-C1-A2. On the other hand, APSs 3 and 4 travel along the route A3-B3-C4-A4-D3-C3-B4-D4-A3.

## Appendix E

To further reduce conflicts and simplify the model, it is designed that the APS located on the outer loop can only travel toward adjacent street corners, and the APS located on the inner dedicated lane can travel towards both adjacent street corners and diagonal street corners, as illustrated in Fig. 26. In this manner, the dedicated lane operates in one direction only. However, different dedicated lanes may have overlapping portions that behave as two-way traffic on the roadway in the overlapping area. For example, in Fig. 26 (d), C3 and D4 are linked, but APS can only travel from D4 to C3. Conversely, APS can travel in both directions between D4 and B4 because they have two dedicated lanes, one from D4 to B4 and another from B4 to D4.
