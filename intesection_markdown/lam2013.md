#### Abstract

This paper presents a strategy for autonomously resolving intersection contention between platoons of autonomous vehicles. Each member of a platoon is represented by a dynamic model and a path tracking controller as well as a vehicle gap controller. These models and controllers are incorporated in simulation environments. Multiple vehicles in convoys simultaneously arriving at an intersection are simulated to ensure an optimized intersection negotiation taking into account a raft of widely varying requirements that exist in a public transport environments. A set of concepts such as initiative, infrastructure adaptation, and priority propagation are introduced and used to promote globally and locally optimal crossing patterns, avoiding vehicle stoppage in most circumstances. Results from a multiple convoy dynamic model simulation validate the strategy presented.


## I. Introduction

In the area of intelligent transportation, one of the most important preconditions for widespread adoption is the safe and efficient use of shared road resources. Given the advancements of autonomous vehicle technologies available today, their use in structured scenarios are fast becoming a reality. The advances in information technology makes it possible to tap into a vast amount of data that can be effectively used to achieve more realistic and better optimized solutions to situations such as public transport in convoys. With the vast amount of information available, their effective processing is now beyond the ability of human operators. The purpose of human operators has been increasingly shifting towards a supervisory role in unstructured operations and will soon be eliminated from structured operations. This is already achieved in well scheduled operations such as shuttles that operate between airport terminals. The driverless, i.e. autonomous vehicles are capable of making more informed logical decisions than would be possible by human operators. The high bandwidth wireless technologies provide complete situational awareness for each member of a convoy by sharing the entire measured or estimated state vectors of all members of all convoys. The wireless technologies also allow a telepresence to a human operator if intervention is needed. These conditions create very fertile grounds to promote cooperative driving, where maneuvers can all be negotiated in real-time and could even lead to automatic rescheduling of nominal daily transport routines.

Currently, in large cities, at peak traffic times long lines of busses, lined up one after the other is a very common sight. Their current operational style is a major contributor to the traffic congestion. Platoon formations on dedicated lanes or completely separate roadways is an attractive solution to future public transportation. A more realistic application of autonomous intersection negotiation is that along dedicated public transport routes such as the T-Ways (Transit Ways) that operate in Sydney, Australia. This is in contrast to building railway lines that are structurally too rigid. Railway lines also require a much higher capital outlay and are too intrusive to existing urban traffic situations. One of the major negatives of mechanically coupled vehicles is the inability to adaptively form gaps between each compartment. The work presented in this paper uses the controlled gap formation to achieve significant improvements in public transport.

## A. Previous Work

Methods for managing traffic flow such as ramp metering, variable speed limits, route guidance and vehicleinfrastructure integration have been regularly explored to combat congestion and infrastructure degradation [1]. In the emerging area of vehicle-infrastructure integration, approaches for efficient autonomous intersection control are of growing interest.

Dresner and Stone in [2] present a reservation-based autonomous intersection management system that is an order of magnitude times better than the standard traffic signal model in terms of throughput and traffic flow, as measured in [3]. The first-come first-serve approach is extended in [4] with a market-inspired cost function where drivers can pay to get through an intersection faster. Clement et al in [5] consider a platoon-signal coordinated approach, and Yan et al in [6] addresses the intersection problem in a more general sense with a dynamic programming algorithm for finding the optimal vehicle sequence through the use of a centralized controller. Coordination optimization through Lagrangian minimization was explored in [7] and multiagent reinforcement learning in [8]. This paper proposes a multi-agent pair-wise competition approach for producing optimal crossing patterns for convoys of vehicle negotiating an intersection. This approach differs to the heuristic algorithm for vehicle-infrastructure priority preemption developed for phased traffic signals in [9]. It will be shown that the proposed method surpasses the results achieved in [2] when considering vehicle priority.

## II. Modeling and Control of a Vehicle

To date, various models have been developed to model the behavior of an automobile with varying degrees of accuracy. For the discussion of autonomous public transport, a single-track nonlinear dynamic model (also known as a front wheel steered systems represented as a bicycle model) with longitudinal and latitudinal slip were developed to evaluate the accuracy and robustness of the path-following and car-following controllers described later in this paper. A good path following controller ensures the minimization of lateral deviations of the vehicle with respect to a desired path and a successful car-following controller ensures the maintenance of a time or space gap between the leading vehicle and the following vehicle.

Each vehicle in the platoon is simulated using its dynamic model and through this approximation of the vehicle states, the longitudinal controller described provides satisfactory time gap maintenance.

## A. Dynamic Model

Dynamic modeling of the vehicle-road system allows for more accurate physical simulation and is performed by estimating the various physical properties of the vehicle, road and tires. A simplified two dimensional single-track nonlinear model for the vehicle body, similar to the one presented in [10], is depicted in Fig. 1 and represented by (1).

$$
D\left[\begin{array}{c}
\dot{v}-\omega \Omega \\
\dot{\omega}+v \Omega \\
\dot{\Omega}
\end{array}\right]+G_{1}\left[\begin{array}{c}
F_{l f} \\
F_{l r}
\end{array}\right]+G_{2}\left[\begin{array}{c}
T_{f t}-R_{f t} \\
T_{r t}-R_{r t}
\end{array}\right]=0
$$

where

$$
\begin{aligned}
D & =\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
m & 0 & 0 \\
0 & m & 0 \\
0 & 0 & J
\end{array}\right] \\
G_{1} & =\left[\begin{array}{cc}
\sin \phi & 0 \\
\cos \phi & 1 \\
a \cos \phi & -b
\end{array}\right] \\
G_{2} & =\left[\begin{array}{cc}
\cos \phi & 1 \\
-\sin \phi & 0 \\
-a \sin \phi & 0
\end{array}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

In (1), $T$ represents traction or propulsion and $R$ represents rolling resistance. Forces, $F$ represents the lateral forces acting on the wheels perpendicular to the face of the wheels as the vehicle slips sideways. The slip angles are shown as the $\beta$ s. The steering angle is $\phi$. The components of the resultant velocity of the centre of mass are $v$ and $w$ in the direction of the longitudinal axis and perpendicular to the longitudinal axis, both through the center of gravity, respectively. The subscripts $f$ and $r$ represents 'front' and 'rear'. For details see [10].

The coefficients and constants used in this model are tuned to replicate the characteristics of a loaded 14.5 meter city transit bus. It has a mass of 16000 kg, and a maximum speed of 100 km/h. Maximum possible acceleration is $2.5 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$ and it has a maximum deceleration of 5.0 $\mathrm{m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$.

In the automated highway system application, autonomous control of a vehicle at a low level can be decomposed down to longitudinal and latitudinal control [11]. Longitudinal control governs the speed of the vehicle and imparts the ability for a vehicle to maintain the correct velocity or spacing from a preceding vehicle. Latitudinal control governs the vehicle steering and imparts the ability for a vehicle to stay in a lane and follow a specified path. A brief description of the longitudinal controller is provided as it is the mechanism responsible for time-gap maintenance between vehicles.

## B. Longitudinal Control

The desired speed and acceleration for a specific vehicle can vary based on enforced highway speed limits and the temporal gap to the preceding vehicle. Gap maintenance is handled using a PD controller that takes into account the distance between vehicles and outputs desired acceleration coupled with a gain. To compensate for the nonlinearities in the vehicle-road system, the function $\hat{M}(\alpha, v)$ uses a throttle map constructed using equilibrium point analysis to estimate the throttle position to achieve acceleration $\alpha$ where the vehicle's velocity is $v$. Equilibrium point analysis is a linearization technique that linearizes a nonlinear system around the system's equilibrium points, where asymptotically stable equilibrium points are expressed as
(5).

$$
\begin{aligned}
\exists x \in \mathbb{R}: \lim _{t \rightarrow \infty} \mathbf{f}(t, x) & =\frac{d x}{d t}=0 \\
\hat{\psi}(t) & =\hat{M}(\alpha, v) \\
e(t) & =\hat{\psi}(t)-\psi \\
u(t) & =K_{p} e(t)+K_{d} \frac{d}{d t} e(t)
\end{aligned}
$$

To achieve the desired longitudinal acceleration $\alpha$ in the nonlinear dynamic model, the speed controller must control the throttle position, producing error given in (7). The error returns the difference between the throttle position $\psi$ and the reference throttle position $\hat{\psi}$, which is defined as the throttle position function $\hat{M}(\alpha, v)$ in (6). The PD controller in (8) drives the throttle error to zero, which eventually eliminates any speed error.

## III. Intersection Contention

The intersecting segments of two crossing paths is a contested region of space defined as the the "intersection patch". This cross-section of both roads is a shared resource that is under contention by vehicles wishing to cross the intersection and is often handled by signal lights and stop signs. In a fully autonomous setting, a non-signalized cooperative strategy is proposed to allow traffic to cross in an optimal manner without unnecessary vehicle stoppage. The core principle of this strategy is to permit the most efficient vehicle, as defined by the objective function defined below, to cross first. Several concepts that influence the effective utility of each vehicles are introduced in the following subsections.

The premise of this intersection management strategy can be summarized as follows:

1) Vehicles within the contest range of an intersection present their crossing plans which includes at minimum the time span during which each of them plansto occupy the intersection patch. Vehicles can use a wireless communication technology such as a mobile mesh network to submit their crossing plans to a designated intersection authority.
2) A collision check occurs among all presented crossing plans, and if a collision occurs, a contest is held with all currently winning plans.
3) The contending vehicle must win all contests to proceed, where the highest utility in each contest is considered the victor. If the contending vehicle wins all of the contests, the contending vehicle's crossing plan is selected and those did not win are required to submit new crossing plans. If the contending vehicle loses at least one of the contests, the vehicle is advised to try again with a new set of times.

For robustness, additional rules are included.

- If the vehicle approaches the intersection and does not have a winning crossing plan, it must stop.
- Loss of communication with the intersection authority will result in the revocation of any currently held winning crossing plans.
- If the vehicle's estimated crossing times exceed the tolerance limit (ie, . 05 seconds) from the originally submitted plan, the vehicle's crossing plans are considered invalid and is required to submit again.
- Existing crossing plans that will occur in the immediate future cannot be contested. This is to prevent the preemption of vehicles that are imminently crossing.

## A. Winning Contests

We define the crossing time for a vehicle through the intersection patch as the time it takes for the vehicle's entire length to cross the traversed section of the intersection patch. The vehicle with the highest objective function $F$ as defined in (9) wins the contest.

$$
\begin{aligned}
F & =k_{l} k_{r} \frac{G}{T} \\
T & =\frac{\left(L_{v}+L_{i}\right)}{v}+T_{b}-W_{b} \\
T_{b} & =k_{c} \sin \theta+k_{f} \\
W_{b} & =i k_{i} \\
G & =H\left(1.0+w . k_{m}\right)+w . k_{a} \\
H & =\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i} p_{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $L_{v}$ is the length of the vehicle, $L_{i}$ is the length of the patch, $v$ is the vehicle speed and $p_{i}$ is the cargo value. In this scenario, each passenger in a vehicle has a value of one. Equations (11), (12), (13) and (14) of the objective function are described below.

Fig. 3: Illustration of the intersection patch between two roadways.

1) Buffer: A safety margin is included when considering potential collisions and is shown in (11). The minimum time between vehicles at an intersection when crossing perpendicularly is defined as $k_{c}$, the minimum time between vehicles when following each other through an intersection is defined as $k_{f}$ and $\theta$ is defined as the difference between the vehicle heading. The coefficients can be adjusted such that vehicles attempting to cross perpendicularly tend to lose contests more often, allowing platoons to remain closer together when moving through an intersection.
2) Initiative: Two vehicles compete for an intersection when they wish to use the same space at the same time. In resolving the contest, the vehicle planning to arrive first at the intersection is considered to have initiative $i$ of the difference in arrival times, and receives a bonus that improves the vehicle's utility ratio by reducing the time by a value proportional to initiative. This initiative mechanism, equation (12) helps settle contests between equal utility and promotes a tighter grouping of vehicle crossing times.
3) Infrastructure Adaptation: The contest mechanism can also be modified to bias contests in favor of specific directions of traffic. In an intersection between a congested high-traffic major road a low-traffic minor road, it may be globally optimal to artificially increase the priority of the high-traffic road to ease overall network congestion. Without an infrastructure adaptation mechanism, the intersection contests will at best produce locally optimal solutions without consideration of adjacent lanes or roadways. This optimization parameter can be tuned in respect to any traffic management strategies affecting traffic flows upstream. In objective function (9), $k_{l}$ is the lane bias and $k_{r}$ is the road bias. The lane bias coefficient is determined locally, and can be defined as a function of the number of intersection patches that must be traversed to completely cross the intersection.

While the lane bias $k_{l}$ can be used to assign major and minor roads, care needs to be taken to prevent suboptimal contention resolution. Fig. 3c depicts a scenario where a longer platoon on a minor road is approaching an intersection with a shorter platoon on a major road. If $k_{l}$ is too high, the heavier traffic on the minor road may be significantly impeded by the lighter traffic on the major road.
4) Priority Propagation: In a single-lane, the slower vehicles in the front are effectively obstructing the vehicles at the back wanting to move quicker. When the lead vehicle in a platoon loses a contest, that means that the entire platoon is delayed. Each vehicle has an assigned value, $p$, representing the priority the vehicle should have in negotiating an intersection. In pursuit of a global optimum, the priorities of all blocked vehicles must be considered in addition to the value of the vehicle vying to cross the intersection patch. If a high-priority vehicle such as an emergency vehicle joins the platoon at the rear, the value of the entire platoon is promoted and the vehicles blocking the high-priority vehicle will be allowed to proceed through the intersection more quickly. Each vehicle's priority can be determined by (14), where $n$ is the number of vehicles blocked and $p_{i}$ is the priority of each blocked vehicle

The impact of each vehicle's priority is reduced by a factor determined by its position in the platoon. This reduction is a locality-inducing mechanism that allows high-priority vehicles that are closer to the intersection to win the contest when other high-priority vehicles are at the rear of the competing platoon.

Fig. 3b which depicts a multi-lane intersection, the platoon moving in the North-South direction will block two lanes of East-West traffic. It varies from Fig. 3c such that the major road has heavier traffic, but the platoon lengths are shorter. Assuming that the vehicle characteristics are the same, platoon-wide priority propagation will significantly favor longer platoon lengths for intersection contention. However, in this multi-lane scenario where traffic in the East-West direction consists of shorter platoon lengths, the platoon of three vehicles traveling in the North-South direction will tend to win and block the group of four vehicles traveling in the East-West direction. To properly resolve this contest, we can extend priority propagation to include neighboring lanes.

5) Waiting: Vehicles that lose a contest are required to submit another crossing plan, typically at a later time. This additional waiting time, $w$, is added to the vehicle's time gap to the preceding vehicle. To prevent a slow-moving low-value vehicle from losing contests indefinitely, a bonus is added to the vehicle's value promoting the vehicle until it has sufficient priority to win the intersection contest. In (13), $k_{m}$ is the wait time multiplier coefficient and $k_{a}$ is the wait time additive coefficient.

## IV. Analysis of Results

Results from simulations successfully validated the cooperative contention strategy proposed in this paper. Using the dynamic model and controllers described in section II, the intersection management strategy described in section III was implemented in C++. The scenario had two platoons staged about 600m away from the intersection moving at about 75kph towards a simple perpendicular intersection similar to Fig. 3a. Each vehicle carries the same non-zero payload value. Platoon 2 is given a slight initiative and is closer to the intersection. As they enter the contest range in the first few seconds, vehicles submit their crossing plans wirelessly and begin to slow down to adjust to the contest results. The contests are held continuously up until the vehicle passes the intersection, resulting in the final crossing pattern as shown in Fig. 4. Parameters were $k_{i}=$ $0.1, k_{c}=1.5 \mathrm{~s}, k_{a}=0.1, k_{l}=1$ and $k_{r}=1$.

In the presented simulation results, platoon two is initially slightly closer to the intersection patch and therefore has initiative. As platoons one and two are equal otherwise, the leading vehicle 2a in platoon 2 does not have to wait and can drive full speed through the intersection. Vehicle 1a, the leading vehicle in platoon 1, in comparison to vehicle 2b has the initiative and has higher priority (due to priority propagation), and therefore becomes the second vehicle to cross the intersection. While vehicle 2b has initiative on vehicle 1b, it is insufficient to compensate for the crossing safety time requirements from the proposed perpendicular crossing. In this cooperative strategy, it is deemed more optimal for vehicle 1b to closely follow vehicle 1a than to allow 2b to cross in between.

## B. Conventional Signaled Traffic

We establish the metric "delayed-value" described below, to assist in the performance comparison between autonomous cooperative strategy presented in this paper and a traditional traffic light controlled intersection.

$$
M=\sum q p
$$

where $q$ is the incurred time delay from waiting and $p$ is the priority value of each vehicle. In practice, the red phase duration of a traffic light can vary drastically based on various factors and can be as long as a few minutes in length. The timing of the traffic lights were manually optimised to remain within realistic values while at the same time making them as competitive as possible. With the presented scenario, no combination of signal timings were found to produce a better result than the cooperatively negotiated results in Table I. The best case scenario obtained through manual tuning resulted in the platoons taking 19 seconds to pass through the intersection, roughly two times slower than the presented autonomously coordinated method.

## C. Varying Payload Values

To evaluate the effect of payload values in intersection passing order, vehicle payload values were modified such that the last vehicle in one of the platoons carried ten times the passengers of the other vehicles. The delayedvalue metric is used again to evaluate the contest strategy performance against a pure first-come-first-serve system as used in [2], which is represented as the baseline where vehicle payloads are not considered. With the exception of one high value vehicle test, Fig. 6 shows that the value-sensitive approach used by the strategy presented in this paper consistently outperforms the baseline valueinsensitive approach. For the high value vehicle "2a" test, the increase in value did not affect the passing order as it was already the first vehicle to pass through the intersection as shown in Table I.

## V. Conclusions

This paper has described a method for the deterministic autonomous and cooperative resolution of intersection contention between platoons of autonomous vehicles. Through experimentation, the method of crossing plan generation was observed to significantly alter contest results. Some error was observed in the vehicle's longitudinal controller due to overshoot and settling times, but was within acceptable tolerance. Future work with confidence models may result in improved crossing plan generation. Additional simulation with alternative platoon models, more lanes and higher traffic densities needs to be performed to assess large scale feasibility. While opportunities for further improvements exist, overall the results observed here are promising and demonstrate a coordinated method of sharing an intersection between platoons of vehicles. This research will serve as a base for future studies about multi-agent vehicular interactions.
