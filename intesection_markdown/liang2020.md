#### Abstract

This paper extends a real-time, Connected Vehicle-based traffic signal control algorithm at isolated intersections to balance between two competing intersection objectives: efficiency and equity. In this approach, a central controller is used to collect real-time locations of connected vehicles at regular intervals, which can then be used to also identify the existence of some of the non-connected vehicles. The control algorithm then aims to optimize the discharge sequence of naturally occurring platoons of vehicles based on their proximity. Specifically, the strategy selects the platoon discharge sequence-and associated signal phase and timing plan-that minimizes average vehicle delay (measure of efficiency) while limiting the maximum delay any individual vehicle may experience (measure of equity). The latter objective is only possible with detailed vehicle-level information available from connected vehicles. The results show that without the threshold on maximum individual vehicle delay, average delay is often minimized at the expense of very large delays imposed onto some vehicles. By implementing a threshold, both the maximum vehicle delay and the distribution of individual vehicle delays-as measured by the standard deviation and a common measure of population inequity, the Gini coefficient-can be improved, often with only negligible impacts to intersection efficiency. However, the tradeoff between equity and efficiency becomes more significant as the maximum vehicle delay threshold decreases. Sensitivity tests show that this control algorithm works well for different total traffic demands and different demand patterns. The proposed algorithm is also effective under imperfect connected vehicle penetration rates when the connected vehicles make up more than 40\% of the traffic stream. The results suggest that the proposed strategy can help significantly reduce long delays and inequitable treatment of vehicles at an intersection when vehicle-level information is available to a signal controller.


## 1. Introduction

Traffic signals help vehicles move through intersections in a safe and orderly manner by dynamically changing the right of way assigned to conflicting traffic streams at regular intervals. However, by temporarily restricting capacity to specific movements, signals also serve as the most common and significant bottlenecks in urban areas, leading to travel delays, excess fuel consumption and harmful environmental emissions (Denney et al., 2012). Various methods have been proposed to select signal timings in a way that provides safe vehicle movement while reducing the negative impacts imparted onto vehicles. These methods range from the use of unique fixed signal timing plans during different times of the day based on historical data, to actuated signals that adjust signal

[^0]timings within a set range based on actual vehicle arrival patterns, and finally adaptive signal control schemes that collect traffic performance measures-such as vehicle flow, density and queue length-and adjust signal plans in real time to improve traffic flow (Koonce and Rodegerdts, 2008).

Adaptive signal control schemes can have different objectives including increasing capacity, reducing delay, reducing emissions, or improving arterial bandwidth (Stevanovic, 2010). However, improving one objective can often can come at the expense of other important performance measures, particularly the equitable distribution of delays across the vehicles using the intersection. For example, minimizing total vehicle delay might impart unreasonably large delays to some vehicles traveling on lesser-used movements. In light of this, this study extends an existing signal control algorithm developed by the authors to explicitly consider the tradeoff that exists between total vehicle delay experienced at an isolated intersection and the maximum delay individual vehicles might experience, which is used as a surrogate for delay equity, when routes do not change with signal timing decisions. The algorithm uses CV information to estimate individual vehicle delay and identify optimal signal timings that minimize the total delay experienced by all vehicles subject to constraints on the maximum delay imparted onto any one vehicle. Simulation tests under full and partial CV penetration suggest that: (1) maximum individual vehicle delay serves as an appropriate surrogate for more complicated equity measures that are difficult to incorporate into a signal timing optimization algorithm; and, (2) that the proposed strategy can be effective at improving the equitable distribution of vehicle delays with negligible increases in total vehicle delay. Such a strategy might thus be very effective in practice when CV technology is more prevalent.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. First, a literature review is presented followed by the introduction of the signal control strategy, including the features that allow for more equitable distribution of individual vehicle delays. Then, simulation test of the proposed algorithm are used to show that by restricting the maximum delay experienced by any signal vehicle, the distribution of individual vehicle delays can become more equitable. Next, sensitivity tests are performed under both full and partial CV penetration. Finally, conclusion remarks are provided.

## 2. Literature review

A variety of methods have been used to optimize adaptive signal timings. These range from more traditional optimization strategies, such as mathematical programming or dynamic programming that rely on analytical relationships between signal timings and intersection performance (Mirchandani and Head, 2001; Lin and Wang, 2004; Cai et al., 2009; Mirheli et al., 2018; Mohebifard et al., 2019), to more advanced strategies, such as reinforcement learning that seeks to uncover hidden relationships between control actions and outcomes (Abdulhai et al., 2003; Cai et al., 2009; Li et al., 2016; Prashanth and Bhatnagar, 2011; Wei et al., 2019). The most common objective considered in these optimization frameworks is to reduce total vehicle delay (Trabia et al., 1999; Wolshon and Taylor, 1999; Mirchandani and Head, 2001; Gartner et al., 2002; Abdulhai et al., 2003; Abbas et al., 2007; Liu et al., 2007; Tubaishat et al., 2007; Cai et al., 2009). Other objectives that are indirectly related to vehicle delay have also been considered, such as minimizing total person delay (Dion and Hellinga, 2002; Christofa and Skabardonis, 2012; Christofa et al., 2013; Ma et al., 2014) - which is a weighted sum of total vehicle delay - minimizing average queue time or queue lengths (Abu-Lebdeh and Benekohal, 2003; Jang et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2015), or maximizing total vehicle throughput or signal progression (Gartner and Stamatiadis, 2002; Lin et al., 2009; Putha et al., 2012; Wongpiromsarn et al., 2012). However, other unique objectives have also been considered, including improving safety (Stevanovic et al., 2013; Stevanovic et al., 2015) minimizing environmental impacts such as fuel consumption or vehicular emissions (Chen and Yu, 2007; Kwak et al., 2012; Han et al., 2016; Jiang et al., 2017) or minimizing imbalances in queues experienced across individual approaches/movements (Aboudolas et al., 2007; Aboudolas et al., 2009; Xin et al., 2013; Tong et al., 2015).

To address multiple criteria at the same time, several studies have attempted to simultaneously consider multiple objectives when optimizing signal timings. The solution to a multiobjective optimization comprises of many different solutions that form the Pareto frontier, i.e., all the solutions where it is not possible to simultaneously improve all objectives. At the most basic level, these unique objectives can be combined into a single objective using weights on each (Gartner et al., 1991; Sharma et al., 2009; Khamis et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2016). For example, Zhao et al. (2016) converted travel time and fuel consumption into a monetary value and optimized the signal timing setting of an isolated intersection using vehicle-to-infrastructure communication information.

While doing so simplifies the problem, it may not be able to reveal tradeoffs between the objectives or opportunities to improve one objective with slight increases to another. Some other methods use bounds on decision variables to achieve desired relationships for other objectives. Zhang and Wang (2011) used minimum and maximum constraints on green times to avoid cycle failures and ensure traffic throughput, respectively, while Li et al. (2013) aimed at maximizing throughput while ensuring that the queue lengths remain below a pre-defined threshold. However, it is difficult to understand the tradeoff among different objectives when the decision space is reduced.

Multi-objective approaches that can reveal the tradeoffs between various objectives have also been considered. These methods have been applied to study the relationship between vehicle delay, safety performance, and environmental impacts (Wei et al., 2001; Sun et al., 2003; Zhou et al., 2008; Park et al., 2009). Sun et al., (2003) conducted one of the first bi-objective optimizations in signal timing research to identify the tradeoff between average delay and number of stops when determining the effective green time at isolated intersections. Branke et al. (2007) aimed at optimizing actuated signal timings by considering the tradeoff between number of stops and travel times, and also average stop times for vehicles and pedestrians. Stevanovic et al. (2013) aimed at optimizing signal timings along an arterial and used improving safety, measured through surrogates using simulation, and maximizing throughput as two objectives, and Stevanovic et al. (2015) added a third objective which is to minimize fuel consumption to create a 3D Pareto frontier.

Several studies have also considered the equity of delay at intersections. Lertworawanich et al. (2011) developed a multi-objective signal optimization scheme for oversaturated networks, where the objectives considered include minimizing spillover, maximizing throughput and minimizing delay inequity. In that paper delay inequity is measured as the difference between the maximum and minimum average delay of different links. Similarly, Hitchcock and Gayah (2018) considered the tradeoff between vehicle delay and the delay imbalance across individual movements/approaches at the intersection-termed vehicle delay inequity-as well as other objectives and proposed a multi-objective optimization framework to explore these tradeoffs. Kesur (2010) considered a multiobjective optimization for minimizing both overall delay and delay imbalance. The delay imbalance is measured as the maximum standardized route delay, defined as the route that has the largest average delay divided by the length of the route. These two studies found that, in some cases, delay inequity amongst movements could be improved with small increases to average vehicle delay at the intersection to more equitably distribute delays at the intersections. However, none of these methods could not consider equity in delay amongst individual vehicles since only aggregate, detector-level information was used.

The availability of Connected Vehicle (CV) technology provides more detailed data that can be used to optimize traffic signal timings. Similar to traditional, non-CV-based adaptive signal control strategies, the majority of CV-based algorithms seek to minimize average vehicle delay (e.g., Jia et al., 2007; He et al., 2012; Lee et al., 2013; Pandit et al., 2013; Guler et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2016; Stebbins et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2016; Beak et al., 2017). However, other objectives have been considered that leverage the individual vehicle-level data that are available in a CV environment. For example, Liang et al. (2019) studied the effectiveness of providing speed guidance to human-driven CVs to reduce the total number of stopping maneuvers at an intersection. A summary of CV based traffic signal control algorithms can be found in Guo et al., 2019. With respect to equity, Feng et al. (2015) is notable as detailed CV information was used to balance queue lengths by minimizing the maximum vehicle queue observed among all lanes. This appears to be the first study that uses vehicle-level information to identify signal timings to provide equity at intersections. However, only a single objective is considered at a time so opportunities to improve equity without significant efficiency losses (i.e., minimal increases in vehicle delays) cannot be identified.

## 3. Methodology

This section describes the proposed methodology to develop an equitable signal control plan using CV information. First, the intersection layout and vehicle types are introduced. Then, the basic signal control algorithm used to minimize total vehicle delay is described, including the proposed feature that promotes more equitable vehicle delay distribution. Note that only qualitative descriptions are provided for components of the algorithm that are covered in detail in existing studies (Guler et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2016; Liang et al., 2018).

### 3.1. Scenario considered

We consider here a signalized intersection with four approaches. Although we assume that the signal is isolated (i.e., there are no intersections upstream/downstream of the subject intersection), the proposed algorithm can be readily applied to individual intersections adjacent to other intersections, such as those along an arterial. The most salient feature is that signal timing decisions at the intersection are made independently of those that might be made at adjacent intersections. Each approach consists of multiple leftturning lanes, through lanes, and right-turning lanes. It is assumed that lane changing is not allowed near the intersection and that conflicting left turns are only served from dedicated left-turning lanes. Non-conflicting right turns can be served either from dedicated right-turning lanes or together with through vehicles. This paper focuses on the latter case for simplicity; however, incorporating dedicated right-turning lanes is straightforward and will not change the overall results. An example of intersection layout with two through lanes and two left-turn lanes on each approach is illustrated on the left hand side of Fig. 1.

### 3.2. Proposed signal control algorithm

The proposed algorithm is illustrated graphically using a flowchart in Fig. 2 and builds upon the signal control algorithm proposed in previous studies (Guler et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2016; Liang et al., 2018; Liang et al., 2019). The left-hand side illustrates the overall steps of the algorithm, while the right-hand side describes the method used to identify the optimal signal timing plan with equity considerations. A pseudocode of the control algorithm is shown in Table 1.

The first step converts the subject intersection into a hypothetical intersection that is used to identify vehicles that would naturally discharge through the intersection together, i.e., vehicle platoons. Without loss of generality, consider that four phase options are available at the signal: north-south through and right turns, north-south left-turns, east-west through and right turns, and east-west left-turns. First, vehicles on lanes traveling in the same direction on the same approach are combined into one hypothetical lane; e.g., see Step 1A in Fig. 1 in which vehicles 6 and 7 are placed into a new hypothetical lane. Next, vehicles on hypothetical lanes that can discharge during the same signal phase are combined into another hypothetical lane; e.g., see Step 1B in Fig. 1 in which vehicles 1 and 5 on opposing through lanes are combined into another hypothetical lane because these two lanes can move together during the NB/SB through phase. The outcome is a hypothetical intersection in which each lane corresponds to a unique signal phase and all of the vehicles on a lane are able to depart during that respective phase.

In the second step, consecutive vehicles in the same hypothetical lane are grouped into platoons if their headway (for moving vehicles) or spacing (for stopped vehicles) is smaller than some pre-defined critical values. For example, moving vehicles 6 and 7 in Fig. 1 are grouped into one platoon (illustrated by a red ellipse around these vehicles) if the headway between them is lower than the critical headway value. Also, stopped vehicles 4 and 9 in Fig. 1 are treated as a single platoon if their spacing is lower than the critical spacing. This process can be expressed by the pseudo-code shown in Table 2.

The third step of the signal control algorithm identifies the optimal signal phasing and timing (SPaT) plan to be implemented at the intersection. Here, we seek to identify the SPaT plan that minimizes the total delay experienced by vehicles incorporated into the algorithm (i.e., those that are either connected vehicles themselves or identified using information from those that are connected) while setting a constraint on the maximum delay that is imparted to any identified vehicle. This can be expressed more formally by the following mathematical program:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \min _{k} T D_{k}=\sum_{c \in N}\left(D_{c, k}-V_{c}\right) \rightarrow \min _{k} \sum_{c \in N} D_{c, k} \\
& \text { s.t. } \forall c \in C,\left(D_{c, k}-V_{c}\right)<T
\end{aligned}
$$

In (1) and (2), $T D_{k}$ is the total delay experienced for a given platoon departure sequence $k$ and is expressed as the summation of the delay of each vehicle $c$ in the set of all vehicles incorporated into the aglorithm, $N$. The delay of any vehicle is equal to the departure time of that vehicle in the sequence $k, D_{c, k}$, minus its virtual arrival time, $V_{c}$. A maximum delay threshold, $T$, is also implemented to effectively eliminate any sequence $k$ in which an identified vehicle experiences an individual delay greater than the proposed threshold value. Note that this constriant requires knowledge of both the vehicle's virtual arrival and departure times. Since the virtual arrival times for non-connected vehicles that are identified using connected vehicle data would be unknown, the constraint is only valid for the set of connected vehicles, $C$ We specifically conjecture here that constraining the maximum vehicle delay-which provides a measure of equity with respect to the worst case delay experienced at the intersection and is fairly easy to estimate in real-time within the proposed algorithm-can provide more equitable distribution of vehicle delays at the intersection by identifying cases where individual vehicles are unreasonably affected by the signal controller. Since some vehicles are expected to have no delay, constraining the maximum vehicle delay should also reduce the range of delays experienced by individual vehicles, and the variation in individual vehicle delays, as well as provide a more equitable distribution of delays imparted onto individual vehicles. The latter assumption will be verified using simulation in subsequent sections.

As described in Guler et al. (2014), a vehicle's estimated departure time $\left(D_{c}\right)$ is the maximum of three possibilities: the current time plus its free flow time from its current location through the intersection; the estimated departure time of the previous vehicle in the same lane plus a headway and start-up lost time; and, the estimated start of green time plus a start-up lost time (based on the estimated departure time of previous vehicles in the given departure sequence and the maximum/minimum constraint on green times). This is expressed in Eq. (3).

$$
D_{c, k}=\max \left\{V_{c} ; D_{c^{\prime}, k}+T_{\text {lost }} ; T_{\text {green }}+T_{\text {lost }}\right\}
$$

where $T_{\text {green }}$ represents the start of the green time, $T_{\text {lost }}$ represents the start-up lost time for vehicle $c$, which depends on its sequence behind the stop bar, and $c$ represents the vehicle that departs immediately before vehicle $c$, which does not necessarily have to be on the same lane or approach.

The three cases correspond to scenarios in which the vehicle is far from the intersection and has no other vehicles ahead of it; the vehicle is following another vehicle in front; and, the vehicle is the first one in its lane to depart after the start of the green, respectively. Unfortunately, due to the way these departure times are calculated, it is not computationally feasible to solve the mathematical program described by (1) and (2) directly. Instead, this optimization problem is solved by enumerating through the set of potential phase sequences as illustrated in the right-hand side of the flowchart provided in Fig. 3. For every possible departure sequence, $k$, considered, the control algorithm first evaluates the individual delay for all identified vehicles. If the delay of any individual vehicle exceeds the threshold, the algorithm deems that departure sequence as infeasible. Then, the sequence that has the lowest estimated average vehicle delay among sequences that are feasible is selected as the optimal platoon departure sequence. Note
that it is possible that no departure sequences will be feasible if the constraint on maximum vehicle delay is set to be too small. In this case, the control algorithm eliminates constraint (2) for that control step and instead identifies the sequence with the lowest estimated average vehicle delay. Constraint (2) is reinstated in the next control decision step. Note that this enumeration is computationally efficient since vehicles are first grouped into platoons and is suitable for real-time application; see Liang et al. (2018) for more details.

In the fourth step, the optimal platoon discharge sequence is converted to an optimal SPaT plan by determining the phase duration as the time required for the last vehicle in a platoon to discharge through the intersection. The selected plan is implemented at the intersection. The optimization process then repeats itself at regular control intervals using updated information from connected vehicles.

## 4. Simulation and results

This section examines the effectiveness of the proposed strategy using a simulation platform. We specifically examine the assumption that more equitable distribution of vehicle delays can be obtained by limiting the maximum delay imparted onto any individual connected vehicle. We first describe the simulation platform and proposed scenario. Then, we provide simulation results without and with constraint (2) to demonstrate how a limit on maximum vehicle delay may improve vehicle delay equity at an intersection. Then, we examine the sensitivity of the proposed algorithm to different demand patterns.

### 4.1. Simulation platform and scenario

The simulation platform was built in JAVA and models the movement of each individual vehicle using the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) car-following model (Treiber et al., 2000). The delay predictions of the simulation platform were tested against a commercially available microsimulation software, specifically AIMSUN, and for various demands and different fixed signal timing plans the delay predictions were found to be within 1\% of each other on average, with a maximum difference of 20\%. More importantly, both simulation platforms showed similar trends in changes to delay when demand or signal timing plans changed.

We specifically consider here an intersection with four-approaches, each with one through lane and one exclusive left-turn lane. For the base-case simulation tests, it is assumed that a total input flow of 1800 veh/hr is evenly distributed across the four approaches. It is assumed that changes in signal timing do not lead to route changes. Left-turning vehicles are assumed to account for 20\% of the total demand. Full market penetration of connected vehicles is assumed in this section such that all vehicles provide information to the signal controller. This assumption will be relaxed in the simulation tests performed in Section 4.

For each simulation run, 1000 vehicles are simulated based on the assumed arrival flow to the intersection and distributions of vehicles on each approach and lane. Individual vehicle headways are randomly generated assuming an exponential distribution to mimic completely random arrivals that might occur at an isolated intersection. However, assuming an isolated intersection in these tests presents a worst case as the algorithm tends to perform better when vehicles already arrive to the intersection in platoons. This fact was tested and confirmed using additional simulation tests in which additional signals were placed upstream and downstream of the subject signal.

The signal control algorithm is implemented to determine the optimal signal phasing and timing scheme, which is updated at regular intervals of 10 s. For every scenario considered, 50 unique runs are performed and the average results are provided. It is worth noting that all scenarios tested in this paper are designed to ensure traffic flow on each approach is not over-saturated, since this type of a signal control algorithm tends to revert to a fixed-time scheme and loses its advantages in over-saturated scenarios (Liang et al., 2018). More generally, recent studies have shown that adaptive traffic signals can improve network performance in lightly congested scenarios but tend to breakdown in very congested traffic situations (Gayah et al., 2014).

### 4.2. Vehicle delay distribution without maximum delay threshold

We first run the algorithm without the constraint in (2) to examine the distribution of vehicle delays when total vehicle delay is minimized. A total of 50,000 individual vehicle delays were available from the 50 runs of 1000 vehicles each. Fig. 3a provides a histogram of the individual vehicle delays, while Fig. 3b highlights the right tail of the distribution. The resulting average vehicle delay among those 50,000 vehicles is 19.41 s. However, it is obvious that the control algorithm imparts high delays to some vehicles in order to minimize the total delay experienced by all vehicles. Specifically, some vehicles experience over 10 times the delay that an average vehicle experiences while traversing the intersection. Note that determining an appropriate level of equity is not straightforward and each agency might have its own standards as to what maximum acceptable waiting times should be. However, previous research literature might provide some insight. For example, according to van der Bijl et al. (2011), less than $20 \%$ of users are willing to accept waiting times that exceed 80 s. The largest delay observed in these results are much greater than 80 s indicating that some users would be unsatisfied with this signal timing. The standard deviation of individual vehicle delays is 19.21 s, which also suggests sizable variations in delay among individual vehicles.

Fig. 3c provides a graphical plot of the cumulative percent of delay experienced across all vehicles as a function of the cumulative fraction of vehicle population. The dark solid line represents the cumulative vehicle delay obtained from the simulation tests, while the dotted line represents the cumulative delays that would arise for a completely equitable scenario in which all vehicles experienced the same delay. The ratio of the area between these two curves and the area under the diagonal, often referred to as the Gini coefficient, $G$, provides a quantitative measure of the inequity of the delay distribution, which can be expressed by Eq. (4) based on Fig. 3c.

$$
G=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(A)}{\operatorname{Area}(A)+\operatorname{Area}(B)}
$$

A value of $G=0$ represents complete equality (all vehicles have the same delay), while a value of $G=1$ represents complete inequality (one vehicle experiences all the delay while the rest experience none). The value of the Gini coefficient, $G$, obtained for the scenario when a constraint on maximum vehicle delay is not applied (i.e., equity is not considered in the signal timing optimization) is 0.4989 . This serves as a baseline value for the remainder of the simulation tests.

### 4.3. Vehicle delay distribution with maximum delay threshold

This section examines the effectiveness of limiting the maximum vehicle delay on the overall vehicle delay distribution. The results are provided for the same simulation scenario as performed in the previous section; however the results and trends are consistent with other scenarios. As a starting point, four maximum vehicle delay thresholds are considered: 40, 80, 120 and 160 s. All of these thresholds are smaller than the maximum delay value observed when no threshold is applied. Fig. 4a provides the histogram of individual vehicle delays when each of these four thresholds are applied. Notice that the 120 and 160 s thresholds are able to successfully restrict the maximum observed value of individual vehicle delay to be less than the threshold value. There are a handful of cases when the 80 s threshold is applied in which vehicles have delays greater than 80 s. This occurs because no feasible signal timing plan exists for certain control steps that can meet the constraint; thus, a small fraction of vehicles experience delays greater than the threshold. For the 40 s threshold, these infeasible cases occur more frequently and a higher fraction of vehicles have a delay greater than the threshold value. To further illustrate this point, Fig. 4b provides the relationship between maximum observed vehicle delay and maximum individual vehicle delay threshold in the control algorithm (solid line). The dashed line represents the theoretical case in which the maximum value is equal to the threshold. Notice that the solid curve falls above this dashed line for higher thresholds, suggesting that the threshold is successfully met, but lies above the dashed line for thresholds below 90 s.

Nevertheless, maximum vehicle delay thresholds too small to be always met are still able to reduce variation in individual vehicle delays and improve vehicle delay equity. Specifically, the standard deviation of individual vehicle delay with a threshold of 160, 120, 80 and 40 s is 18.9902, 18.6357, 17.1071, and 15.0986 s, respectively. To further examine the impact of the threshold on individual vehicle delay equity, Fig. 5a provides the plot of cumulative percent of delay experienced across all vehicles vs. the cumulative fraction of vehicle population for the same threshold values. ${ }^{1}$ As shown, these curves move closer to the diagonal that represents a completely equitable distribution of vehicle delays as the maximum vehicle delay threshold decreases. The corresponding computed Gini coefficient as a function of the maximum vehicle delay threshold for a wide range of threshold values is shown in Fig. 5b. The plot reveals that the Gini coefficient is relatively steep at the lowest thresholds and flattens at the highest thresholds. This seems reasonable as higher thresholds potentially impact a smaller fraction of vehicles (i.e., relatively few vehicles would potentially experience delays greater than the threshold if it was not applied) and thus the impact of these high thresholds on overall delay equity is modest. However, more vehicles would be impacted as the threshold decreases and this serves to provide larger reductions in vehicle delay inequity. The relationship between standard deviation of vehicle delay (not shown) follows a similar pattern in which standard deviation of vehicle delays is relatively insensitive to the threshold for larger values but steeply decreases at the lowest values.

### 4.4. Tradeoff between delay equity and efficiency

The previous section showed that applying a maximum vehicle delay threshold provides signal timings that yield more equitable distribution of vehicle delays at the intersection and that the level of equity increases as the maximum delay threshold decreases. However, improved vehicle delay equity comes at a cost: reduced intersection efficiency. To demonstrate this tradeoff, Fig. 6 il-lustrates the relationship between average vehicle delay (measure of intersection efficiency) as a function of both maximum observed vehicle delay and the Gini coefficient measure of vehicle delay inequity when various maximum vehicle delay thresholds are applied. The points on the plots represent the average values observed across the 50 simulation runs for each of the threshold values tested, while the error bars represent a 95\% confidence interval for the average value among all tested simulation runs.

Fig. 6a reiterates that actual maximum individual vehicle delays are accurately constrained by larger threshold values (e.g., those in the range of 160-90 s). However, if the threshold is set too low (e.g., threshold of 50 s or lower), the threshold cannot be met and thus observed maximum delays become greater than the threshold that is set. Perhaps more interestingly, the blue portion of the curve associated with the higher thresholds illustrate a clear inverse relationship between average vehicle delay and maximum individual vehicle delay. Specifically, average vehicle delay increases as maximum observed vehicle delay decreases. However, for the red portion of the curve, both maximum vehicle delay and average vehicle delay increase. This represents cases with smaller thresholds that cannot be met quite often. Thus, the blue portion of the curve in Fig. 6a represent a Pareto Frontier that describes the optimal tradeoff between maximum vehicle delay and average vehicle delay.

## 6. Conclusions

This paper describes how a Connected Vehicle-based signal control algorithm can be extended to provide a more equitable distribution of delay among individual vehicles. The algorithm uses the locations and speeds of connected vehicles at regular intervals to identify the existence of non-connected vehicles stopped at the intersection to incorporate their presence in delay calculations. Both connected and identified non-connected vehicles are grouped into naturally occurring platoons and all possible platoon departure sequences are identified. The set of possible platoon departure sequences are used to identify the sequence that minimizes total vehicle delay expected at the intersection while ensuring that the maximum delay that any individual connected vehicle remains below a set threshold. The maximum delay threshold is used in this paper to design a signal controller that can be more equitable in terms of distribution of delays across individual vehicles. The algorithm is then tested under a variety of settings in a simulation environment.

The study reveals that maximum vehicle delay serves as a useful surrogate for vehicle delay equity in real-time traffic signal control. This metric is strongly correlated with other inequity measurements such as the Gini coefficient, standard deviation of individual vehicle delays and range of observed delays. Perhaps more importantly, maximum vehicle delay is relatively easy to compute in real-time when CAV information is used to inform the signal timing process.

The threshold on maximum vehicle delay can be met successfully when the threshold is set at a large enough value. Specifically, the simulation tests performed in this work reveal that the lowest threshold that is regularly met typically corresponds to about the 95th percentile delay value experienced by individual vehicles when no threshold is applied. This can provide some guidance when setting a maximum vehicle delay threshold in practice to ensure that it is effective. If the threshold is set lower than this value, it often cannot be met and individual vehicles may still have larger delays than the threshold value. Nevertheless, a lower threshold still seems to result in a more equitable distribution of vehicle delays as measured by the Gini coefficient or other measures of equity, even if it is sometimes violated.

The improvement in equity measures obtained by implementing a maximum vehicle delay threshold comes with the cost of negatively impacting overall intersection efficiency, measured here by the average delay experienced by all vehicles traversing the intersection. In general, the marginal benefit of sacrificing average vehicle delay for improvements in vehicle delay equity (both measured using maximum vehicle delay and the Gini coefficient) decreases as the maximum vehicle threshold is reduced. In many cases, significant improvements in equity can be achieved with negligible increases in average vehicle delay. The transition point when gains in equity come at the cost of significant reductions in intersection efficiency generally occurs when the maximum vehicle delay threshold is set to about the 98th percentile value.

The proposed method can achieve similar effectiveness among a large range of total traffic demands and under different demand distribution patterns. The algorithm also works under imperfect connected vehicle penetration. Even when all vehicles are not connected, the proposed method is still able to achieve the similar trade-off pattern between average and maximum vehicle delay among connected vehicles. However, the algorithm is not effective for CV penetration rates lower than about 40\%.

This algorithm has been tested on a simple isolated intersection considering only private vehicles. As mentioned in Section 4.1, the results present a worst-case scenario since the algorithm performance improves when vehicles are naturally grouped together, as would occur if implemented on a single intersection (or multiple intersections independently) along an arterial. Future extensions can consider performance on more complicated intersections with different phase options. More interestingly, a similar methodology can be used to provide equity in delays between different modes, such as cars, buses, and pedestrians. Thresholds to minimize the maximum delay of different modes can be implemented independently, or new constraints, such as minimizing the difference between delays of pedestrians and vehicle passengers, can be implemented. Current research is looking at extending the algorithm to optimize performance of multiple modes simultaneously. The algorithm proposed here is also being extended to consider arteriallevel intersection operations and the joint optimization of signal timings across multiple intersections that make up a signalized corridor.

Lastly, the algorithm assumes that traffic patterns do not change with signal timings. However, vehicles may change their routes based on the delays experienced. For example, a vehicle experiencing a very long delay on one day might decide to use a different route on another day. Such dynamics between routing decisions and signal timing decisions are important to capture for networklevel traffic control. Further work is needed to examine these impacts with respect to equitable signal timings at intersections.
