#### Abstract

At complex intersections, traffic congestion causes pollution and leads to accidents, high commute times and many other problems. Correct traffic signal timing can help to reduce the congestion and it can improve the traffic flow. We present a new microscopic traffic simulation-optimization model with Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) for traffic signal timing and demonstrate the model with an application to a major roundabout with 28 traffic signal heads in lzmir, Turkey, using real data. A VISSIM (Verkehr In Städten-SIMulationsmodell) model of the Osman Kibar Roundabout, based on actual traffic flow data, is developed. A PSO model was used to search for traffic signal timings that would minimize mean travel time through the roundabout, and the microsimulation model (VISSIM) was used to evaluate the solutions from PSO. The changes in the traffic signal timings resulted in a 55.9\% decrease in average delay time per vehicle passing through the roundabout, and a 9.3\% increase in the number of vehicles passing through the roundabout per unit time in the bestcase scenario.


## Keywords

Traffic signal timing, simulation optimization, Particle Swarm Optimization, roundabouts, VISSIM (Verkehr In Städten-SIMulationsmodell)

## I. Introduction

Traffic congestion is one of the major problems of urban life. An urban traffic system, composed of vehicles, pedestrians, traffic lights and a traffic network structure, represents a complex problem to be solved. ${ }^{1}$ The rate of expansion of demand for new roads and other transportation systems continues to exceed the rate of expansion of supply; therefore, congestion causes an increasing loss of time and resources in urban areas.

Improving public transportation and intelligent transportation system (ITS) applications are some of the main approaches to solving the traffic congestion problem. One such approach is the effective use of traffic signals, which is a part of the ITS applications. Traffic signal control systems are used to control and regulate traffic flow for pedestrians and vehicles at road intersections or other locations where competing flow of traffic is present. ${ }^{2}$ Traffic congestion is primarily a flow problem. Therefore, it can be managed through the regulation of various signals in relation to each other. Researchers generally agree that the effective staging of traffic signals can help to reduce traffic congestion by improving the flow of the vehicles in urban areas. ${ }^{3}$

In this paper, we focus on roundabouts. The signalization of roundabouts is discouraged in the US according to the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) roundabout informational guide. However, in a number of countries, including the United Kingdom, France, Sweden, Australia, Netherlands and Turkey, roundabouts are signalized, especially where the vehicle and pedestrian traffic is high. ${ }^{4}$ Therefore, effective signal use at roundabouts is important for improving the traffic flow and, thus, reducing traffic congestion.

This work presents a simulation-optimization model for traffic signal timing at roundabouts. The proposed model uses a Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) model to search for optimal settings for the traffic signal timings and a traffic microsimulation model (VISSIM: Verkehr In Städten-SIMulationsmodell) to evaluate the performance

[^0]of traffic signal timing settings. The proposed model is applied to the Osman Kibar Roundabout, one of the biggest intersections in Izmir, Turkey. The roundabout connects major inter-city roads to Izmir, and has one of the highest observed traffic volumes in the city.

The main objective of this study is to reduce the waiting times at traffic signals by optimizing signal timings based on the simulation-optimization model. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first study to use PSO and the microscopic traffic simulation tool for roundabout signal timing optimization. The reduction in waiting times will result in smoother traffic flow, and hence reduce fuel usage and decrease emissions.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents more information on the problem literature survey. Section 3 explains the proposed solution method in detail. A real life implementation of the proposed solution method is presented in Section 4, along with details of the experimental design and results from the experimentation. Finally, conclusions and future work are discussed in Section 5.

## 2. Literature survey

Traffic signal timing optimization is a complex problem, especially for intersections with multiple traffic signals and connections to major arterials. ${ }^{5}$

The traffic signal light alternates between red and green and this requires the introduction of the discrete variable for the traffic signal timing optimization problem, which makes the problem combinatorial, and considering the complexity of the whole urban traffic system, its optimization problem quickly becomes very large. ${ }^{6}$

The prerequisite of improving any system is the ability to measure the effects of the changes made to the system. Due to the nature of traffic systems, the data is variable during the day, week, month or year. The number of vehicles, percentages of different types of vehicles in the urban traffic system, the distance and location information of the intersections, the number of pedestrians, and entry and exit points of the vehicles and pedestrians are required to solve the traffic signal optimization problem optimally.

Signal timing optimization requires measurement of performance for a particular setting of the signal timing. It is challenging to develop an analytical model that considers different vehicles from different directions, at nondeterministic rates. Therefore, to the best knowledge of the authors, there are relatively few analytical models for signal timing optimization models in the literature. ${ }^{7}$ In addition, a simulation-based model is capable of creating systems of greater complexity in which different signaling controls, user groups and management strategies are utilized. ${ }^{8}$

One of the earliest and best known stage-based fixedtime strategies was SIGSET. ${ }^{9}$ SIGSET's objective function was a nonlinear delay function derived by Webster. ${ }^{10}$ SIGSET used $m$ linear constraints on the capacity of stage specifications, which resulted in a linearly constrained nonlinear programming problem. SIGCAP was developed also by Allsop, ${ }^{11}$ who aimed to maximize intersection capacity. SIGCAP, however, was a linear programming (LP) problem.

Improta and Canteralla ${ }^{12}$ solved a similar single intersection signal optimization problem, but included stage specifications (phased-based fixed-time strategy). Their approach determined split and cycle time, as well as stage specifications for optimization of total delay or system capacity. To determine stage specifications, they had to add binary variables, which made their problem a mixedinteger linear programming (MILP) problem, and thus more difficult.

For a network of intersections, one of the earliest MILP models was developed by Little ${ }^{13}$ in 1966 under the name MAXBAND. Little developed MILP for $n$ intersections of a two-way arterial, for which split and cycle times are assumed to be given. His model determined the optimal offset based on maximizing the number of vehicles that can travel a given range without stopping. Later, MAXBAND was transformed into a portable FORTRAN code, which was able to handle three-artery networks with up to 17 signals. ${ }^{14}$ Chaudhary et al. ${ }^{15}$ reduced the computational requirements of MAXBAND. Stamatiadas and Gartner ${ }^{16}$ extended MAXBAND so that it became applicable to networks of arterials.

Webster, ${ }^{10}$ in his work from 1958, states that

> Since a theoretical calculation of delay is very complex and direct observation of delay on the road is complicated by uncontrollable variations, it was decided to use a method whereby the events on the road are reproduced in the laboratory by means of some machine which simulates behavior of traffic.
> This observation suggests the use of simulation for traffic problems.

The "Urban Traffic Control System" was the first traffic simulation software developed under the direction of FHWA and later named NETSIM, short for Network Simulation. FREESIM (abbreviated from Freeway Simulation) was an enhanced version of NETSIM, which could handle more complex freeway geometrics and provided a more realistic representation of traffic on a freeway. In 1998, NETSIM and FREESIM were combined and offered to the public under the new name CORSIM.

There are two main approaches in traffic modeling using simulation: microscopic and macroscopic approaches. In a microscopic model, each car is simulated individually whereby dynamic variables of the models represent microscopic properties, such as the position and velocity of particular vehicles. The macroscopic models
take a more aggregated approach, where it is assumed that traffic flows as a whole are comparable to fluid streams. Therefore, in this case, the focus is on traffic flow characteristics, such as density, mean speed of traffic flow, etc.

TRANSYT-7F is a macroscopic traffic simulation model, which also performs optimization using Genetic Algorithms (GAs) and a hill-climbing method. TRANSYT-7F considers platoons of vehicles rather than individual vehicles, but simulates the flow in shorter time increments, which allows more detailed representation than most other macroscopic models. ${ }^{17}$

Synchro is another well-known macroscopic simulation package with signal timing optimization capabilities. Synchro can optimize cycle lengths, green splits, offsets and phase sequences. Synchro optimizes the four signal timing parameters by evaluating a series of cycle lengths, while applying a heuristic method for green splits, and conducting an exhaustive search for left-turn phase position, and a quasi-exhaustive search for offsets. ${ }^{18}$ However, TRANSYT-7F and Synchro are unable to fully consider the important aspects of traffic behaviors due to the nature of the macroscopic simulation model. For example, lanechanging behavior and vehicle interactions are not considered in the macroscopic models in a realistic manner.

Although there are studies with macroscopic models, there is a growing recognition of the usefulness of stochastic microscopic simulation models. ${ }^{19}$

Microscopic simulation models allow realistic scenarios to be tested under real-world conditions, and also provide network-wide performance measures, including travel times, delays and emissions. ${ }^{20}$ Microscopic simulation models are increasingly considered a valid tool for signal timing and capacity studies. ${ }^{21}$

CORSIM was among the earliest microscopic simulation tools developed. VISSIM, SUMO, PARAMICS and SIMTRAFFIC are among the microscopic models available currently. Park et al. ${ }^{21}$ compared various microscopic simulation models in terms of computation time and their capability to model a coordinated actuated signal system. An earlier but a much more extensive review of microscopic models is given in the SMARTEST project. ${ }^{22}$

Oner et al. ${ }^{23}$ presented a simulation model of signals at a roundabout for improvement of traffic flow. In their experimentation, a list of predetermined cycle times and phases was evaluated using a simulation model developed in Rockwell Automation Arena simulation software.

Among the heuristic optimization methods, the GA method is dominant in the determination of signal timing. Foy et al. ${ }^{24}$ proposed a GA to determine signal timing for a two-phase system. Hadi and Wallace ${ }^{25}$ developed a GA to be used in combination with the TRANSYT-7F optimization routine to determine signal timing and phasing. Clement and Taylor ${ }^{26}$ developed a GA and a knowledgebased system for dynamic traffic signal control. AbuLebdeh and Benekohal ${ }^{27}$ applied GAs to oversaturated arterials for traffic control and queue management. Park et al. ${ }^{28}$ and Lee et al. ${ }^{29}$ applied the GA with CORSIM microscopic traffic simulation.

The extensive list of GA-based signal timing optimizations based on single-objective searches cannot be covered here comprehensively (see the literature review in Stevanovic et al. ${ }^{30}$ ). There are, however, additional studies that are implemented an evolutionary multi-objective optimization to retime traffic signals. Sun et al. ${ }^{31}$ applied NSGA-II to optimize delay and stops for an isolated intersection under two-phase control. NSGA-II obtained a close approximation to the Pareto set while using an analytical formula to determine delay and stops. Abbas et al. ${ }^{32}$ applied NSGA-II to a small three-signal network while choosing signal settings from a predetermined set obtained from a single-objective optimization at different cycle lengths.

Thus, the multi-objective optimizer only considered variations in cycle length, simplifying the exercise considerably. Kesur ${ }^{33}$ recommended a multi-objective optimization for cases involving numerous optimization variables.

Use of other meta-heuristic methods to optimize signal timing has been limited. Chen and $\mathrm{Xu}^{34}$ applied PSO for training a fuzzy logic controller located at intersections with the aim of determining green light timings. They used a simple network with two basic junctions to test their model. More recently, using a custom microscopic traffic flow model, Peng et al. ${ }^{35}$ developed a PSO algorithm for a restrictive one-way road with two intersections. Finally, García-Nieto et al. ${ }^{3}$ proposed a PSO algorithm to optimize traffic light cycle programs using a microscopic traffic simulator, SUMO.

There are also some studies that utilize neural networks, ${ }^{36-38}$ cellular automata, ${ }^{39}$ fuzzy control ${ }^{40}$ and fuzzyneuro methods ${ }^{41}$ for urban traffic signal control, but they diverge considerably in their settings, and for that reason are not included.

To the best knowledge of the authors, there is no study in the literature that optimizes traffic signal timings at roundabouts using a microsimulation tool together with PSO.

## 3. Problem and model

Traffic signals are vital for organizing traffic flow. Optimal signal timing helps to reduce traffic delays and reduce queues at the traffic signals.

A traffic signal timing setting is described by the operation of its lights and durations of these. The traffic light sequences are red (stop), red/yellow (prepare to go), green (go) and yellow (prepare to stop). The sum of the duration of each sequence is known as the cycle time.

In this section, we present the details of the proposed simulation-optimization model to solve the traffic signal
timing problem at roundabouts for minimizing average time spent at the roundabout. Then the model is implemented for the signal timing settings of the Osman Kibar roundabout in Izmir, Turkey, and results are given in Section 4.

The proposed model searches through combinations of traffic signal timings for the roundabout, using a PSObased algorithm. The PSO algorithm then calls upon a microscopic simulation model of the roundabout to evaluate the current solution. Results from the simulation model are fed back to the PSO algorithm. Although microscopic simulation models represent traffic systems more realistically, they also require significantly more computation time. PSO is a population-based algorithm that is especially successful at continuous domain problems, and therefore is expected to find "good" solutions in a significantly shorter amount of computational time.

Solutions are evaluated via simulation, since formulating a mathematical model for this complex and continuous domain problem is very difficult, and even if formulated, obtaining a solution most likely may not be possible. Only a detailed simulation model is capable of reflecting the effects of a particular roundabout setting with all the stochasticity involved. The traffic flow at a complex roundabout includes a number of sources of traffic, usually with non-homogeneous arrival times. Also, passing time for a vehicle does not only depend on the entrance and exit points, but it is also highly dependent on the traffic flow and mix in the roundabout, which affects vehicle acceleration/deceleration and speed. These factors make it extremely difficult to accurately model a complex roundabout using analytical methods.

It is difficult to realistically simulate the traffic flow at a roundabout with a general purpose discrete event simulation tool, due to the characteristics of traffic flow. Therefore, we use a special purpose traffic microsimulation package, VISSIM. ${ }^{42}$ Microsimulation differs from general purpose simulation packages in the sense that it models the detailed interactions of individual "units", such as people or vehicles. Treating each unit as an autonomous entity allows greater accuracy in modeling of traffic flow. A traffic microsimulation model has an individual entity for each vehicle, pedestrian, etc.; therefore, it is possible to realistically represent the acceleration, deceleration, lane changing or car following behavior of a specific roundabout.

PSO is a population (swarm)-based heuristic method, designed initially for continuous optimization problems. In PSO, each potential solution, called a particle, represents a location in the problem space. The population of particles is called the swarm. ${ }^{43}$ The particles start at random locations and search for solutions according to a given objective function, by moving through the problem space.

At any point time $t$, each particle $i$ has a position, $x^{i}{ }_{t}$ and a velocity, $v^{i}{ }_{t}$ during the search. The position represents the solution, and the velocity represents the change in that solution. At each iteration, particles update their positions and velocities according to

$$
\begin{gathered}
x_{t+1}^{i}=x_{t}^{i}+v_{t+1}^{i} \\
v_{t+1}^{i}=w \cdot v_{t}^{i}+c_{1} \cdot r_{1} \cdot\left(p_{t}^{i}-x_{t}^{i}\right)+c_{2} \cdot r_{2} \cdot\left(b_{t}-x_{t}^{i}\right)
\end{gathered}
$$

In Equations (1) and (2), $p^{i}{ }_{t}$ is the best solution of particle $i$ by iteration $t$ and $b_{t}$ is the global best any particle has achieved by iteration $t$.

The parameters are as follows.

- $w$ is the inertia weight. It determines the effect of the previous velocity. If $w$ increases, particles tend to search more in new areas; the typical value for $w$ is near 1.0. ${ }^{44}$
- $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$ are the coefficients. They determine the relative effect of the local best position and the global best position, respectively. According to Merkle and Middendorf, ${ }^{44}$ the typical values of $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$ are equal to 2.0.
- $r_{1}$ and $r_{2}$ are uniform random values UNIF[0, 1].

The main idea is that particles update their positions and velocities based on both their best position and the best position of the swarm. The relative effect that is the best for each particle and for the swarm is determined by the coefficients, and also on the random numbers.

Figure 1 describes the pseudocode of PSO. The PSO algorithm starts by initializing the swarm, in which each particle is formed with their position and velocities. Then, the best value variables for each particle and for the swarm are found. Following this, the main loop starts, and repeats

```
Pseudocode of PSO:
Initialize Swarm
Repeat
    for each particle
        Evaluate objective function f at the particles location
    endfor
    for each particle
        Update the personal best position
    endfor
    Update the global best position
    for each particle
        Update the velocity (equation 2)
        Compute the new location of the particle (equation 1)
    endfor
until termination criterion (max. number of iterations) is met
```

At each iteration, particles are evaluated via simulation, particles' and swarm's best are updated, and particles' position and velocity are updated according to Equations (1) and (2).

Each solution representing a different traffic signal timing combination at a roundabout with $n$ signal heads is represented as a $2 \times n$ matrix (Figure 2). Column $i$ of the matrix shows the green phase start time and green phase duration of the $i$ th signal head. Because the duration of each yellow light is fixed at three seconds, this information does not need to be included in the solution representation. For each given cycle time, this information is enough to determine the red phase duration and hence the whole signal distribution. The PSO algorithm searches through appropriate combination of red/green light timings for a given cycle time, and moves on to a new prospect cycle time once the best solution for the current cycle time is found.

The velocity of a particle is also represented as a $2 \times n$ matrix (Figure 3). Column $i$ of the velocity matrix denotes the amount and direction of changes in the green phase start time and green phase duration of traffic signal head, respectively. Row 1, column $i$ shows the percentage change in the green phase start time, compared to the immediately previous green phase start time of traffic signal head $i$. Similarly, Row 2 of column $i$ shows the percentage change in the green phase duration of traffic signal head $i$. Therefore, values in the velocity matrix can be positive or negative.

### 4.2 Parameter tuning

PSO algorithm parameters must be tuned for the specific problem under consideration in order to improve the performance. This involves finding the best possible configuration of the parameters of the PSO algorithm, that is, the one that yields the best results in the instances in which the algorithm will be used.

The parameters involved in tuning are population size, inertia, $c_{1}, c_{2}$ and maximum iteration number (termination condition), which determines the replication length. Population size determines the number of swarm particles.

The PSO algorithm uses a fixed number of iterations as the termination condition. However, if a new swarm best solution is found during the last $k$ iterations, the PSO algorithm continues to run as long as there is an improvement in the swarm's best during the last $k$ iterations after the maximum number of iterations. When the search goes through successive $k$ iterations without any improvement in the swarm's best after the maximum number of iterations, the algorithm stops.

In addition to the termination condition, inertia, coefficients $c_{1}$ and $c_{2}$ and swarm size are included into parameter tuning at two levels (Table 3). During this parameter tuning, the cycle time is set to 149 seconds, which is calculated according to Highway Capacity Manual (HCM) traffic signal phase calculation formulas for Osman Kibar Roundabout (adapted from Roess et al. ${ }^{2}$ ). The simulation is replicated 10 times at each setting (specific setting of the parameters) and the averages for the replications are calculated.

The best combination for parameters obtained is 10, 1.0, 1, 2, 5-30 for swarm size, inertia, $c_{1, c 2}$ and maximum iteration number, respectively.

The warm-up period for the model is set to 600 seconds (10 minutes), when the roundabout is expected to reach steady-state conditions. The model is run for different cycle time values to find the most effective one. The effective cycle time is determined according to the maximum total number of vehicles passing through the roundabout. The model is run 10 times for each cycle time, which is calculated according to 90\% level of confidence in our outputs. ${ }^{45}$

### 4.3 Experimentation

The total number of vehicles passing and the average delay per vehicle at the roundabout are recorded in order to make a comparison between the current system and the output of the proposed PSO-based simulation-optimization method.

The simulation replication length is set to 40 minutes (with 10 minutes of warm-up period) for each run, using a computer with an Intel Core i7 CPU Q720 1.60 GHz processor with 3.0 GB RAM running Windows 7. The majority of the run-time is for the animated simulation model of the roundabout.

The performance measures for the current conditions are gathered using the current traffic signal timings when the cycle time is 92 seconds. In the current system setting, the average number of passing vehicles is 1827 and the average delay time per vehicle is 83.84 seconds. The number of vehicles entering the roundabout is observed as 4178 per hour, as shown in Table 1. The simulation model was run for 30 minutes; therefore the number of vehicles generated in the simulation model, considering the number of vehicles in the roundabout, is about the same as the actual data for the cycle time of 92 seconds. The simulation model is verified and validated by comparing the actual traffic conditions and the model output with the current cycle time. The starting cycle length is first set to 149 seconds, obtained by using HCM signal phase calculation formulas. The cycle length is then decreased at 5 second intervals starting from 70, since the Department of Transport recommends that the maximum cycle time for a signalized roundabout should be 70 seconds. ${ }^{46}$

Both the average number of vehicles and delay increase once the cycle time falls below 45 seconds. This behavior is expected, because when the cycle time shortens, the greater proportion of green time available is taken up with acceleration/deceleration and lane changes. To find the exact cycle time after which the performance suffers, a more detailed set of experiments must be run for the cycle times in the range of 40-45 seconds. Considering the average numbers of vehicles and average delay times for 40 and 45 second cycles are very close, 45 seconds of cycle time seems acceptable from a practical point of view. The level of service (LOS) of the roundabout is also improved by the 45-second cycle time. ## 5. Conclusions and future work

In this study, we present a novel simulation-optimization model for determining traffic signal timing at roundabouts. Signalized roundabouts are used extensively in a number of countries, including Turkey. An improvement on the timing of these traffic signals will improve traffic flow by reducing average delay time at roundabouts and increasing the average number of vehicles passing per unit time.

The proposed model uses PSO to search through signal timing combinations, and a microsimulation model of the roundabout in question to evaluate the performance of any setting. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first study to use PSO and the microscopic traffic simulation tool for roundabout signal timing optimization. The proposed model was tested at Osman Kibar Roundabout in Izmir, which has 28 set of traffic signals.

Results from the computational study show that the PSO-based microsimulation-optimization model can identify cycle times achieving a 55.9\% decrease in delay time and 9.3\% increase in number of vehicles per unit time. The dramatic decrease in average delay time will result in significant fuel and therefore also emission savings, in addition to the benefits of reduced wait time.

Results also show that HCM formulations may not be very appropriate for determining roundabout traffic signal timing. The delays caused by the HCM calculated 149second cycle were found to be more than five times worse than those caused by the best solution found by the proposed method.

The next steps would be to expand the model to handle a network of signalized roundabouts (or intersections), and to collect more detailed information on the traffic characteristics in the roundabout.
