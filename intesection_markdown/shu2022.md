#### Abstract

Knowledge transfer is a promising concept to achieve real-time decision-making for autonomous vehicles. This paper constructs a transfer deep reinforcement learning (RL) framework to transform the driving tasks in the intersection environments. The driving missions at the unsignalized intersection are cast into a left turn, right turn, and running straight for automated vehicles. The goal of the autonomous ego vehicle (AEV) is to drive through the intersection situation efficiently and safely. This objective promotes the studied vehicle to increase its speed and avoid crashing other vehicles. The decision-making policy learned from one driving task is transferred through three transfer rules in another driving mission and evaluated. Simulation results reveal that the decision-making strategies related to similar tasks are transferable and have a high success rate. It indicates that the presented control framework could reduce time consumption and realize online implementation. Therefore, the transfer RL concept is helpful for establishing the real-time decision-making policy for autonomous vehicles.

## II. Transfer Deep Reinforcement Learning

In this section, the transfer deep RL method is introduced. First, parameters of the RL framework are built by the Markov decision process (MDP). The realization of the dueling deep Q-learning algorithm is then given. The merits of this algorithm and its applicable scenes are analyzed. Furthermore, transfer learning is utilized to transfer the trained knowledge of different driving tasks.

## A. RL Concept

In the RL framework, an intelligent agent aims to search for the optimal control sequence. The selection process is guided by the environment. By interacting with the environment, the agent could improve the quality of the control action. This interaction is always mimicked by the finite MDP, as a tuple ( $S, A, \mathrm{P}, \mathrm{R}$ ). $S$ and $A$ represent the state space and action space. R is the reward function, which is determined by the state variable and the control action. Finally, P is the transition model to indicate the transformation of state variable [21].

RL methods have been applied in many research domains to derive the optimal control strategies [22]-[26]. In these problems, the goal of the agent is maximizing the accumulated reward. This reward is the sum of the immediate reward and discounted future rewards as follows:

$$
R_{t}=\sum_{k=t}^{T} \gamma^{k-t} \cdot r_{k}
$$

where $t$ is the time step, and $T$ is the time limitation. $r_{k}$ is the instantaneous reward received from the environment. $\gamma \in[0,1]$ is a discount parameter to realize the trade-off of current and future rewards.

The control policy obtained by the agent is $\pi$. To compute the control action at each time instant easily, two value functions are represented via the relevant state variable $s_{t}$ and control action $a_{t}$ as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\pi}\left(s_{t}\right) & \doteq E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{k=t}^{T} \gamma^{k-t} \cdot r_{k} \mid s_{t}\right] \\
Q_{\pi}\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right) & \doteq E_{\pi}\left[\sum_{k=t}^{T} \gamma^{k-t} \cdot r_{k} \mid s_{t}, a_{t}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

where (2) and (3) are the state-value function and action-value function. $\mathrm{E}_{\pi}[\cdot]$ denotes the expected value of the accumulated reward following policy $\pi$.

To conveniently generate the control action at each step, the action-value function is rewritten as the recursive form:

$$
Q_{\pi}\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)=E_{\pi}\left[r_{t}+\gamma \max _{a_{t+1}} Q_{\pi}\left(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1}\right)\right]
$$

where $s_{t+1}$ and $a_{t+1}$ are the state and action at the next step. The Bellman optimality equation is used to compute the maximum value of the Q-table:

$$
Q^{*}\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)=\sum_{s_{t+1}, r_{t}} p\left(s_{t+1}, r_{t} \mid s_{t}, a_{t}\right)\left[r_{t}+\gamma \max _{a_{t+1}} Q^{*}\left(s_{t+1}, a_{t+1}\right)\right]
$$

where $p\left(s_{t+1}, r_{t} \mid s_{t}, a_{t}\right) \in \mathrm{P}$ is the transition probability in the transition model. The tuple ( $s_{t+1}, r_{t}, s_{t}, a_{t}$ ) is named as a transition or an observation in the RL algorithm to record the intersection of the agent and environment. Finally, the optimal control action is derived from the maximum Q-table as follows:

$$
a_{t}^{*}=\arg \max _{a_{t}} Q\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)=\arg Q^{*}\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)
$$

## B. Dueling Deep Q-Learning Algorithm

Deep RL is a combination of deep learning (or the neural network (NN)) and RL [27]. The classical deep RL algorithm is deep Q-learning, which is first used to play the Atari games [28]. The Q-learning algorithm is described as the following shape to renew the Q-table:

$$
Q(s, a) \leftarrow Q(s, a)+\alpha\left[r+\gamma \max _{a^{\prime}} Q\left(s^{\prime}, a^{\prime}\right)-Q(s, a)\right]
$$

where $\alpha \in[0,1]$ is the learning rate in RL methods. In the RL algorithms, the epsilon $(\varepsilon)$ greedy strategy is utilized to choose the control action from the action space at each step.

In deep Q-learning, the neural network is employed to approximate the Q-table as $\mathrm{Q}(s, a ; \omega)$, wherein $\omega$ represents the weights and biases of the NN. The inputs of the NN are the state variables and the control actions, while the outputs are the estimated Q-table.

To measure the differences between the trained network and the actual Q-table, the loss function is introduced as:

$$
L(\omega)=E\left[\left(r+\gamma \max _{a^{\prime}} Q\left(s^{\prime}, a^{\prime} ; \omega^{\prime}\right)-Q(s, a ; \omega)\right)^{2}\right]
$$

where $\omega^{\prime}$ and $\omega$ are parameters of the target network and the evaluation network, respectively. Signing $\mathrm{y}=\mathrm{r}+\gamma \operatorname{maxa}{ }^{\prime} \mathrm{Q}\left(\mathrm{s}^{\prime}\right.$, $\mathrm{a}^{\prime} \omega^{\prime}$ ), the gradient descent method is usually applied to update the evaluation network as follows:

$$
\nabla_{\omega} L(\omega)=E\left[\left((y-Q(s, a ; \omega)) \nabla_{\omega} Q(s, a ; \omega)\right)\right]
$$

In the dueling deep Q-learning algorithm, a dueling network is established to approximate the action-value function (Q-table). The essence of this network is a combination of two neural networks. One NN is used to calculate the state-value function $V(s)$, and another one aims to compute the advantage function $A(s, a)$ [29]. The sketch of the dueling network is depicted in Fig. 2.

In this work, the action-value function in dueling deep Q-learning is written as follows:

$$
Q(s, a ; \omega, \varsigma, \tau)=V(s ; \omega, \varsigma)+A(s, a ; \omega, \tau)
$$

where $\tau$ and $\zeta$ are the parameters of the NN to approximate the value-action function and the advantage function. For the generation of the optimal control action using dueling deep Q-learning, (10) is formulated as forwarding mapping:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& Q(s, a ; \omega, \varsigma, \tau)=V(s ; \omega, \varsigma)+A(s, a ; \omega, \tau) \\
& \quad=V(s ; \omega, \varsigma)+\binom{A(s, a ; \omega, \tau)-}{\frac{1}{|A|} \sum_{a^{\prime}} A\left(s, a^{\prime} ; \omega, \tau\right)}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $|A|$ is the norm of the action space, $a^{\prime}$ is the next action. The optimal action is derived by (11) as the following expression:

$$
a^{*}=\arg \max _{a^{\prime}} Q\left(s, a^{\prime} ; \omega, \varsigma, \tau\right)=\arg \max _{a^{\prime}} A\left(s, a^{\prime} ; \omega, \tau\right)
$$

In this article, the dueling deep Q-learning is incorporated with the transfer learning idea to transfer the learned knowledge in the decision-making problem for autonomous vehicles. The dueling network is trained by the gradient descent technique in (9). The trained parameters of these networks from one driving task are transformed and applied into another driving mission under transfer learning framework. By doing this, the training time can be reduced sharply. Taking the control performance as the primary goal, this transfer RL concept is capable of being applied in online implementations.

## C. Transfer Learning for Knowledge Transformation

The selection of control actions by the intelligent agent in RL is a process of trial and error. It is always time-consuming. For different studied problems, the training progress needs to be repeated. Moreover, the performance of deep RL methods is extremely dependent on the setting of hyperparameters. How to improve the training efficiency and ensure the control effectiveness is still a challenge in deep RL research.

The transfer learning technique is a novel paradigm of machine learning [30]. This approach tries to transform the learned knowledge from the source tasks to the target tasks. For example, regarding the studied decision-making problem in this paper, the goal is to drive as fast as possible without collision. The related tasks are turning left, going straight, and turning right at the unsignalized intersection. Hence, the control policy derived from one task is able to be applied in another mission. The integration of transfer learning and deep RL is an efficient modality to promote learning efficiency. Several attempts have been made in different research fields [31]- [32].

For the deep RL methods, the transformation in transfer learning means the reuse of the parameters of NN. The NN in the source tasks is named expert network, and the NN in the target tasks is the student network. For specification in this work, the transfer learning represents altering the epsilon greedy strategy for control action choice. Three rules are defined to determine the control action in the target tasks [33], as shown in Fig. 3. The transfer rule states that with probability $p_{1}$, the agent chooses the action suggested by the expert network. The related probability is described as follows:

$$
p_{1}=\beta_{0}\left(1-t / T_{\text {tran }}\right)
$$

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/c1653ac1-e2ea-4515-9191-167a02903da7-04.jpg?height=571&width=679&top_left_y=228&top_left_x=1156)
Fig. 3. Changed epsilon greedy policy for action selection in the target task.

where $\beta_{0}$ is the initial transfer belief, and $T_{\text {tran }}$ is the transfer period, during which the agent in the target network is affected by the expert network. $T_{\text {exp }}$ is the initial exploration period in the target task.

The second rule is exploration, which implies that the agent selects a random action with probability $p_{2}=\varepsilon\left(1-p_{1}\right)$. The $\varepsilon$ in epsilon greedy strategy is annealed from 1 to 0.1 . The third rule is exploitation, and it indicates that the agent chooses the best control from the student network with probability $p_{3}=$ $(1-\varepsilon)\left(1-p_{1}\right)$. Guiding by these three rules, the agent could efficiently utilize the knowledge from the expert network. This feature is capable of maintaining performance and elevate the training efficiency in the target tasks.

In this work, the driving tasks are three types, which are turning left, turning right, and going straight. For the automated vehicle in the intersection environment, one of these missions is set as a source task, and the remaining two tasks are target tasks. For different source tasks, we evaluate the learned expert in the above three tasks. The relevant drivability, collision conditions, and learning efficiency of the studied autonomous vehicles are estimated. In the next section, the driving scenario, behavior controller, and default parameters of the deep RL technique are expounded, wherein the transfer RL method is employed to construct the decision-making strategy for self-driving vehicles.

## B. Behavior Controller

In this paper, a double-layer framework is used to control the behavior of surrounding vehicles and AEV. The upper frame manages the longitudinal behavior and the lower frame manages the lateral behavior. In detail, for AEV, the upper framework uses decision-making based on deep RL to manage longitudinal behavior, while the lower framework implements a low-level controller which allows AEV to track a given target speed and follow a target lane. For the surrounding vehicles, the lower frame is the same as AEV, using a low-level controller to manage the lateral behavior, while the upper frame utilizes an intelligent driver model.

More concretely, the lateral controller is a simple proportional-derivative controller, combined with some nonlinearities. The lateral controller consists of two parts, position control and heading control. The expression of heading control is as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\psi_{\mathrm{t}} & =\psi_{L}+\Delta \psi_{t} \\
\dot{\psi}_{\mathrm{t}} & =K_{p, \psi}\left(\psi_{t}-\psi\right) \\
\delta & =\arcsin \left(\frac{1}{2} \frac{l}{v} \dot{\psi}_{t}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\psi_{t}$ is the target heading to follow the lane heading and position, $\psi_{L}$ is the lane heading and $K_{p, \psi}$ is the heading control gain. $\psi$ is the heading angle, $\delta$ is the steering angle, $v$ is the forward speed, and $l$ is the vehicle length. Position control is represented as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
v_{l} & =-K_{p, l} \Delta d_{l} \\
\Delta \psi_{t} & =\arcsin \left(\frac{v_{l}}{v}\right)
\end{aligned}
$$

where $v_{l}$ is the required lateral velocity, $K_{p, l}$ represents the position control gain and $\Delta d_{l}$ is the lateral position of the vehicle with respect to the lane center-line.

For surrounding vehicles, the upper framework uses the intelligent driver model to manage longitudinal behavior, which helps generate realistic features of road conditions [35]. The desired distance $\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{d}}$ can be calculated by the following formula:

$$
d_{d}=d_{0}+T \cdot v+\frac{v \cdot \Delta v}{2 \sqrt{a_{\max } \cdot b_{\max }}}
$$

where $d_{0}$ is the minimum distance between one vehicle and another on the same lane, and $T$ is the desired time gap. $a_{\text {max }}$ is the maximum acceleration and $b_{\text {max }}$ is the maximum deceleration according to the comfortable purpose. $\Delta v$ is the relative velocity

## C. Default Setup of Parameters

As described in Section II, this paper regards the decisionmaking of intersection as an MDP, which is represented by state space, action space, a transition model, and a reward function. These settings are described below in this part.

For state-space, the focus of observation is the position $s$ and speed $v$ of the vehicle. In order to facilitate the calculation, the position and speed are respectively expressed in the horizontal direction and vertical direction of the intersection. So for each vehicle observed, its state $S_{i}=\left\{s^{i}{ }_{x}, s^{i}{ }_{y}, v^{i}{ }_{x}, v^{i}{ }_{y}\right\}$, where $i$ means the index of the vehicle, and $x$ represents the horizontal direction of the intersection, and $y$ represents the vertical direction of the intersection. Finally, the state space can be expressed as $S=$ $\left\{S_{a}, S_{1}, S_{2}, \ldots, S_{n}\right\}$, where $a$ means the index of the AEV, and $n$ is the total number of surrounding vehicles observed.

As mentioned above in Section II, the steering angle $\delta_{t}$ of the AEV is controlled by a low-level lateral controller. Therefore, the selection of action is decided by deep RL and only affects the longitudinal acceleration of the AEV. The action space is defined as follow:

$$
a_{t} \in[-5,0,5] \mathrm{m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}
$$

The transition model is established by using the 2-DOF bicycle model to get the vehicle speed and position after state transition. The velocity of the vehicle is easily obtained by acceleration, as shown in Eq. (22):

$$
v_{t+1}=v_{t}+a_{t} \cdot d t
$$

where $v_{t+1}$ represents the vehicle velocity of the next time step after $v_{t}$, and $d t$ is the length of one time-step. The heading angle $\psi_{t+1}$ of the vehicle can be calculated from the following formulas:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \beta_{t}=\arctan \left(1 / 2 \tan \delta_{t}\right) \\
& \omega_{t}=2 v_{t} \cdot \sin \beta_{t} / l
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\psi_{t+1}=\psi_{t}+\omega_{t} \cdot d t
$$

where $\delta_{t}$ is the front wheel angle, and $\omega_{t}$ is the yaw rate of the vehicle. $l$ is the vehicle length, and $\beta_{t}$ is the slip angle at the center of gravity of the vehicle. Based on the above formulas, the horizontal velocity $v_{x}$ and the vertical velocity $v_{y}$ can be further calculated:

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
v_{x, t+1}=v_{t+1} \cdot \sin \left(\beta_{t}+\psi_{t}\right) \\
v_{y, t+1}=v_{t+1} \cdot \cos \left(\beta_{t}+\psi_{t}\right)
\end{array}\right.
$$

Since the velocity is known, the position $s_{\mathrm{X}}$ in the horizontal direction and $s_{\mathrm{y}}$ in the vertical direction can be obtained:

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
s_{x, t+1}=s_{x, t}+v_{x, t+1} \cdot d t \\
s_{y, t+1}=s_{y, t}+v_{y, t+1} \cdot d t
\end{array}\right.
$$

Guided by reward signals, AEV learns how to act in their environment. Learning may be difficult due to sparse or delayed rewards, so the proper reward is crucial. Considering efficiency and safety, the reward is set as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& r= \\
& \left\{\begin{array}{cl}
1 \cdot \text { highest-speed-5⋅ collision, }, & \text { if not reach the } \\
\text { ending point } \\
1, & \text { if reach the ending point }
\end{array}\right.
\end{aligned}
$$

where collision $=1$ if AEV collides, otherwise collision $=0$. Similarly, highest-speed $=1$ if the velocity of the AEV is the highest, otherwise highest-speed $=0$. Since the speed is state variable, it can be decided by current state and control action. Thus, the reward in (28) are determined by state variables and control actions. This reward function is set to encourage AEV to execute a vertical left turn to reach the destination as soon as possible without collision.

Finally, the presented decision-making strategy for autonomous vehicles at intersection is trained and assessed in the simulation environment based on the OpenAI gym Python toolkit [36]. In the simulation, the AEV was trained 4000 episodes. The duration of each episode is 13 seconds and the time-step of the control policy in every episode is 1/5 second. The driving scenario is constructed as Fig. 4. In the scenario, there are 10 surrounding vehicles. The length of each vehicle is 4 m. During each episode, the longitudinal and lateral motion of the surrounding vehicles are controlled by the intelligent driver model and the proportional-derivative controller.

In the deep Q-learning and dueling deep Q-learning algorithm, the discount rate $\gamma$ is 0.99, the learning rate is 0.001. The size of the batch is 64 , and the capacity of memory is 15000 . In the transfer learning algorithm, the initial transfer belief $\beta_{0}$ is 0.9 , and the transfer period $T_{\text {tran }}$ is 300 , the $\varepsilon$ in epsilon greedy strategy is annealed from 1 to 0.1 . With respect to the decision-making problems at intersections, the trained networks from one driving task are transformed into the target mission. Since the dueling deep Q-learning is demonstrated to be superior to the deep Q-learning in this work, the dueling deep Q-learning with and without transfer learning are compared in this part.

## V. Conclusion

This paper proposes a transfer RL framework to improve the control performance and learning efficiency for automated vehicles' decision-making problems. Three driving tasks are involved, which are left-turning, going straight, and right-turning. The learned knowledge from the source driving task is transformed into the target missions. Three aspects of evaluated experiments are conducted to expound the advantages of the combination of dueling deep Q-learning and transfer learning. Results indicate that the proposed decision-making policy could improve learning efficiency and performance.

Future works aim to realize the online implementation of the decision-making policies for autonomous vehicles. The connected environment can be discussed. The real-world driving data is able to be applied. More advanced deep RL algorithms are capable of being utilized.
