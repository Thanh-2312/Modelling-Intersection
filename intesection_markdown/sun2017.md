#### Abstract

With the advent of connected and automated vehicle technology, in this paper, we propose an innovative intersection operation scheme named as MCross: Maximum Capacity inteRsection Operation Scheme with Signals. This new scheme maximizes intersection capacity by utilizing all lanes of a road simultaneously. Lane assignment and green durations are dynamically optimized by solving a multi-objective mixed-integer non-linear programming problem. The demand conditions under which full capacity can be achieved in MCross is derived analytically. Numerical examples show that MCross can almost double the intersection capacity (increase by as high as 99.51\% in comparison to that in conventional signal operation scheme).

## 1. Introduction

Intersections are frequent bottlenecks of an urban road network, with limited capacity that may cause residual queues and excessive congestion. To improve the utilization of the intersection capacity, some conventional mitigation strategies, such as throughput maximization, queue balancing, negative offset, metering and gating, have been proposed and utilized to reduce congestion to certain extent (e.g. Gazis, 1964; Lieberman et al., 2000; Liu and

[^0]Chang, 2011; Sun et al., 2015a, 2016). However, further improvement of intersection efficiency by merely adjusting signal control parameters under conventional intersection operation scheme is very difficult. The inefficiency of current intersections is not merely because their capacity is under-utilized, but, more importantly, because the capacity itself is not maximized but constrained by conventional intersection management scheme.

At a conventional intersection, capacity can be calculated for a lane or a lane group under given roadway, geometric, traffic, and control conditions (HCM, 2010). Considering the capacity of an intersection, it should be the summation of capacity for all lane groups (Coates et al., 2012). If more lane groups can be served simultaneously, the intersection capacity would be greater. To increase intersection capacity, researchers have proposed several unconventional intersection designs or traffic operation schemes. To date, there are mainly two types of unconventional intersection designs that potentially increase intersection capacity by simultaneously utilizing all approaching lanes of a road. The first type is represented by the continuous flow intersection (CFI) (Goldblatt et al., 1994), which employs displaced left-turn lanes to eliminate the conflict between left-turn traffic and opposing through traffic at an intersection so that both movements can pass the intersection simultaneously in one signal phase (Fig. 1(a)). Other similar designs include parallel flow intersection (PFI) (Parsons, 2007) and CFI-Lite (Sun et al., 2015b). The second type is the tandem intersection (TI), which arranges left-turn vehicles and through vehicles in tandem queues on approaching lanes so that each movement can use all lanes during its green time (Fig. 1 (b)) (Xuan et al., 2011). However, for CFI or TI, capacity can be fully utilized only if the demand matches well with the geometry regarding the number of lanes for each movement and the location of pre-signals. Zhao et al. (2015) presented an integrated optimization of lane markings, the length of the displaced left-turn lane and the signal timings to further improve the efficiency of CFI and PFI. However, their method is applicable only when the traffic demand is stable. Due to cycle-by-cycle fluctuations in traffic volumes of different movements, the intersection capacity with these unconventional designs, albeit usually being increased significantly, can be hardly tapped to its fullest.

The advent of connected and automated vehicle (CAV) technology provides promising opportunities to improve intersection operation with additional degree of freedom. Towards this end, numerous research has been proposed aiming at optimizing vehicles' trajectories based on signal status information (Guler et al., 2014; Rakha and Kamalanathsharma, 2011; He et al., 2015; Wu et al., 2015), optimizing signal operation based on approaching vehicles' trajectories (Goodall et al., 2013; He et al., 2014; Feng et al., 2015), as well as jointly optimizing both signal operation and vehicle trajectory (Li et al., 2014). Reduction of vehicle delay, number of stops, fuel consumption and emissions were reported from their studies. Assuming a 100\% CAV environment, several

Fig. 1. Unconventional intersection designs that increase capacity: (a) continuous flow intersection; (b) tandem intersection.

signal-free intersection management strategies were also proposed based on reservation-based conflict avoidance algorithms (Dresner and Stone, 2008; Fajardo et al., 2011; Lee and Park, 2012; Zohdy and Rakha, 2014). These
strategies, however, mainly focused on longitudinal vehicle control with conventional intersection management. Other than achieving shorter inter-vehicle headways from CAVs (e.g., Talebpour and Mahmassani, 2016), they did not demonstrate evidence of improvement for intersection capacity. A most recent research showed that, in some cases, such reservation-based intersection control methods can cause larger delay and disrupt platoon progression, and therefore are less efficient than conventional traffic signals (Levin et al., 2016). On the other hand, with connectivity and automation of the CAVs, more versatile traffic management strategies become viable by deploying both longitudinal and lateral control, e.g. cooperative lane changing and car following. Thereby, vehicles can be dynamically assigned to different lanes when arriving at the intersection, and virtual CFI or TI can be developed for advanced traffic operation scheme with CAVs. To the best of our knowledge, this type of concept has not yet been introduced.

In this paper, we propose an innovative intersection operation scheme for CAVs. This new scheme inherits the concepts of unconventional intersection design, i.e. CFI or TI, to use all lanes of a road at any time, and benefits from the maneuverability of CAVs to determine the lane assignment dynamically. By doing this, the proposed method can maximize the capacity of intersections with various traffic demand.

## 2. MCross: maximum capacity intersection operation scheme for CAVs

The traffic signal operation scheme proposed in this paper is named as MCross: Maximum Capacity inteRsection Operation Scheme with Signals. The concept of MCross is shown in Fig. 2, in conjunction with the conventional signal operation scheme for comparison. In a conventional operation scheme (Fig. 2(a)), each leg connected to the intersection is divided into two directional (ingress and egress) roadways by a median barrier (the centerline), and then each ingress roadway is further divided into through and left-turn lanes by lane markers. For simplicity, rightturn traffic is not considered in this paper. The signal phases are illustrated by a series of intersection-centered squares in dashed lines. Vehicles in the innermost square can pass the intersection within current phase if they have the right-of-way, and vehicles in any ring between two squares can pass the intersection in the subject phase if not blocked by queues. Conventionally, four phases in a cycle are required to serve through and left-turn movements from all legs. For example, the first phase ( $\Phi_{1}$ in Fig. 2(a)) is used to serve eastbound (EB) left-turn and westbound (WB) left-turn movements, leaving the capacity of any other approaching lane unused.

In MCross, with all vehicles being CAVs, the capacity of an intersection can be maximized following two steps. First, the arriving vehicles from each approach will be grouped as platoons as shown in Fig. 2(b). The platoons are scheduled to arrive at the intersection sequentially such that vehicles would not have to stop at the stop line during red period or gradually accelerate at the beginning of green period. In the second step, left-turn and through vehicles will be assigned as those in CFI or TI before they reach the stop line. More importantly, the lane assignment (in other words, the positions of CAV platoons from certain movements) in a phase is not static but can be changed dynamically. For example, as shown in Fig. 2(c)-(d), three sub-phases, $\Phi_{1-1}, \Phi_{1-2}$ and $\Phi_{1-3}$, constitute phase $\Phi_{1}$. These sub-phases can be activated sequentially without any transition period, as that in the TI. Also, all the left-turn traffic is assigned to the left most lanes of the road to prevent conflicts with opposing through traffic at the intersection, as that in the CFI. One phase can have several sub-phases of different types and durations, depending on the volume and turning ratio of arriving traffic. With properly selected sub-phases, the intersection can work at full capacity under various demand conditions.

Note that an important assumption of MCross is that CAVs can drive to specific lanes upon reaching the intersection. Especially, if a CAV needs to cross the centerline to lanes originally used to serve traffic toward the opposing direction, it must not conflict with any opposing traffic. In general, this could be done when through traffic from the perpendicular road is given green time. We leave the investigation on this issue for our future research.

## 3. The capacity maximization problem

In MCross, the main tasks are maximizing intersection capacity in a phase serving one road, and allocating green splits for the two crossing roads in a cycle. Note that optimizing the green splits for different phases has been well studied in the literature. The first task is more essential because the more the capacity can be achieved on one road, the less green time is needed, and the more freedom we have in allocating green splits. The problem of maximizing the capacity of one road is the focus of this paper. We take the EB/WB road as an example, and formulate the capacity optimization problem as a multi-objective mixed-integer non-linear programming (MO-MINLP) problem, with details described in the following.

- Input parameters:

The capacity maximization problem is based on two sets of input parameters. One is the demand $\mathbf{D}=\left[d_{\mathrm{EL}} d_{\mathrm{WT}} d_{\mathrm{ET}}\right.$ $\left.d_{\mathrm{WL}}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}$, where $d_{\mathrm{EL}} d_{\mathrm{WT}} d_{\mathrm{ET}}$ and $d_{\mathrm{WL}}$ are the arrival demand for EB left-turn, WB through, EB through and WB left-turn movements, respectively. The other is the default lane assignment of the road, $M$ lanes for EB traffic and $N$ lanes for WB traffic (see Fig. 2(b)). Note again that lane assignment is fixed only in the middle segment of the road. The total $M+N$ lanes will be collected together and dynamically re-assigned to all EB/WB movements near the intersection. Also, $P$ lanes for southbound (SB) traffic and $Q$ lanes for northbound (NB) traffic are assumed.

- Decision variables:

First, we need to design a sequence of sub-phases $\mathbf{S}_{k}, k=1,2, \ldots, K$, each with different lane assignment for the CAVs to follow. $\mathbf{S}_{k}=\left[n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{EL}} n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{WT}} n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{ET}} n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{WL}}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}$, where $n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{EL}}, n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{WT}}, n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{ET}}$ and $n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{WL}}$ are the number of lanes for EB left-turn movement, WB through movement, EB through movement and WB left-turn movement, respectively. Note again that the left-turn lanes are allocated to the left most roadway as that in CFI, and the lanes for the four movements have fixed relative position. From north to south, the order of lanes is EB left-turn, WB through, EB through and WB left-turn. Therefore, we only need to determine the number of lanes for each movement, and then the positions of these lanes will be uniquely given. Note here $K$ is not a pre-determined parameter, but also a decision variable. $\mathbf{S}_{k}$ and $K$ are non-negative integer variables. Second, we need to determine the duration for each sub-phases, $t_{k}$, which are non-negative continuous variables.

- Constraints:
a) Conservation of traffic volume and lanes

An admissible solution needs to ensure that all the demand is served.

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_{M}^{k} t_{k} s \geq d_{M}, \quad M={ }^{\prime} E L^{\prime}, \quad \text { ' } W T^{\prime},{ }^{\prime} E T^{\prime}, \quad \text { ' } W L^{\prime}
$$

where $s$ is the saturation flow rate for one single lane, which is assumed to be constant for both through and left-turn lanes. We also assume that saturation flow rate for a lane with CAVs is the same as that with regular vehicles, so that the difference of intersection capacity solely comes from how traffic is operated at the intersection.

For each sub-phase, the total number of assigned lanes should be equal to the number of lanes for the road.

$$
n_{E L}^{k}+n_{W T}^{k}+n_{E T}^{k}+n_{W L}^{k}=M+N
$$

b) Enough downstream receiving space

The number of exit lanes on the receiving arm must be greater than or equal to the number of approaching lanes on the sending arm.

$$
n_{E L}^{k} \leq Q, n_{W T}^{k} \leq N, n_{E T}^{k} \leq M, n_{W L}^{k} \leq P
$$

In fact, the constraints should be set stricter, because it is possible that the downstream egress lanes are occupied by ingress vehicles from the other direction. Therefore, we must ensure that there are enough spaces for the traffic to go through the intersection as well as the downstream links, i.e.,

$$
\left\{\begin{array}{l}
n_{W T}^{i}+n_{E T}^{j}+n_{E L}^{j} \leq M+N \\
n_{E T}^{i}+n_{W T}^{j}+n_{W L}^{j} \leq M+N
\end{array}, \text { for any } i<j\right.
$$

c) Limited lane change

Although the temporary use of opposite lanes can increase intersection capacity, it would be desirable to restrict this usage to prevent frequent back-and-forth lane changing. A reasonable way is to allow at most one lane in each direction to be used by opposing traffic

$$
\begin{aligned}
& N-1 \leq n_{E L}^{k}+n_{W T}^{k} \leq N+1, \text { or } \\
& M-1 \leq n_{E T}^{k}+n_{W L}^{k} \leq M+1
\end{aligned}
$$

With current constraints, it is still possible that through traffic from both directions would travel towards each other on one lane, imposing safety concerns. Although the constraints ensure that there are enough spaces (adjacent vacant lanes) for CAVs to change lanes and avoid collision, it will be desired to minimize this type of lane change.

- Objectives:

Two objectives are considered to guarantee the efficiency and safety of the intersection. The primary objective is to maximize the capacity of the road at the intersection. For this objective, there is an evident upper bound, i.e., the full capacity when all the $M+N$ lanes are utilized at any time during the phase serving EB/WB traffic. Alternatively, given any demand, the objective is to minimize the time needed to serve it.

$$
\min J_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{K} t_{k}
$$

Then, the corresponding ideally best value with full intersection capacity is expressed as follows

$$
J_{1}^{*}=\frac{d_{E L}+d_{W T}+d_{E T}+d_{W L}}{s(M+N)}
$$

The secondary objective is to minimize the number of vehicles that need to cross the centerline to the roadway originally used by on-coming traffic. Note that in MCross, all left-turn traffic must cross the centerline, and through traffic may cross the centerline in some cases. Considering constraint (5), if the total number of lanes for EB left-turn and WB through traffic is less than $N$, one WB lane will be temporarily used by EB through traffic; if the total number of lanes for EB left-turn and WB through traffic is greater than $N$, one EB lane will be temporarily used by WB through traffic; if the total number of lanes for EB left-turn and WB through traffic equals $N$, no through traffic will cross centerline. For any sub-phase $k$, the number of crossing vehicles is

$$
n_{c v}^{k}=\left\{\begin{array}{rl}
t_{k} s\left(n_{E L}^{k}+n_{W L}^{k}\right), & \text { if } n_{E L}^{k}+n_{W T}^{k}=N \\
t_{k} s\left(n_{E L}^{k}+n_{W L}^{k}+1\right), & \text { else }
\end{array},\right.
$$

and the secondary objective can be expressed as

$$
\min J_{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{K} n_{c v}^{k}
$$

- The MO-MINLP problem:

Overall, the MO-MINLP problem is formulated as follows

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text { MO-MINLP: Find } & K, \mathbf{S}=\left[\begin{array}{llll}
n_{E L}^{1} & n_{E L}^{2} & \ldots & n_{E L}^{K} \\
n_{W T}^{1} & n_{W T}^{2} & \ldots & n_{W T}^{K} \\
n_{E T}^{1} & n_{E T}^{2} & \ldots & n_{E T}^{K} \\
n_{W L}^{1} & n_{W L}^{2} & \ldots & n_{W L}^{K}
\end{array}\right] \text {, and } \mathbf{t}=\left[\begin{array}{llll}
t_{1} & t_{2} & \ldots & t_{K}
\end{array}\right] \text {, to } \\
\text { minimize } & \begin{array}{l}
\boldsymbol{J}=\left\{J_{1}, J_{2}\right\} \\
\text { s.t. } \\
\\
\\
\mathbf{S} \geq \mathbf{0} \\
\mathbf{t} \geq \mathbf{0}
\end{array}
\end{aligned}
$$

## 4. Solution method

Solving an MO-MINLP is not easy in general. However, the MO-MINLP in this paper can be decomposed to several simpler problems and solved with a hierarchical method, also known as the lexicographic method (Miettinen, 1999). Specifically, the MO-MINLP can be solved in three steps.

- Step 1: Find all feasible solution for integer variables

The difficultly of solving an MINLP problem is mainly due to the integer variables and non-linear constraints. Considering lane assignment for a road with fixed total number of lanes, there are limited numbers of feasible solutions. Note that in MCross, lanes serving the same traffic movements are allocated together and the relative position of each lane group is fixed. From combinatorics, it is easy to know that for a road with $M+N$ lanes, the total number of lane assignments for the four different traffic movements are $(M+N+3)!/(M+N)!/ 3!$, i.e., $M+N+3$ choose 3. After enumerating all the combinations, we check each combination, i.e., each lane assignment, with constrains (2) (3) and (5), and then get all feasible solutions $\mathbf{Z}_{h}, h=1,2, \ldots, H$, where $H$ is the total number of feasible solutions.

After doing this, constraint (1) can be reduced to a linear one. We can rewrite constraint (1) as follows

$$
\sum_{h=1}^{H} n_{M}^{h} T_{h} s \geq d_{M}, \quad M={ }^{\prime} E L^{\prime}, \quad \text { ' } W T^{\prime}, \quad, \quad E T^{\prime}, \quad, \quad W L
$$

where $h$ is the index of different feasible solutions, and $T_{h}$ is the time duration for the $h^{\text {th }}$ feasible solution.
Constraints (10) and (1) are equivalent. Denote the set of optimal lane assignment solutions as $\mathbf{S}^{*}$. For any $h$ and $k$, if $\mathbf{Z}_{h}=\mathbf{S}_{k}{ }^{*}$, let $T_{h}=t_{k}$; else, $T_{h}=0$. In other words, constraint (10) assumes all feasible solutions of lane assignment are used, while assignments not included in the optimal set are with zero duration. Every initial solution $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{t}$ can be mapped to a unique vector $\mathbf{T}=\left[T_{1} T_{2} \ldots T_{H}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}$ with the method above, and every new decision vector T can be mapped to a unique solution $\mathbf{S}$ and $\mathbf{t}$ given the order of non-zero values in $\mathbf{T}$. The decision of order for these sub-phases will be discussed later.

- Step 2: Optimize the primary objective

With (10), optimization of the primary objective is reduced to a linear programming problem. The secondary objective will be discussed later.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text { LP1: Find } & \mathbf{T}, \text { to } \\
\text { minimize } & J_{1}=\mathbf{A T} \\
\text { s.t. } & \mathbf{B T} \geq \mathbf{D}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\mathbf{A}$ is a $1 \times H$ vector whose elements are all $1, \mathbf{B}=\left[\mathbf{Z}_{1} \mathbf{Z}_{2} \ldots \mathbf{Z}_{H}\right]$ is the $4 \times H$ matrix that includes each feasible lane assignment in one column, $\mathbf{T}=\left[\begin{array}{llll}T_{1} & T_{2} & \ldots & T_{H}\end{array}\right]^{\mathrm{T}}$ is the $H \times 1$ vector of durations for each lane assignment (subphase). $\mathbf{B T} \geq \mathbf{D}$ is equivalent to (10) and (1).

Note that multiple optimal solutions may exist for LP1.

- Step 3: Optimize the secondary objective.

Considering the secondary objective of minimizing the number of crossing vehicles, the expression of $J_{2}$ can be further simplified as follows. Recall the total number of left-turn vehicles in each direction is assumed a fixed input value. Minimizing the number of crossing vehicles is equivalent to minimizing number of crossing through vehicles, or minimizing the total time allocated to sub-phases in which through vehicles occupy one opposite directional lane. From (5), we know $n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{EL}}+n^{k}{ }_{\mathrm{WT}}-N \in\{-1,0,1\}$, so the secondary objective can be expressed alternatively as

$$
\min J_{2}=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\left(n_{E L}^{h}+n_{W T}^{h}-N\right)^{2} T_{h}=\mathbf{C T}
$$

where $\mathbf{C}$ is a $1 \times H$ vector of constants.
With the optimal value of $J_{1}{ }^{*}$ derived from LP1 in Step 2, optimization of the secondary objective can be formulated as another linear programming problem.

```
LP2: Find T, to
    minimize \(J_{2}=\mathbf{C T}\)
        s.t. \(\quad \mathbf{A T}=J_{1}{ }^{*}\)
            \(\mathbf{T} \geq \mathbf{0}\)
```

After getting $\mathbf{T}^{*}$ from solving LP1 and LP2, the order of sub-phases can be determined according to the number of left-turn phases in each sub-phase with non-zero values in $\mathbf{T}^{*}$.

It is worth noting that none of the three steps is time consuming or computationally intensive. Therefore, the proposed MO-MINLP problem for MCross can be easily solved in real time, which smooths the way for real-world implementation in the future.

## 5. Full capacity condition

One interesting problem that follows the MO-MINLP problem is whether the ideally best value $J_{1}{ }^{*}$ given by (7) is attainable in MCross, i.e., whether the full capacity can be achieved. To this end, only the primary objective and LP1 is discussed in this section.

Given the fixed geometric design of an intersection, the attainability of full capacity depends on the input demand D. Assume that sufficient demand exists for each movement. Non-full capacity could be caused by the variability of the proportions of the total demand for each movement. For the ease of illustration, in the following, the demand for each movement is expressed as a percentage of the total demand, and the time for each sub-phase is expressed as a percentage of $J_{1}{ }^{*}$. Therefore, D and T are now in percentage, and the original LP1 can be equivalently transformed to the following new linear programming problem with

```
LP1n: Find T, to
    minimize \(J_{1}=\mathbf{A T}\)
        s.t. \(\mathbf{B T} \geq \mathbf{D}\)
            \(\mathbf{0} \leq \mathbf{T} \leq \mathbf{1}\)
            \(\mathbf{0} \leq \mathbf{D} \leq \mathbf{1}\)
```

The value $J_{1}{ }^{*}$ is a function of $\mathbf{D}$ and the theoretical best value is $J_{1}{ }^{*}=1$.
Proposition 1. $J_{1}{ }^{*}=\mathrm{f}(\mathbf{D})$ is a convex function of $\mathbf{D}$.
Proof. By the definition of a convex function, for any $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}$ and $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}$ that satisfy the constraints in LP1n and $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}} \neq \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}$, and any $\theta, 0<\theta<1$, we will prove that $\mathrm{f}\left(\theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right) \leq \theta \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\theta) \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)$.

Let $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}=\theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}$. It is easy to know that $\mathbf{0} \leq \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}} \leq \mathbf{1}$, i.e., $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}$ also satisfies the constraints in LP1n. Therefore, the domain of the function is a convex set. Denote the optimal solution $\mathbf{T}$ and optimal value of $J_{1}$ as $\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{a}}{ }^{*}$ and $J_{1 \mathrm{a}}{ }^{*}$ if $\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}$, $\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{b}}{ }^{*}$ and $J_{1 \mathrm{~b}}{ }^{*}$ if $\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}$, and $\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{c}}{ }^{*}$ and $J_{1 \mathrm{c}}{ }^{*}$ if $\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}$. Then we have the following inequalities

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathbf{B T}_{\mathrm{a}}{ }^{*} \geq \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}} \\
& 0 \leq \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{a}}{ }^{*} \leq 1 \\
& 0 \leq \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}} \leq 1
\end{aligned}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathrm{BT}_{\mathrm{b}}{ }^{*} \geq \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{b}} \\
& 0 \leq \mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{b}}{ }^{*} \leq 1 \\
& 0 \leq \mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{b}} \leq 1
\end{aligned}
$$

Multiply (12) by $\theta$ and multiply (13) by $1-\theta$, and then sum up together, we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathbf{B}\left(\theta \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{a}}^{*}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{b}}^{*}\right) \geq \theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}} \\
& \mathbf{0} \leq \theta \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{a}}^{*}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{b}}^{*} \leq \mathbf{1} \\
& \mathbf{0} \leq \theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}} \leq \mathbf{1}
\end{aligned}
$$

The inequalities in (14) indicate that $\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{c}}=\theta \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{a}}{ }^{*}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{b}}{ }^{*}$ is a feasible solution for LP1n if $\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}$. From the definition of function $J_{1}{ }^{*}=\mathrm{f}(\mathbf{D})$, we have

$$
J_{1 \mathrm{c}}^{*} \leq \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)=\mathbf{A} \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathbf{A}\left(\theta \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{a}}^{*}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{b}}^{*}\right)=\theta J_{1 \mathrm{a}}^{*}+(1-\theta) J_{1 \mathrm{~b}}^{*}
$$

In other words,

$$
\mathrm{f}\left(\theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right) \leq \theta \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\theta) \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)
$$ $\square$

Proposition 2. For a convex function $J_{1}{ }^{*}=\mathrm{f}(\mathbf{D})$ and two input vectors $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}$ and $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}, \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}} \neq \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}$, if there exists $\beta, 0<\beta<1$, such that $\mathrm{f}\left(\beta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\beta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)=\beta \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\beta) \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)$, then for any $\theta, 0<\theta<1, \mathrm{f}\left(\theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)=\theta \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\theta) \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)$.

Proof. We prove it by contradiction. Assume that there is a $\theta, 0<\theta<1$ and $\mathrm{f}\left(\theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right) \neq \theta \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\theta) \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)$. Then we have $\mathrm{f}\left(\theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)<\theta \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\theta) \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)$. Without loss of generality, assume $\theta<\beta$, then there exists $\gamma, \gamma=(\beta-\theta) /(1- \theta)$, such that $\beta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\beta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}=\gamma \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\gamma)\left(\theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)$. From the definition of convex function, we have

$$
\mathrm{f}\left(\beta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\beta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right) \leq \gamma \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\gamma) \mathrm{f}\left(\theta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\theta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)<\gamma \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\gamma)\left(\theta \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\theta) \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)\right) .
$$

Substitute $\gamma=(\beta-\theta) /(1-\theta)$ into (17) and we get $\mathrm{f}\left(\beta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\beta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)<\beta \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\beta) \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)$, which leads to a contradiction to the initial assumption $\mathrm{f}\left(\beta \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+(1-\beta) \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)=\beta \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+(1-\beta) \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right) . \square$

Proposition 2 says that if a curve is convex and the two ends and one middle point on the curve are in a line, then the curve must be a line. Actually, this conclusion can be easily extended to higher dimensions.

Proposition 3. For a convex function $J_{1}{ }^{*}=\mathrm{f}(\mathbf{D})$ and four different input vectors $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}, \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}, \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}$ and $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{d}}$, if there exists $\boldsymbol{\beta}$, $\boldsymbol{\beta}>\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\beta}=\left[\beta_{1} \beta_{2} \beta_{3} \beta_{4}\right]$ and $\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}+\beta_{4}=1$, such that $\mathrm{f}\left(\beta_{1} \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+\beta_{2} \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}+\beta_{3} \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}+\beta_{4} \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{d}}\right)=\beta_{1} \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+\beta_{2} \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)+\beta_{3} \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)+\beta_{4} \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{d}}\right)$, then for any $\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\theta}>\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\theta}=\left[\theta_{1} \theta_{2} \theta_{3} \theta_{4}\right]$ and $\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}+\theta_{4}=1, \mathrm{f}\left(\theta_{1} \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}+\theta_{2} \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}+\theta_{3} \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}+\theta_{4} \mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{d}}\right)=\theta_{1} \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{a}}\right)+\theta_{2} \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{b}}\right)+\theta_{3} \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{c}}\right)+\theta_{4} \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{d}}\right)$.

Proof. It can be proved using similar method as that in the proof of Proposition 2. □

Now we can derive the full capacity condition using these propositions. In the problem studied in this paper, the total demand comprises four proportions corresponding to EB through, EB left-turn, WB through and WB left-turn movements. The four proportions sum up to 100\%. Therefore, three proportions of different movements are enough as state variables. Here we select the first three of them as state variables. Given the geometric parameters such as $M$, $N, P$ and $Q$, we can first enumerate all the feasible solutions for lane assignment as discussed in Step 1 in Section 4. Intuitively, if the ratio of demand for each movement equals the ratio of number of lanes for that movement, all the demand can be served at full capacity. For each feasible lane assignment $\mathbf{Z}_{h}$, let $\mathbf{D}_{h}=\mathbf{Z}_{h} /(M+N)$. It is easy to know that the corresponding optimal solution and optimal value of the objective function for LP1n with $\mathbf{D}=\mathbf{D}_{h}$ are $\mathbf{T}^{*}=\mathbf{T}_{h}$ and $J_{1 h}{ }^{*}=1$, which is the theoretical optimal one. Therefore, $\mathbf{D}_{h}, h=1,2, \ldots, H$, provide $H$ characteristic points in the state space that can utilize full capacity.

Proposition 4. The convex hull of the characteristic points $\left\{\mathbf{D}_{h}\right\}$ gives the full capacity condition.

Proof. The proof is two-fold: 1) every point inside the convex hull represents a demand that can be served at full capacity, and 2) every demand that can be served at full capacity can be represented by a point inside the convex hull.

First, it is easy to know that the convex hull of the characteristic points is a polyhedron and $J_{1 h}{ }^{*}=1$ holds for any node of the polyhedron. From Proposition 1, for any point $\mathbf{D}_{\text {in }}$ inside the polyhedron, $J_{\text {lin }}{ }^{*}=\mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\text {in }}\right) \leq \mathrm{f}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{h}}\right)=1$. On the other hand, from (7) and the formulation of LP1n, we have $J_{1 \mathrm{in}}{ }^{*} \geq J_{1}{ }^{*}=1$. Consequently, from Proposition 3, $J_{1 \mathrm{in}}{ }^{*}=1$ holds for any point inside the polyhedron.

Second, assume any demand $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{x}}$ that can be served by $\mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{x}}$ with full capacity, i.e., $J_{1 \mathrm{x}}{ }^{*}=\mathbf{A} \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{x}}=T_{\mathrm{x} 1}+T_{\mathrm{x} 2}+\ldots+T_{\mathrm{x} H}=1$. Then, $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{x}}$ can be alternatively expressed as $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{x}}=\mathbf{B} \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{x}}=T_{\mathrm{x} 1} \mathbf{Z}_{1}+T_{\mathrm{x} 2} \mathbf{Z}_{2}+\ldots+T_{\mathrm{x} H} \mathbf{Z}_{H}$. Recall the constraint $\mathbf{0} \leq \mathbf{T}_{\mathrm{x}} \leq \mathbf{1}$, therefore, $\mathbf{D}_{\mathrm{x}}$ is inside the convex hull of $\left\{\mathbf{D}_{h}\right\}$. □

## 7. Concluding remarks

This research is motivated by taking advantage of the controllability of CAVs to maximize intersection capacity. To this end, an innovative intersection operation scheme named MCross is proposed. MCross is able to serve twodirection traffic from one road in one signal phase and maximizes the intersection capacity by utilizing all lanes from the road at any time. The capacity maximization problem is formulated as an MO-MINLP problem and decomposed to several simple problems to facilitate solving. The results showed that MCross performed much better than the conventional one and was able to attain full capacity in many cases.

In MCross, to drive all CAVs cooperatively to their designated lanes in time could be challenging, especially for CAVs that need to cross the centerline and avoid collision with on-coming traffic. Another important problem to be addressed is the dynamic update of traffic arrivals. Although MCross has great flexibility to deal with demand fluctuation by dynamic lane assignment, its performance could still be influenced because of the sub-phase sequence constraint. To address these problems, a dynamic optimal motion planning algorithm for CAVs is under development in our ongoing research.

So far, MCross is limited to theoretical analysis. Field implementation would not be possible until a sufficient amount of CAVs are available in the real world. Fortunately, one impressive feature of MCross is that it does no rely on 100\% CAVs environment. MCross can be implemented to an intersection of a dedicated CAV road and a regular road, which could be seen in much nearer future. While discussing the subject road serving CAVs in previous sections, there is no requirement for the other road. The other road can serve mixed traffic of regular vehicles and CAVs as long as only CAVs are permitted to turn into the dedicated CAV road. In this case, CAVs from the dedicated road can pass the intersection with high capacity in one phase, while the mixed traffic from the other road follows conventional signal phases (including protected/permissive left-turn phases). Moreover, the green time savings from the dedicated CAV road can be utilized to serve the other road, making regular vehicles also benefit from CAV technologies in MCross.

Last but not the least, the scalability to larger control areas is worth investigating for MCross. We leave this for future research.
