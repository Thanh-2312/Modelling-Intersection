#### Abstract

This paper presents an optimization-based approach for safe and efficient intersection crossing traffic management under autonomous and connected ground traffic environment. The proposed algorithm, named MICA (Mixed integer programming based Intersection Coordination Algorithm), generates the fastest intersection crossing discrete-time trajectory for each vehicle approaching an intersection. Constraints are carefully designed and incorporated into the optimization process to avoid collisions while crossing an intersection. The performance of the proposed MICA is evaluated through extensive simulations using a microscopic traffic simulator, SUMO. The simulation results show that MICA performs substantially better in terms of the traffic throughput compared to both an optimized traffic light mechanism and DICA which is an intersection control algorithm proposed in our earlier work.


Index Terms-Autonomous Vehicle, Intelligent Intersection Management, Mixed Integer Programming (MIP).

## I. Introduction

As a potential solution to the traffic congestion problem, autonomous transportation systems, especially autonomous intersection control has attracted a lot of research and development efforts. The problem to be solved in this field is that how to improve the safety and throughput of intersection crossing traffic without using traffic lights or stop signs by leveraging the advantages of autonomous vehicles. Many researchers have investigated various optimization approaches to increase the throughput, such as a nonlinear programming formulation with relaxing nonlinear constraints [1], a model predictive control framework which dynamically generates a sequence of collision-free motions [2], a decentralized framework whereby each autonomous vehicle minimizes energy consumption subject to the requirements of throughput and safety [3], etc. Most of these approaches are trying to avoid the appearance of vehicles with conflicting routes inside an intersection at the same time to ensure safety. Some researchers studied algorithms that allow conflicting vehicles exist inside an intersection simultaneously to further improve performance. To achieve this objective, most approaches discretized the intersection space into grid cells so that vehicles of conflicting routes can exist at the same time within an intersection but not within the same cell. In [4], Autonomous Intersection Management was proposed which allows route-conflicting vehicles enter the intersection simultaneously as long as they don't occupy the same cell at the same time. However, no global coordination is made for crossing vehicles to obtain optimal traffic flow.

There are many research works focusing on the optimization of autonomous intersection control and lots of efforts being devoted to allowing route-conflicting vehicles to exist inside a same intersection to improve efficiency. However, few researchers have tried to optimize the intersection control while allowing the simultaneous appearances of conflicting vehicles inside an intersection. Dai et al. [5] transformed the intersection control model into a convex optimization problem with an objective function of multiple criteria like safe speeds and accelerations to improve the quality of experience from passenger's perspective. They linearized the collision avoidance constraints through scheduling the entering priority of vehicles at the intersection to reduce computational cost. However, this linearization may decrease performance since for example, the scheduling requires the earlier arrival vehicle cross the collision area first for two possibly colliding vehicles. Reference [6] proposed a vehicle-intersection coordination scheme which generates smooth flows of traffic by preventing any pair of conflicting vehicles from approaching their CrossCollision Point at the same time. Considering states of all vehicles, the paper formulated a constrained nonlinear optimization problem to minimize the risk function to generate safe trajectories of vehicles with unused time and space in the intersection space area reduced. Whereas, the algorithm needs more computational cost to coordinate more vehicles since they are optimizing the traffic flow of all approaching vehicles at the same time. And the optimization problem is nonlinear thus the solution is not guaranteed to be the global optimum.

This paper proposes an optimization approach similiar to [5], [6] for intersection control but overcomes their limitations in terms of fixed scheduling and nonlinearity of problem formulation based on our previous research on intelligent intersection coordination algorithms [7]-[9]. We have several main contributions as follows. Our approach is only dealing with head vehicles (see Section II for definition) which largely reduces the computation load of the central control unit and the communication needs between vehicles and the intersection. The algorithm is optimizing a new head vehicle's trajectory using Mixed Integer Programming (MIP) under various constraints thus named MICA (MIP-based Intersection Coordi-
nation Algorithm). The main contribution of the paper is the modeling of potential collisions with space-conflicting vehicles using mixed integer programming by the introduction of binary variables representing the potential behaviors of a vehicle. As a result of this modeling, MICA allows a later arrived vehicle exits the intersection before the earlier conflicting vehicle if performance will be increased. We also modeled immediate front vehicles as constraints in the formulated problem to ensure the vehicle is free from collisions. The problem formulation is simple and achieves the global optimal trajectory for a new head vehicle. The simulation results show that MICA reduces average trip time and fairness among vehicles significantly over existing methods.

The remainder of this paper is organized into five sections. Section II introduce basic assumptions and interaction scheme between an intersection and a vehicle for MICA. The trajectory optimization problem is formulated in Section III with each constraint explained detailedly. In Section IV, we propose the MICA algorithm incorporating the optimization approach. The efficiency of MICA is evaluated through extensive simulations in Section V. Finally, Section VI concludes the paper.

## II. Vehicle-Intersection Interaction

In the intersection traffic we study in this paper, we assume that all vehicles are connected as well as autonomous. Hence we call them CAVs. Specifically, each CAV has the ability of wireless Vehicle-to-Intersection (V2I) communication and also can autonomously avoid collision with its immediate front vehicle while it is approaching an intersection. Also, all CAVs are assumed to be able to obtain their positions, speeds, and the relative distances to the enter line (see Figure 2) of an intersection precisely. In this paper, an intersection traffic is controlled in a centralized manner by an intersection controller, named the Intersection Control Agent (ICA). As mentioned above, the ICA of an intersection communicates with CAVs wirelessly via V2I communication. We define a region, called the communication region, around an intersection within which CAV can communicate with ICA wirelessly. It is also assumed that each CAV is equipped with an RFID chip. Through the detectors installed at the entrance of the communication region, ICA can detect each CAV's VIN (Vehicle's Identification Number), approaching lane, and also the time when the vehicle enters the communication region.

Figure 1 shows the high-level interaction between a CAV and ICA. In the intersection control framework proposed in this paper, we say that a CAV becomes a head vehicle only when there are no other vehicles between the CAV and the entrance of the intersection, or the vehicle which is immediately in front of the CAV has begun to enter the intersection. If a vehicle becomes a head vehicle, it immediately sends a REQUEST message to ICA in order to request an intersection crossing trajectory to be generated for the vehicle to follow. We call such a trajectory a confirmed trajectory. We assume that a REQUEST message contains all necessary information such as a vehicle's current speed, position, intersection crossing route, etc. for ICA to generate a confirmed trajectory. After a

## III. Problem Formulation

In this section, we describe our new approach to improve the overall intersection crossing traffic throughput. We solve the problem of each vehicle's trajectory generation for intersection crossing through a mixed integer programming formulation that makes sure vehicles can cross an intersection as fast as possible while collisions among vehicles are safely avoided.

## A. Single Vehicle Trajectory Generation

We begin to formulate a mixed integer programming optimization problem by first considering the case of trajectory generation for a single vehicle. In this case, we assume that there is only one vehicle approaching an intersection. Then, our goal in this simple situation is to generate a discrete-time trajectory for the vehicle so that it can cross the intersection as quickly as possible while satisfying various constraints, such as the speed and acceleration limits.

Let $\gamma$ be the route (or path) for the vehicle to follow when it crosses an intersection and also let $s$ be the variable that represents the position of the vehicle along the route $\gamma$. We assume that $\gamma$ is fixed for the given incoming and outgoing lanes across an intersection. We also use $h$ to denote the fixed sampling time, $v_{\text {max }}$ to denote the maximum speed limit for the vehicle, $a_{\text {min }}$ and $a_{\text {max }}$ to denote the minimum and maximum acceleration rates respectively. Note that, for a fixed $h$, we can represent the discrete-time trajectory of a vehicle as a sequence of positions of the vehicle along $\gamma$ such as $\left\{s_{0}, s_{1}, s_{2}, \ldots, s_{k}, \ldots, s_{n}\right\}$ for some positive integer number $n$ where $s_{k}$ represents the position of the vehicle along $\gamma$ at the $k$ th time step. Here, we implicitly assume that $s_{i} \leq s_{j}$ if $i<j$ and this relation will be incorporated as a constraint in our optimization problem formulation below. Choose $n$ as
a sufficiently large number. Let $\mathbf{s}=\left[\begin{array}{llll}s_{0} & s_{1} & \cdots & s_{n}\end{array}\right]^{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$. Then the fastest crossing discrete-time trajectory for a vehicle can be obtained by

$$
\max _{\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}} s_{n}
$$

under some constraints which are described below.
The first constraint that we need to consider is the ordering relation between any pair of $s_{i}$ and $s_{j}$ in $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ where $i, j \in[0, n]$. Since s should represent an ordered sequence of positions of the vehicle along $\gamma$ at each sample time step, it is clear that we should constrain the values for each variable $s_{k}$ in s as follows:

$$
s_{k}^{l b} \leq s_{k} \leq s_{k}^{u b}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
s_{k}^{l b}=0 \text { if } k=0, & s_{k}^{l b}=s_{k-1} \text { if } k \neq 0 ; \\
s_{k}^{u b}=s_{k+1} \text { if } k \neq n, & s_{k}^{u b}=v_{\max } \cdot n \cdot h \text { if } k=n .
\end{array}
$$

Next, we note that vehicles on roads should not drive backward and should obey the speed limit which is enforced by laws. Thus, we have the following constraint for speed:

$$
0 \leq v_{k} \leq v_{\max }
$$

where $v_{k}$ is the speed of the vehicle at the $k$ th time step. In order to incorporate this constraint into our optimization formulation in (1), we need to rewrite the velocity variable $v_{k}$ in terms of the positions $s_{k}$ in s as

$$
v_{k}=\frac{s_{k}-s_{k-1}}{h} \quad \text { for } k \in[1, n] \text {. }
$$

In addition to the speed constraint, we also consider constraints for the acceleration (or deceleration) rate of a vehicle in order to make sure the discrete-time trajectory generated by the optimization process is dynamically feasible. Let $a_{k}$ be the acceleration rate of a vehicle at the $k$ th time step. Then, the constraint for acceleration $a_{k}$ is given by

$$
a_{\min } \leq a_{k} \leq a_{\max } \quad \text { for } k \in[1, n] .
$$

Again, in order to incorporate these constraints into the optimization formulation in (1), it is necessary to represent $a_{k}$ in terms of the variables $s_{k}$ in s as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& a_{1}=\frac{v_{1}-v_{0}}{h}=\frac{s_{1}-s_{0}-v_{0} h}{h^{2}}, \\
& a_{k}=\frac{v_{k}-v_{k-1}}{h}=\frac{s_{k}-2 s_{k-1}+s_{k-2}}{h} \quad \text { for } k \in[2, n]
\end{aligned}
$$

where $v_{0}$ is the speed of the vehicle at the 0th time step which we assume is known to ICA via direct measurement or V2I message sent from the vehicle to ICA at that moment.

In this situation, due to the existence of other vehicles, we need to develop additional constraints and include them in the optimization process so that the vehicle of interest can

1) Constraints from space-conflicting vehicles: Suppose that there is a set of vehicles $\mathcal{C}$ which have arrived at the communication region earlier than the vehicle $c^{i}$ and hence their optimal discrete-time trajectories to cross the intersection have already been generated and confirmed. Let $\mathcal{C}^{*}$ be the set of vehicles in $\mathcal{C}$ whose routes are conflicting with the vehicle $c^{i}$ 's route $\gamma^{i}$. Now, let us consider a vehicle $c^{j} \in \mathcal{C}^{*}$ to derive collision avoidance constraints for $c^{i}$ 's optimal trajectory generation. For this, we first note that, as discussed in our previous work [8], for any pair of intersection crossing routes whose both ends (entering and exiting of an intersection) are different from each other, space conflicting (or overlapping) occurs only at a certain small portion of their routes. Also, one can see that these space conflicting short ranges along routes can be determined offline for all pairs of conflicting intersection crossing routes. Thus, to make the vehicle $c^{i}$ avoid any potential collisions with vehicle $c^{j}$, it is necessary to ensure that $c^{i}$ does not occupy the space conflicting area when $c^{j}$ occupies that area.

Figure 2 illustrates an example situation when the vehicle $c^{j}$ is already a confirmed vehicle as the vehicle $c^{i}$ is entering the communication region. In this situation, since there is no other vehicle in front of $c^{i}$ on its lane, $c^{i}$ is the head vehicle and being considered for confirmation by ICA (i.e., the optimal discrete-time trajectory is being generated by ICA). The two long lines with arrows represent the routes of the two vehicles. Their routes have the conflicting area $A$ (with red diagonal lines) which is the area that both of the vehicles should not occupy at the same time to avoid collisions. In the figure, $P_{i n, j}^{i}$
denotes the location on $\gamma^{i}$ that $c^{i}$ starts to enter the area $A$ and $P_{\text {out }, j}^{i}$ denotes the location on $\gamma^{i}$ that $c^{i}$ becomes free from collision with $c^{j}$. Note that we represent the location of a vehicle by the vehicle's front bumper location along its route. Thus we choose the location of $P_{\text {out }, j}^{i}$ to be at one vehicle length $\left(L^{i}\right)$ away from the right boundary of $A$ since the length of a vehicle should also be considered for collision avoidance. Note also that the locations $P_{i n, j}^{i}$ and $P_{o u t, j}^{i}$ along $\gamma^{i}$ are assumed to be known since they can be computed offline for every pair of intersection crossing routes and, in the sequel, we use $\left.\gamma^{i}\right|_{j}$ to denote the conflicting area on $\gamma^{i}$.

Now we note that, for $c^{i}$, there are two ways to avoid any potential collisions with $c^{j}$ : (i) Arrive at $\left.\gamma^{i}\right|_{j}$ after $c^{j}$ exits its $\left.\gamma^{j}\right|_{i}$, or (ii) exit $\left.\gamma^{i}\right|_{j}$ before $c^{j}$ arrives at its $\left.\gamma^{j}\right|_{i}$. To capture this logical condition when solving the optimization problem for trajectory generation for $c^{i}$, we adopt a binary variable $b_{j}^{i} \in\{0,1\}$ to represent $c^{i}$ 's behavior with respect to $c^{j}$ and reformulate our optimization problem in (1) as a mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) problem. Let $p_{i n, j}^{i}$ and $p_{o u t, j}^{i}$ be the distances from the vehicle $c^{i}$ 's current position to $P_{i n, j}^{i}$ and $P_{\text {out }, j}^{i}$ along $\gamma^{i}$ respectively. Then, the constraints for collision avoidance with $c^{j}$ can be represented as

$$
\begin{aligned}
& s_{\tau(i n, j)}^{i} \geq p_{o u t, j}^{i}+M \cdot\left(b_{j}^{i}-1\right) \\
& s_{\tau(o u t, j)}^{i} \leq p_{i n, j}^{i}+M \cdot b_{j}^{i}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $M$ is an arbitrarily large number, $s_{k}^{i}$ is the $c^{i}$ 's position on $\gamma^{i}$ at the $k$ th time step, $\tau(i n, j) \in\{0,1, \ldots, n\}$ is the time step when $c^{j}$ arrives at $P_{i n, i}^{j}$ and $\tau(o u t, j) \in\{0,1, \ldots, n\}$ is the time step when $c^{j}$ completely leaves $\left.\gamma^{j}\right|_{i}$, i.e. $c^{j}$ arrives at $P_{\text {out }, i}^{j}$. Note that $\tau(i n, j)$ and $\tau(o u t, j)$ can be easily computed based on $c^{j}$ 's confirmed optimal discrete-time trajectory. If $b_{j}^{i}=1$, (7) holds and this means that $c^{i}$ is already out of the conflicting area at the time when $c^{j}$ enters the conflicting area. Similarly, if $b_{j}^{i}=0,(8)$ holds meaning that $c^{i}$ is still before the conflicting area at the time when $c^{j}$ exits the conflicting area. Thus, the binary variable $b_{j}^{i}$ ensures that the two equations cannot hold at the same time.

Now we have modeled the constraints from one conflicting vehicle in $\mathcal{C}^{*}$. Following the same process, we can easily extend to include all other vehicles in $\mathcal{C}^{*}$ by computing the corresponding times and location parameters related to their conflicting areas with vehicle $c^{i}$.
2) Constraints from front vehicles: Besides vehicles in $\mathcal{C}^{*}$ considered above, a vehicle which is immediately in front of $c^{i}$ may also have a direct influence on $c^{i}$ 's crossing behavior since $c^{i}$ should plan its trajectory to avoid rear-end collision with its front vehicle. As shown in Figure 3, in crossing an intersection, $c^{i}$ needs to avoid rear-end collisions with three different types of front vehicles: (i) a front vehicle with the same entrance lane ( $c_{e n}^{f}$ ), (ii) a front vehicle with the same exit lane ( $c_{e x}^{f}$ ), and (iii) a front vehicle with the same route $\left(c_{r o}^{f}\right)$. Below, we discuss how to constrain the motion of the vehicle $c^{i}$ to avoid collisions with these different types of front vehicles. First, let us consider the case of collision avoidance with a front vehicle $c_{e n}^{f}$. If we use $\tau(e n, f)$ to denote the time step when $c_{e n}^{f}$ enters the enter line of an intersection and $v_{\tau(e n, f)}^{f}$ is the speed of $c_{e n}^{f}$ at that moment, then, to ensure that the vehicle $c^{i}$ does not collide with $c_{e n}^{f}$ while $c^{i}$ is approaching the intersection, we constrain the motion of $c^{i}$ so that both vehicles are separated in time at least $t_{\text {sep }}$ which can be determined conservatively by assuming that $c^{i}$ is currently approaching an intersection at its maximum speed $v_{\text {max }}$. Thus we define $t_{\text {sep }}$ as

$$
t_{\text {sep }}=\frac{v_{\max }-v_{\tau(e n, f)}^{f}}{\left|a_{\min }\right|}+\frac{g_{\min }}{v_{0}}+\delta .
$$

In (9), $g_{\text {min }}$ is a constant that represents the minimum distance gap between two adjacent vehicles on a same lane regardless of their speeds and $\delta$ is another constant that represents a safety margin in time. The first term in (9) is the time $c^{i}$ needs to decelerate from $v_{\text {max }}$ to $v_{\tau(e n, f)}^{f}$ with its maximum deceleration rate while the second one is the minimum time required to ensure the minimum distance gap $g_{\text {min }}$. Then we can use the following constraint to ensure that $c^{i}$ arrives at the intersection entrance after $\tau(e n, f)$ with a certain amount of time margin:

$$
s_{\tau(e n, i)}^{i} \leq p_{e n}^{i}
$$

where $\tau(e n, i)=\tau(e n, f)+\left\lceil\frac{t_{\text {sep }}}{h}\right\rceil$ is the earliest time step at which $c^{i}$ can arrive at the entrance of the intersection, and $p_{e n}^{i}$ is the location of the intersection entrance on $\gamma^{i}$.

Next, for the case of collision avoidance with a front vehicle $c_{e x}^{f}$, we can develop a constraint using the same idea that introduces a minimum separation time $t_{\text {sep }}$ between $c^{i}$ and $c_{e x}^{f}$ when they exit an intersection. Specifically, we make $c^{i}$ exit the intersection at least $t_{\text {sep }}$ time later after $c_{e x}^{f}$ exits. In this case, $t_{\text {sep }}$ can be obtained in the same way as in (9) except that $v_{\tau(e n, f)}^{f}$ must be replaced by $v_{\tau(e x, f)}^{f}$ in the first term of the equation where $\tau(e x, f)$ is the time step when $c_{e x}^{f}$ exits the intersection and $v_{\tau(e x, f)}^{f}$ is the speed of $c_{e x}^{f}$ at that moment. Following the similar process, we can have the following constraint for $c^{i}$ to avoid collision with $c_{e x}^{f}$ :

$$
s_{\tau(e x, i)}^{i} \leq p_{e x}^{i}
$$

where $\tau(e x, i)=\tau(e x, f)+\left\lceil\frac{t_{\text {sep }}}{h}\right\rceil$ is the earliest time step at which $c^{i}$ can exit the intersection, and $p_{e x}^{i}$ is the location of the intersection exit on $\gamma^{i}$.

Now, to avoid collision with a front vehicle $c_{r o}^{f}$, we note that it can be done by combining constraints from the first two types of front vehicles. Thus, we include both (10) and (11) at the same time as constraints in our optimization problem to constrain the motion of the vehicle $c^{i}$ to avoid collisions with this type of front vehicles.

## V. Simulation

Simulations were implemented in an open-source traffic simulator SUMO (Simulation of Urban MObility) [10] to show the performance of MICA. The algorithm was programmed as Python applications which interact with SUMO through its API: TraCI. The mixed integer programming optimization problem was solved using Gurobi optimization software [11].

## A. Simulation Setup

In simulations, we use a four-way intersection that has three entrance lanes and two exit lanes each way. Among the three entrance lanes, the left-most lane is dedicated for left-turn vehicles while the right-most can be used by right-turn vehicles. Through vehicles can use the right two lanes. The maximum allowed speed $v_{\text {max }}$ for all vehicles is $70 k m / h$. Vehicles enter the communication region of the intersection with different speeds which are randomly chosen within the range from $40 \%$ to 100\% of $v_{\text {max }}$. All vehicles have the same rectangular shape and size ( 5 meters long and 1.8 meters wide). We set $a_{\text {max }}=2 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$ and $a_{\text {min }}=-4.5 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$, respectively. The distance from the enter line of the communication region to that of the intersection region is set as $50 m$.

Vehicles are spawned randomly on each entrance lane with an intersection route which is also randomly chosen. We use several different random seeds to generate different traffic patterns and make simulations reproducible. The probabilities for a new generated vehicle to take the left, through or right route are 20\%, 60\% and 20\% respectively. Traffic volume is represented by the average number of vehicles generated for a 10-minute simulation. We use five different traffic volumes \{100, 200, 300, 400, 500\}. For every volume, we ran simulations of three different traffic patterns to get the average results as the final result for each traffic volume. Simulations were run using $0.05 s$ as the time step and a simulation is terminated when the simulated time reaches 10 minutes. All simulations were run on a 64bit Windows computer and its processor is Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-4770 CPU @ 3.40 GHz with 8 GB RAM.

## B. Simulation Results

The throughput performance of MICA is compared with that of an optimized traffic light algorithm as well as that of DICA proposed in [8]. For the traffic light algorithm, we determined the optimal signal cycles for different traffic volumes using the exponential cycle length model $C_{0}=1.5 L e^{1.8 Y}$ from [12]. In the model, $L$ represents the total lost time within the cycle.

The lost time for each phase is assumed to be 4 seconds [13]. Since there are four phases in a cycle, the value of $L$ is set to 16 in simulations. $Y$ is the sum of critical phase flow ratios. Flow ratio of a lane is the obtained by dividing demand by capacity while critical phase flow ratio is the maximum flow ratio within a same phase. The duration of the yellow light of each phase is 3 seconds.

For each vehicle, the trip time is defined as the time experienced from the moment it enters the communication region to the moment it exits the intersection region. The average trip time is the average of trip times of all vehicles who have crossed the intersection completely during simulation. We also calculated the stopped rate which is defined as the percentage of vehicles who experienced at least one time step of stop when crossing the intersection among all crossed vehicles. Also, we use the Jain's fairness index $f$ [14], [15] to compare the fairness of each algorithm with respect to trip times of crossed vehicles. The Jain's fairness index takes values within [0, 1]. If all crossed vehicles have similar trip times, then $f$ is close to 1 . If we let $\eta_{i}$ be the trip time of vehicle $c^{i}$ who crossed the intersection in a simulation run and $N$ be the number of crossed vehicles, then we can compute the Jain's fairness index $f$ as follows:

$$
f\left(\vec{\eta}=\left[\eta_{1}, \eta_{2}, \cdots, \eta_{N}\right]\right)=\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{i=N} \eta_{i}\right)^{2}}{N \sum_{i=1}^{i=N} \eta_{i}^{2}}
$$

The performance comparisons between algorithms are shown in Figure 4. The result shows that MICA performs substantially better than others in both performance metrics (average trip time and stopped rate) in all traffic volume cases. In particular, one can see that the average trip time of MICA remains nearly constant while those of other algorithms increase as the traffic volume increases. Also, we can find that most vehicles cross an intersection without making a complete stop under MICA. Even in the highest traffic volume case when more than 60\% of the crossed vehicles experienced a stop under the other two algorithms, MICA maintains less than 10\% stopped rate for the same situation. Thus, the results show that the capacity of MICA in handling the intersection crossing traffic is much larger than others.

## VI. Conclusion

This paper uses the Mixed Integer Programming (MIP) to generate trajectories for vehicles to follow when they cross an intersection. In formulating the optimization problem, constraints for collision avoidance are carefully designed in order to ensure the safety of intersection crossing vehicles. Simulation results show that the proposed MICA (MIP-based Intersection Coordination Algorithm) achieves substantially higher throughput as well as fairness performance than other intersection control approaches, such as the optimized traffic light and DICA. Currently, we are improving MICA to make it applicable in practice by incorporating more realistic V2I wireless communication characteristics into the framework. Also, we plan to develop MICA on a network of intersections to optimize the traffic throughput performance on a larger scale.
