# Right-Angle Crash Occurrence at Signalized Intersections 

Xuesong Wang and Mohamed Abdel-Aty

Right-angle crashes are prone to be severe at signalized intersections. To understand right-angle crash occurrence better and eventually to develop efficient countermeasures, this study investigated the effect of intersection traffic volume, geometric design features, and traffic control and operational features on right-angle crash occurrence at four-leg signalized intersections. Data from a total of 197 such intersections were collected from the Central Florida area. Right-angle crashes were modeled at the intersection, roadway, and approach levels. For the models at roadway and approach levels, crashes were assigned to a certain level, and then the disaggregated crashes were related to the specific roadway or approach features. The generalized estimating equations, which can account for site correlation among repeated observations from the same intersections, were used for the disaggregated models. The cumulative residuals method was used to evaluate the functional forms of the traffic volume and the models' overall performance. For the significant factors identified, the logarithm of the product of the conflicting through volumes, the number of through lanes, and the late-night and early-morning flashing operations is found to affect right-angle crash occurrence consistently. Left-turn offset, angle of the intersection, speed limit on the intersecting roadways, and yellow and all-red intervals also are significant in some models. The variables' relative significance was identified.

Angle crashes result when vehicles collide while traveling on crossing paths. Right-angle crashes are those angle crashes in which the colliding vehicles were proceeding straight ahead from two intersecting roadways. Right-angle crashes typically result when one vehicle runs a red light or does not clear the intersection at the onset of the conflicting movement's green signal. A Florida study found that 45\% of right-angle crashes involve injury (nonincapacitating, incapacitating, and fatal injury), whereas only around 25\% of other crashes involve injury (1). Angle or red-light-running-related crashes at signalized intersections have been investigated by many studies, whereas comparatively fewer studies have focused on right-angle crashes specifically. Most red light running studies used right-angle, left-turn, and rear-end collisions as target crashes (2). Moreover, a previous study found that around 90\% of right-angle crashes were included in angle crashes (as classified in police reports); however, the proportion of right-angle crashes among angle crashes was only 33\% (3).

[^0]Right-angle crashes can be explored at the roadway or approach level, in which crashes are assigned to a specific level and then the disaggregated crashes are related to specific roadway or approach features. However, splitting an intersection will produce repeated observations, which necessitates site correlation among the roadways or approaches from the same intersection. In addition, a crash could be associated with the roadway or the approach of the at-fault vehicle (as designated in the police report) or with the roadway or approach whose stop line was closest to the crash site (defined as near-side in this study). The objective of this study is to investigate right-angle crashes at different levels (intersection, roadway, and approach) in order to better understand right-angle crash occurrence and identify the significant factors influencing crash occurrence, which include geometric design features, traffic control and operational features, and traffic-related explanatory variables. For the models at the roadway and approach levels, the criteria for assigning crashes as at-fault or near-side are compared, and the effect of the site correlation is accounted for by using the generalized estimating equations (GEEs), which are an extension of generalized linear models to the correlated data (4, 5).

## PREVIOUS STUDIES

Some studies have investigated angle crashes at the intersection level. The negative binomial regression is commonly applied for crash frequencies of different types including angle crashes. Kim et al. (6) fitted angle crash frequency for 165 two-lane intersections collected from rural areas in the state of Georgia. At signalized intersections, differences exist in traffic volumes, site geometry, signal operations, as well as safety performance on the various approaches of the intersections. Therefore, modeling the total number of angle crashes at intersections may obscure the real relationship between the crash causes (e.g., intersection characteristics) and their effect (e.g., angle crashes).

At the roadway level, Mitra et al. (7) fitted a head-to-side-crash (most are angle crashes) frequency model for 52 four-leg signalized intersections in Singapore over 8 years (1992-1999). Mohamedshah et al. (8) modeled red-light-running-related crashes at the roadway level for both major roadway as entering road and minor roadway as entering road over 4 years (1993-1996) for 1,756 urban four-leg signalized intersections. At the approach level, which has four observations per intersection, Poch and Mannering (9) fitted an angle crash frequency model for 63 four-leg intersections over 7 years (1987-1993). In all of these studies, the angle or the red-light-runningrelated crashes were assigned to a certain roadway or approach with the at-fault vehicle and negative binomial regression was applied.

Hall (10) applied Poisson regression for 14 crash groups including right-angle crashes over 4 years (1979-1982) at the approach level for 199 intersections in urban areas in Great Britain. Right-angle
crashes were assigned to the near-side approach. The factors of conflicting through flows, approach width, and average cycles per hour were identified to be positively related to right-angle crash occurrence. The angle with left-hand approach, central refuge island, and inflow per second of green time (proxy measures of reserve capacity) was identified to be negatively correlated with right-angle crashes. In the study, right-angle crashes include pedal cycle, motorcycle, and pedestrian crashes.

To test if the frequency of collisions is related to the traffic flows to which the colliding vehicles belong and not to the sum of the entering flows, Hauer et al. (3) divided the initial crashes into 15 patterns based on the vehicle maneuvers before the collision, in which right-angle crashes are Pattern 4. Data were collected in metropolitan Toronto, Ontario, Canada, for 145 four-leg, fixed-time signalized intersections over 3 years (1982-1984). Four negative binomial models for right-angle crashes were fitted for the different time periods in a day, and the through movement on only the minor approach (the approach with lower flow) of the intersecting approaches was identified to be significant and included.

Songchitruksa and Tarko (11) proposed a method to evaluate rightangle crashes on the basis of the frequency of short postencroachment times and the conflicting through volumes. They found that the product of the conflicting through volumes (having a positive sign) and the ratio of left-turn volume to total intersection volume (having a negative sign) was significant.

In previous studies, right-angle crashes were assigned to the nearside approach and modeled at the approach level. As for the functional form of traffic flows, some studies identified the through movements on only minor approaches as significant (3), whereas other studies included both conflicting flows (11). In addition, most previous studies on right-angle crashes focused on examining the relationship between traffic flow and crash occurrence. It is worth investigating the safety effect of intersection features including traffic flows on right-angle crash occurrence at different levels and assigning crashes in different ways (at-fault or near-side).

For the statistical model applied, because linear regression lacks the distributional property to adequately describe random, nonnegative, skewed, and typically sporadic vehicle crash frequencies (12), negative binomial regression has commonly been used. Dividing an intersection into roadways or approaches may produce site correlation among the data. Serious problems arise when basic count data models are used for correlated data, since basic count data models assume that the dependent variables are independent. For roadwayor approach-level models, the error structures become a mixture of random between-intersection errors and correlated within-intersection errors. GEEs provide an extension of the generalized linear model to the correlated data (4, 5), which has been applied for correlated crash frequency data (13-16).

## RESEARCH OBJECTIVES

To understand right-angle crash occurrence better and eventually to develop efficient countermeasures, in this study data on rightangle crashes, as well as geometric design features, traffic control and operational features, and traffic characteristics of four-leg signalized intersections, were collected. Right-angle crashes were modeled at intersection, roadway, and approach levels and the different criteria (at-fault or near-side) were used to assign crashes for the model at roadway or approach level. The GEEs with negative binomial regression were used to account for the site correlation among the data for the disaggregated models.

## DATA PREPARATION

A total of 945 signalized intersections from Orange and Hillsborough Counties in the Central Florida area were inspected. Intersections with at least one state road were considered since the crash, traffic volume, and control data are complete and available. The intersections that had major changes in geometry, signal timing, and traffic flow from 2000 to 2005 were excluded; therefore, the traffic operations for the selected intersections remained fairly uniform during the study period. Finally, 197 four-leg signalized intersections were selected, and intersection geometric design features, traffic control and operational features, traffic flows, and crashes over the study period were obtained. Comparison of the selected intersections against a random large statewide sample (1,562 signalized intersections) indicated that the sample used in this study is quite representative.

## Intersection Characteristics

Geometric design features for the intersections were extracted by inspecting the aerial imagery contained in the software Google Earth (17), which puts high-resolution aerial and satellite imagery and other geographic information on the desktop. For each approach, the number of through lanes, the number of left-turn lanes and whether they were exclusive, the presence of a median, whether there was an exclusive right-turn lane, the types of left-turn offset (negative, zero, or positive), and the angle with the intersecting approach were retrieved, and the direction of each intersection roadway was identified.

For both counties, the type of traffic control is correlated with the signal coordination; all coordinated intersections are semi-actuated, and all isolated intersections are fully actuated. Some intersections that are controlled by normal signals during most of the day transfer to flashing-mode operation during the late night and early morning. The yellow and the all-red intervals, the type of left-turn control for each approach (which includes permitted, protected, or protected/ permitted), and the speed limit for each approach were provided by the county traffic engineering department.

In Hillsborough County, the daily approach volumes for each intersection are available for only one year from 2000 to 2005; these data were converted into the approach annual average daily traffic (AADT) by using the growth rates over the study period provided by the traffic department. For Orange County, traffic volume is counted for roadway segments (in both approaching and departing directions) annually by the county traffic engineering department; the approach AADT was obtained by averaging the roadway segment AADT from 2000 to 2005. In both counties, the approach turning movements (right-turning, through, and left-turning) for the peak hours were counted for a year from 2000 to 2005. The approach daily turning movements were derived from the approach AADT supplemented by the proportion of approach turning movements at the peak hour.

## Crash Data

The crash analysis reporting system maintained by the Florida Department of Transportation (FDOT) is the most complete crash database for state road intersections in Florida, and it was used to retrieve the crash data over 6 years for the selected intersections. There was a total of 13,218 crashes over 6 years, and the annual average crash frequency per intersection was around 11.

The crash site location (e.g., at an intersection), the initial crash type (e.g., angle), the vehicle movement (e.g., straight ahead), the direction of travel (e.g., west), and the contributing cause (e.g., disregarded
traffic signal) for both vehicles and drivers are stored in the crash database. Of 2,431 initial angle collisions, 836 were right-angle crashes. Another 97 crashes classified as non-angle were considered right-angle crashes by inspecting their original crash reports through the state crash report document image retrieval system. Therefore, the total number of right-angle crashes for the selected intersections was 933, which was 4.74 per intersection in 6 years. The proportion of right-angle crashes among angle crashes was 34.4\%; 10.4\% were from other crash types. These percentages are close to the data used by Hauer et al. (3).

## METHODOLOGY

Negative binomial regression is a common tool for modeling crosssectional count data like right-angle crash frequencies at signalized intersections. GEEs provide an extension of generalized linear models (GLMs) to the analysis of clustered data, which can account for the site correlation among the repeated observations from the same intersections.

## Modeling Correlation in GEEs

GEEs come from specifying a known function of the marginal expectation of the dependent variable as a linear function of covariates, assuming that the variance is a known function of the mean, and specifying a working correlation matrix for the observations for each intersection (4, 5). Suppose that the frequency of right-angle crashes that occurred at intersection $i$ on roadway (or approach) $j$ is $y_{i j}\left(i=1,2, \ldots, K ; j=1,2, \ldots, n_{i}\right)$. In this case, $K$ is equal to 197; for the roadway-level models, $n_{i}=2$; and for the approach-level models, $n_{i}=4$. Let the vector of crash frequency for the $i$ th intersection be $Y_{i}=\left(y_{i 1}, \mathrm{~L}, y_{i n_{i}}\right)$ with corresponding means $m_{i}=\left(m_{i 1}, \mathrm{~L}\right.$, $m_{i n_{i}}$ ) and $V_{i}$ be an estimator of the covariance matrix of $Y_{i}$. Suppose that $x_{i j}=\left(x_{i j 1}, \mathrm{~L}, x_{i j p}\right)$ denotes a $p^{\prime} x 1$ vector of explanatory variables associated with $y_{i j}$. The GEE for estimating $b$ is an extension of the GLMs to the correlated data. If $n_{i}=1$, the GEE estimates are the same as the negative binomial regression. The link function and linear predictor setup are as for regular GLMs. The reader is referred to work by Liang and Zeger (4), Zeger and Liang (5), and Hardin and Hilbe (18) for more details.

Independent, exchangeable, autoregressive, and unstructured correlation matrices are the common working correlation structures. The goal of selecting an appropriate working correlation structure is to estimate parameters more efficiently (19). For roadway-level models, since there are only two observations per intersection, there is no difference in choosing exchangeable, autoregressive, or unstructured correlation structures. For the approach-level models, each intersection has four observations, and they were sorted following the sequence of eastbound, southbound, westbound, and northbound in the data. As the two approaches of the same roadway are more similar in design and control, there is a natural order for the four observations: two approaches from the same roadway are clustered within an intersection. The unstructured correlation structure assumes a different correlation between any two observations taken at the same intersection, and it is the most general of the correlation structures and imposes no restriction (18). It was applied for the approach-level GEE model to account for site correlation.

## GEE Model Assessment: Cumulative Residual Method

GEE estimates are obtained when a quasi-likelihood technique is used; therefore, the goodness-of-fit tests for the traditional negative binomial regression are not valid for the GEE negative binomial. Lin et al. (20) present a graphical and numerical cumulative residual method based on the cumulative sums of residuals for checking the functional form of continuous covariates and the link function of GEEs. For a GEE model, the distribution of the stochastic processes under the assumed model can be approximated by the distribution of certain zero-mean Gaussian processes whose realizations can be generated by simulation. Each observed residual pattern could then be compared, both graphically and numerically, with a number of realizations from the Gaussian process. Both the maximum absolute value of the observed cumulative sum and the $P$-value for a Kolmogorov-type supremum test can be calculated. Like the raw residual plot, if the model is correct, the residuals are centered at zero and the plot of the residuals against any coordinate should exhibit no systematic tendency.

## Variable Relative Effects: Type III Analysis

The Type III chi-square value for a particular variable is the difference between the generalized score statistics for the model with all the variables included and the generalized score statistics for the model with this variable excluded. The hypothesis tested in this case is the significance of this variable given that all the other variables are in the model. The small $p$-value indicates that the effect of this variable is highly significant (21).

## MODELING RESULTS

The right-angle crash frequency models at intersection, roadway, and approach levels were fitted by using the basic negative binomial and GEEs with the negative binomial as the link function using the SAS GENMOD procedure (21). Intersection traffic flows, geometric design features, and traffic control and operational features were explored for their safety effects on right-angle crash occurrences.

At the approach level, the at-fault and the near-side GEE models have higher $p$-values than the negative binomial models, as shown in Table 6. The roadway-level near-side GEE model is the best-fit model, with the maximum $p$-value of 0.9354 .

At the roadway level, the estimated correlation between two observations is 0.1827 and 0.4002 for the at-fault and the near-side models, respectively. The correlation matrix for the approach-level at-fault model shows that the approaches on the same roadway have a larger correlation. For example, eastbound and westbound and southbound and northbound have correlations of 0.2047 and 0.5006, respectively. For the approach-level near-side model, the maximum correlation is 0.3340.

## Variable Interpretation

The product of the conflicting through volumes, the number of through lanes, and the late-night-early-morning flashing operations is found to significantly affect right-angle crash occurrences in all models consistently. There is a significant difference between counties for right-angle crash frequency. Left-turn offset, angle of the intersection, speed limit on conflicting roadways, and yellow and clearance interval durations are also found to be significant in certain models.

The traffic volume is the primary variable of interest. The relationship between the traffic volume and right-angle crashes has been explored in the previous studies (3, 10, 11). Bonneson et al. (22) found that the approach volume is correlated with red light running frequency and red light running rate is correlated with approach right-angle crashes. In this study, the logarithm of the product of the conflicting through volumes was found to be significant consistently in the models at the different levels, which confirms the assumption that the frequency of collisions is related to the traffic flows to which the colliding vehicles belong and not to the sum of the entering flows. For example, the logarithm of the product of the through movements on entering and near-side crossing approaches is significant for the near-side approach model. Use of the product of the conflicting flows considers the interaction between conflicting flows.

The number of through lanes is correlated with the through volumes. In addition, the larger number of through lanes is associated with a longer crossing distance. Therefore, the number of through lanes was expected to be positively correlated with right-angle crash frequency. However, it has been found that this factor has a negative sign consistently in the models at different levels, as shown in Tables 4, 5, and 6. In a study predicting the red light running frequencies, Bonneson and Son (23) found that red light running decreases with increasing cross-street width. In another study, Bonneson and Zimmerman (24) concluded that drivers are less likely to violate a red indication at wide intersections.

In the at-fault roadway and approach models, it was found that providing zero or positive offset for opposing left-turn lanes at the crossing roadway will reduce right-angle crashes. The reason may be that the zero or positive offset will reduce the crossing distance compared with the negative left-turn offset.

Skewed intersections have wider crossing distance and poor sight distance, which might cause more right-angle crashes; however, in this study, skewed intersections are associated with fewer rightangle crashes, which is consistent with what Hall found (10).
