#### Abstract

Future intersection crossings for autonomous vehicles will not be controlled by traffic signals, rather a controller will be used for communication among vehicles that need to cross an intersection. In this work, we propose an innovative management system called Deep Reinforcement Learning-based Autonomous Intersection Management (DRLAIM) system, which is the first system to use deep reinforcement learning. We train the system to learn a good intersection control policy by interacting with traffic environment through reinforcement learning. The brake-safe control model is used to ensure the safety of each autonomous vehicle while crossing. Experiment results show that after training using reinforcement learning, the throughput of intersection control model increased by 83\%. In comparison with the Fast First Service (FFS) policy, the average waiting time of DRLAIM reduced by about 1.2\% to 11.4\%.


## I. Introduction

Intersection crossing policy is one of the most important issues in transportation systems. A good intersection crossing policy should guarantee road safety and make traffic flow more smooth. Road accidents at intersection significantly impact traffic flow and account for a significant proportion of all accidents. With the help of wireless communication and selfdriving technologies, various Autonomous Intersection Management (AIM) methods have been proposed which have designed advanced intersection crossing policies without human intervention [1]-[3]. AIM systems communicate with vehicles to obtain states of vehicles and use some crossing policies to schedule the vehicle crossing sequence efficiently, while guaranteeing safety. Several research work show that AIM systems outperform signal-based approaches [1], [2]; however, there are still much left for improvement. For example, in the state-of-the-art method called Fast First Service (FFS) [3] the average waiting time for high incoming traffic rates is quite high. Thus , in this research, we propose a Deep Reinforcement Learning-based Autonomous Intersection Management (DRLAIM) system to learn an intersection crossing policy that has a lower average waiting time at intersections.

## II. Deep Reinforcement Learning-based Autonomous Intersection Management

Our proposed DRLAIM system architecture is as depicted in Figure 1, which consists of two parts including vehicle and intersection controller. In the vehicle part, a brake-safe control model [3] guarantees the vehicle remains in the brake-safe state where it can always safely brake without colliding with other vehicles with higher priorities.

The intersection controller consists of four parts including queue, environment, priority assignment, and agent. The queue is designed with $n$ sub-queues, one for each lane, where $n$ is the total number of incoming lanes at a particular intersection. The environment module monitors the state of traffic environment, in terms of the queue size, average waiting time of vehicles and number of vehicles inside the intersection. The agent is responsible for choosing an action that allows vehicles to pass an intersection. At time step $t$, the agent observes the state $s_{t}$ from the environment, and chooses an action $a_{t}$ which allows vehicles to pass. Then the agent sends the action $a_{t}$ and corresponding requests from the queue to the priority assignment model to execute the action $a_{t}$. After executing the action, the state of the environment transits to $s_{t+1}$ and the agent receives a reward $r_{t+1}$. After the priority assignment model receives an action $a_{t}$ and corresponding requests, it will append the corresponding vehicles to the priority list that stores priority relations among vehicles one by one. The priority assignment model then sends confirm messages and priority list to corresponding vehicles.

## A. Deep Reinforcement Learning for Intersection Control

We define the three elements in Reinforcement Learning, that is, state space $S$, action space $A$, and reward $R$.

1) State Space: The queue sizes of all $n$ lanes at time $t$ are represented by the set $s_{t}^{Q}=\left\{s_{t}^{q, 1}, \ldots, s_{t}^{q, n}\right\}$ and the average waiting time of vehicles in each lane at time $t$ by $s_{t}^{W}=\left\{s_{t}^{w, 1}, \ldots, s_{t}^{w, n}\right\}$. The number of vehicles inside an intersection at time $t$ is represented by $s_{t}^{I}$. The state of an intersection at time $t$ is obtained by combining the above three into $s_{t}=\left\{s_{t}^{Q}, s_{t}^{W}, s_{t}^{I}\right\}$, which is called the traffic environment.
2) Action Space: The action space is of size $n+2$, where one action corresponds to choosing one of each of the $n$ lanes for all requesting vehicles of that lane to pass the intersection and the remaining two types of actions are (1) let all vehicles that have sent requests to pass, and (2) all vehicles are not allowed to pass.
3) Reward: After experimenting with 8 different definitions for the reward function, we chose to use $r=r_{q}-\max _{i} w_{t+1}^{i}+$ $r_{t h}$, where $r_{q}$ is the change in total queue size, $\max _{i} w_{t+1}^{i}$ is the maximum waiting time at lane $i$ at time $t+1$, and $r_{t h}$ is the change in throughput (number of cars leaving intersection).
4) Learning: The reinforcement learning algorithm used in this work is Q learning [4]. The definition of action-value function $Q\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)$ is

$$
Q\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)=r_{t+1}+\gamma \max _{a \in A} Q\left(s_{t+1}, a\right)
$$

where the $r_{t+1}$ is the received reward and $\max _{a \in A} Q\left(s_{t+1}, a\right)$ is the maximum value of Q-value in memory while performing a specific action $a$ at the next state $s_{t+1} . \gamma$ is the discount factor, where $0 \leq \gamma \leq 1$.

Furthermore, the Q-value is updated is as follows.

$$
\begin{array}{r}
Q\left(s_{t}, a_{t}\right)=(1-\alpha) Q\left(s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}\right)+\alpha( \\
\left.r_{t+1}+\gamma \max _{a \in A} Q\left(s_{t+1}, a\right)\right)
\end{array}
$$

where $\alpha$ is the learning rate and $0 \leq \alpha \leq 1$.
To circumvent the curse of dimensionality occurring in Q learning, instead we employ deep Q learning [5] which combines deep neural network with Q learning. By training the neural network, we can use it to approximate the optimal action-value function. The deep neural network $f$ is defined as $f: S \mapsto C$, where $S$ is the state space and $C$ is the values of all actions. The loss function is defined as follows.

$$
\operatorname{Loss}=\frac{1}{2}\left(r_{t+1}+\gamma \max \left(f\left(s_{t+1}\right)\right)-f\left(s_{t}\right)\right)^{2}
$$

where $r_{t+1}+\gamma \max \left(f\left(s_{t+1}\right)\right.$ is the true action-value and $f\left(s_{t}\right)$ is the predicted action-value.

## III. Experiments

The testing platform used in our experiment is a simulator called Simulation of Urban Mobility (SUMO) [6]. We compare our method using the model trained for 50 epochs using action time step 0.5 second with the Fast First Service (FFS) policy proposed in [3]. For each method, we simulated ten times for each of the 4 inflow rates, 25, 50, 75, 100 vehicles per minute. Table I shows the average performance results. Though the performance of throughput in FFS all outperform DRLAIM, the difference is small. The performance of AWT in DRLAIM outperforms that in FFS except for the low inflow rate of 25 vehicles per minute. We can see that the AWT of DRLAIM is less than that of FFS (reduced from 1.2\% to 11.4\%) except for the low inflow rate of 25 vehicles per minute. We believe that the reduction in AWT by DRLAIM is beacuse of the consideration of waiting time in the design of the proposed reward function, which leads to a much fair action selection policy.

## IV. Conclusions

We proposed a Deep Reinforcement Learning-based Autonomous Intersection Management (DRLAIM) system to learn an intersection crossing policy with low average waiting time. In DRLAIM, the brake-safe control model [3] determines the accelerate control of all vehicles that guarantees safety by avoiding collision. The intersection scheduling model based on deep reinforcement learning develops an action selection policy to determine an incoming lane from where vehicles can pass an intersection by observing and interacting with the traffic environment. In the comparison with the FFS policy, the average performance in terms of AWT in DRLAIM is reduced by 1.2\% to 11.4\%. Furthermore, though we consider the waiting time in the design of the proposed reward function to learn a fair action selection policy, the difference of average performance of throughput between DRLAIM and FFS is tiny.
