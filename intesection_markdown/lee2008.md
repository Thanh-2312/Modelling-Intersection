#### Abstract

This paper presents a new pedestrian simulation (PS) model for signalized crosswalks in Hong Kong. This PS model is capable of estimating the variations of walking speed particularly on the effects of bi-directional pedestrian flows so as to determine the minimum required duration of pedestrian crossing time. Video records taken from the observational surveys at the selected crosswalk in urban area were used to extract the required data for model calibration. It was found that the design walking speed for signalized crosswalks should be varied by the effects of the bi-directional pedestrian flows. It was also interesting to note that the negative impact of the bi-directional flow effects (ranging from uni-directional to bi-directional pedestrian flows) on the chance of pedestrian crossing the crosswalk is increasing from free-flow to at-capacity flow conditions. The new PS model is also validated using an independent data set so as to examine the reliability of the simulation results. The validation results show that the new PS model can provide an accurate evaluation on the changes of walking speed and its standard deviation under different scenarios with particular emphasis on the effects of the bi-directional pedestrian flows. The advancement of this PS model can be applied to assess the effects of each improvement measure and to evaluate the benefits of each scenario in practice.

## 1. Introduction

Among various pedestrian facilities, signalized crosswalks are one of the most complex facilities, with high risk for pedestrians in congested urban areas, since pedestrians and vehicles share the same road space but different time intervals, in accordance with the traffic signal cycles. The complexity of the traffic signals often creates problems since the provision and allocation of crossing time for pedestrians is usually apportioned from conflicting vehicular traffic flows. Therefore, the impact of the duration of the pedestrian green and flashing green times is thus a worthwhile study endeavor.

In Hong Kong, the current practice in signalized intersection design is to assume the pedestrian walking speed to be constant (i.e. 1.2 m/s) and fixed regardless of the prevailing pedestrian flow conditions. In view of this assumption, it is considered that the current approach is over-simplified. Pedestrians may not have adequate time to cross a signalized crossing particularly when the opposing pedestrian flow is high and the pedestrian flows on the crosswalk approaches to its capacity (Goh and Lam, 2004). It is thus felt that for pedestrian safety, the assessment/design of a signalized crosswalk should be based on its capacity but with the consideration of the walking speed under different flow ratios.

[^0]Seneviratne and Javid (1994) pointed out that the size of pedestrian facility is currently determined based on the design level of service. This simple approach to facility sizing is shown to have fundamental deficiency. Therefore, they proposed two analytical methods, one for determining optimal design density and another for determining optimal design flow, to determine the size of pedestrian facility. In Hong Kong, Lam et al. (2002) studied the pedestrian speed-flow relationships at signalized crosswalks in areas with different land uses. They also pointed out that pedestrians may not have adequate time to pass through the crosswalk when the pedestrian flow on the crosswalk and the opposing flow are heavy. In particular, they investigated the effects of bi-directional pedestrian flow on walking speed at capacity and effective capacity on Hong Kong signalized crosswalks. They suggested that a further study on the development of a pedestrian simulation model was required so as to simulate the bi-directional flow effects on signalized crosswalk.

Forecasting pedestrian flows and delays in congested urban areas requires a sophisticated pedestrian simulation model such as the PEDROUTE package (Halcrow Fox and Associates, 1994) developed for the London underground stations. In recent years, researchers, such as Blue and Adler (2001) focused on cellular automata (CA) microsimulation. CA microsimulation is characterized as an artificial life approach to simulate pedestrian behaviors and movements. Hoogendoorn et al. (2003) developed a pedestrian simulation model using user-optimal dynamic assignment in continuous time and space technique to analyze the instance walking infrastructure in a two-dimensional space. However, only uni-directional pedestrian movements are considered. The bi-directional flow effects have not particularly emphasis on their studies. In such a city with high population density like Hong Kong, pedestrian may not have sufficient crossing time to transverse the crosswalk as the crosswalk is overcrowded and they face dense oncoming pedestrians.

A number of studies have been conducted on average pedestrian delays at signalized crosswalks. In the highway capacity manual (Transportation Research Board, 2000), a simple model for estimating pedestrian delay is proposed with the assumption of uniform pedestrian arrival rate. Similar model can be found in Pretty (1979). Hunt and Al-Neami (1995) also proposed a regression model for estimating pedestrian mean delay. In Australia, Virkler (1998) observed that the delay reductions were due to some pedestrians passing through the crosswalks during flashing green times. Recently, Li et al. (2005) proposed a new function for estimating the average pedestrian delay. It was found that pedestrians arriving during pedestrian green time might also have delay. However, their studies are only confined to the estimation of the pedestrian delay. In United Kingdom, Griffiths et al. (1984a,b,c, 1985) developed a model to estimate pedestrian delays at zebra and pelican crosswalks. They were related to the mean delays on pedestrians and vehicles. However, the bi-directional flow effects have not explicitly taking into account in these previous studies. A sophisticated pedestrian simulation model particularly for environments with a heavy opposing pedestrian flow is required for predicting the variation of the pedestrian delays at signalized crosswalks.

In this study, a new pedestrian simulation (PS) model for signalized crosswalks was developed in Hong Kong. A simulation software package, service model, was adopted to estimate pedestrian walking times and delays at the chosen crosswalk in urban area. Observational survey with video recording was conducted at the study area to extract required data for model calibration. These data are cycle configuration, pedestrian arrival patterns, walking times, pedestrian delays and the stop/ cross probability when the pedestrians arrived during flashing green time. The pedestrian speed-flow function for signalized crosswalks calibrated by Lam et al. (2002) is incorporated into the developed PS model. Simulation results are presented to demonstrate the model's ability for estimating the variations on both pedestrian walking times and delays. This new PS model can be used to assess the effects of each improvement measure. Simulation results can also be used to evaluate the benefits for each scenario. Model validation is carried out with an independent set of data collected. A comparison between the observed data and simulation results is made and discussed. It is expected that the new PS model would be useful for enhancing the current assessment/design standards in Hong Kong and could be used as a reference source for other Asian cities with similar pedestrian flow characteristics.

## 2. The service model

The aim of any simulation package is to model the characteristics and interaction of all the elements of a system as faithfully as possible in order to predict accurately what will happen if a situation is changed. Six simulation packages (Service Model, Automod II, PEDROUTE, STEPS, Egress and Legion/Vegas) have been compared and reviewed by the Hong Kong Mass Transit Railway (MTR) Corporation (1998). The approach and degree of details in modeling pedestrian movement behaviors together with route choices under various situations were found to be the main differences in the six simulation packages.

The package, service model, developed by Promodel Corporation (2004) is used by the MTR Corporation to model the pedestrian movements in the underground stations (Lee and Lam, 2001). It is a window-based simulation tool for simulating and analyzing service systems. It provides flexibility and power for modeling any situations such as the provision of interface sub-routines for modeling pedestrian flow and facilities utilization. It also provides automatic error and consistency checking. Animated representation of the system is one of the main advantages of the service model. After a simulation run, it can automatically tabulate and plot the simulation results. The simulation environment provided by the service model is adequate to handle both normal and emergency situations. The model developer can completely control the approach, logic and degree of modeling detail in the service model so as to simulate the pedestrian flows and delays under normal situations or non-recurrent conditions with chosen special events. Therefore, the service model was selected as the platform for developing the pedestrian simulation model in this study.

## 3. Model calibration

Data such as pedestrian arrival patterns, stop/cross probability during flashing green time, pedestrian walking times and delays at each signal cycle were extracted from the video recorded from the observational survey. The collected data was used to calibrate the new PS model.

### 3.1. Experimental site

Causeway Bay is one of the most popular shopping districts in Hong Kong. It is flocked with crowds of people and heavy traffic throughout the day. Recently, the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region Government proposed to implement some pedestrianisation schemes ${ }^{2}$ and a pedestrian priority zone ${ }^{3}$ in Causeway Bay in order to give pedestrians priority in the use of road space. As the pedestrian movements within the chosen crosswalk are critical during the peak periods, an understanding or knowledge of peak hour pedestrian flow characteristics would help in the design and improvement of the pedestrian facilities in congested urban areas. Therefore, a pedestrian signalized crosswalk at Causeway Bay was selected for model calibration. This chosen signalized crosswalk is one of the busiest signalized intersections in Hong Kong urban areas. The physical characteristics, such as length, width and signal cycle time of this selected crosswalk are illustrated in Fig. 1. The length and width of the crosswalk are 18.2 m and 14.5 m, respectively. The cycle length was 120 s. The pedestrian green and flashing green times were about 30 s and 13 s, respectively. Moreover, the pedestrian red time was 77 s which included the all-red time (i.e. 2 s). For pedestrian signal at crosswalk in Hong Kong, the sequence is red, green, flashing green and red. When the pedestrian "steady red" signal (i.e. red stationary man) is illuminated, pedestrian shall not cross or start to pass through the crosswalk. The pedestrian green signal (i.e. green walking man) when illuminated by a steady light indicates that pedestrians can transverse the crosswalk. When the green signal is illuminated by an intermittent light (i.e. flashing green man), it implies that (i) pedestrians who are already on the crosswalk should speed up to completely pass through the facility before the pedestrian "steady red" signal is illuminated; and (ii) pedestrians who are not on the crosswalk should not start to cross the facility.

Blue and Adler (2001) pointed out that there were three fundamental elements of pedestrian movements that a bi-directional microscopic pedestrian simulation model should cater for:

(a) Side stepping - the desire of a pedestrian to "switch-lanes" move laterally to either enable increased velocity or avoid head-on conflicts;
(b) Forward movement - the desired speed of the pedestrian and the placement of other persons in the immediate neighborhood; and
(c) Conflict mitigation - the manner in which pedestrians approaching each other from opposite directions manage to avoid a head-on collision or deadlock.

These pedestrian movements can be easily observed at a congested crosswalk. When the pedestrian flow on the crosswalk approaches its capacity together with pedestrians face a heavy opposing flow, pedestrians have less freedom to choose their own walking speeds (forward movement). Then, they will try to shy away (or side stepping) from the opposing pedestrians so as to mitigate head-on conflicts (conflict mitigation).

Unlike the microsimulation rules used in CA, there are several built-in routing rules in the service model package. They are common rules for pedestrian routes such as:

(a) "First available" - pedestrians select the first location that has available capacity;
(b) "Most available" - pedestrians select the location that has the most available capacity;
(c) "Random" - pedestrians select randomly among two or more available locations; and
(d) "Probabilistic" - pedestrians select the location based on the probability specified.

In reality, pedestrians usually try to minimize their walking times when they walk on a crosswalk. They would like to select the first location that has available capacity. In view of this, the routing rule - "first available" was selected and
adopted in the PS model. This rule is the most realistic one to simulate the pedestrian walking behavior. This routing rule causes the first location (with available capacity) listed in a block of routes to be selected. Pedestrians can change lanes only when an adjacent location has available capacity. For the step forward movement, a minimum clearance distance of 0.5 m is kept in the front so as to eliminate conflicts with the opposing pedestrian. If an empty location between two pedestrians is available, pedestrian who first enters the empty space has the occupancy right (as stated in the "first available" rule) to be located in this space.

### 3.5. Pedestrian stop/cross probability during pedestrian flashing green time

In Hong Kong, there are three phases of pedestrian signal: pedestrian green time, flashing green time and red time. Pedestrians are not allowed to walk thought the crosswalks during pedestrian red time, but allowed to transverse during pedestrian green time. When the pedestrians arrived during flashing green time, they are also not allowed to enter the crosswalks. However, for those who were already on the crosswalks, they are forced to speed up until they can completely transverse the crosswalk for safety reason.

From the observation results, the stop/cross probabilities when the pedestrians arrived during flashing green time were determined. The duration of the flashing green time is 13 s . The trade-off between stop and cross decision is about half of the duration of pedestrian flashing green time (i.e. 6 s) from the empirical data. It was found that majorities of the pedestrians will immediately transverse the crosswalk if they arrive during the first 7 s of flashing green time. However, it was worth noting that over 50\% of pedestrians will not start to cross when they arrived during the last 6 s of flashing green time. Therefore, a time period of 6 s is selected for the stop/cross probability criteria. Two assumptions had been made. Firstly, it was assumed that all pedestrians will transverse the crosswalk once they arrived during pedestrian green time. Another assumption was that all pedestrians arriving during pedestrian red phase will not transverse the crosswalk until the next pedestrian green phase.

### 3.6. Pedestrian speed-flow relationship on signalized crosswalks

The relationship between pedestrian flows and walking times for Hong Kong signalized crosswalks in areas with different land uses had previously been calibrated by Lam et al. (2002). The signalized crosswalks in their study were situated in commercial and shopping areas with/without a light rail transit (LRT) station. The walking time function for crosswalks in shopping areas without a LRT station is chosen for incorporating in the PS model. The chosen pedestrian BPR (Bureau of Public Roads, 1964) function is given below:

$$
t(v)=0.7927+1.1192 \times\left(\frac{v}{78.5}\right)^{5.0365}
$$

where $v$ is the two-way pedestrian flow (ped/m/min); and $t(v)$ is the estimated average walking time (s) at flow $v$.
This walking time-flow function for pedestrians can only estimate the average walking time under certain pedestrian flow condition. A distribution of walking time data should be adopted based on the observed data collected by Lam et al. (2002). It was found that normal distribution can provide a good fit to the distribution of walking time data collected by Lam et al. (2002). Therefore, a new walking time function taking account the distribution of the observed data is presented as follows:

$$
t^{*}(v)=t(v) \pm N(0,1) \times S
$$

where $v$ is the two-way pedestrian flow (ped/m/min); $t(v)$ is the estimated average walking time (s) at flow $v ; N(0,1)$ is a random number which follows the standardized normal distribution with mean = 0 and standard deviation = 1; $S$ is the standard deviation of the mean walking time; and $t^{*}(v)$ is the newly estimated walking time ( s ).

Then, the new walking time function taking into account the distribution of the walking time data was then transformed into a walking time-density function for signalized crosswalk and incorporated into the developed PS model.

### 3.7. The effects of bi-directional pedestrian flow on signalized crosswalks

When pedestrians use a crosswalk with a heavy opposing pedestrian flow, they tends to shy away from that flows and have less freedom to choose their walking speeds. As a result, the effective capacity of the crosswalk and pedestrian walking speed is reduced. These effects are mainly due to the imbalance of pedestrian directional split (i.e. flow ratio) and particularly significant when the pedestrian flows are close to the capacity of the facility.

The pedestrian flow ratio is defined as:

$$
\text { Flow ratio }(r)=\frac{\text { One-way flow }}{\text { Two-way flow }}
$$

Pedestrian flow is the number of pedestrians passing through a measurement section of a walking facility per meter width per minute. The direction with a higher pedestrian flow is defined as major flow direction while the direction with lower pedestrian flow is defined as minor flow direction. Therefore, the range of the flow ratio $(r)$ is $0.0<r<0.5$ for the minor flow direction and $0.5 \leqslant r \leqslant 1.0$ for the major flow direction.

### 3.8. The bi-directional flow effects on effective capacity

The generalized walking time function for indoor walkway facilities was firstly proposed by Lam et al. (2003). This function can estimate the pedestrian walking time under different flow conditions ranging from free-flow to congested-flow conditions. This function also takes into account the bi-directional flow effects ranging from uni-directional flow to heavy opposing flow. However, there is no such a generalized walking time function for signalized crosswalk. Therefore, the relationship of the flow ratio against the effective capacity of the crosswalk proposed by Lam et al. (2002) was used to estimate the reduction on capacity due to the bi-directional flow effects. Table 1 summarizes the reduction on capacity due to the bidirectional flow effects. It can be seen that when the flow ratio equals to 0.1 (i.e. pedestrians in the minor flow direction are facing heavy opposing flows), the reduction on capacity is the largest (i.e. 14.7\%). It can be partially explained by the fact that pedestrians in the minor flow direction are very difficult to weave through the heavy opposing flows (Lam et al., 2003). It is also worth to note that the reduction on capacity is very small (i.e. 1.7\%) when the pedestrian flow is equal to 0.5 or greater. It can be explained that pedestrians in the major flow direction are dominant and formed as the uni-directional flow.

The bi-directional flow effects on capacity can be considered in the developed PS model on the basis of the relationship between the flow ratio and the effective capacity of the crosswalk proposed by Lam et al. (2002). The walking times would be generally increased when pedestrians walked on the crosswalk facing heavy opposing flows. However, further study is required to calibrate a generalized walking time function for signalized crosswalk with taking into account the effects of the bidirectional pedestrian flow under different flow conditions ranging from free-flow to congested-flow conditions.

## 5. Case study

In Hong Kong, it is assumed in the current practice in signalized intersection design that the pedestrian speed is 1.2 m/s. This design speed is fixed regardless of the prevailing pedestrian flow conditions. In view of this assumption, it is considered that the current approach is over-simplified. Pedestrians may not have adequate time to completely transverse a signalized crossing particularly when the opposing pedestrian flow is high and the pedestrian flows on the crosswalk approaches to its capacity during pedestrian green and flashing green phases. Therefore, for pedestrian safety, the assessment/design of a signalized crosswalk should be based on its capacity but with the consideration of the walking speeds under different flow ratios.

The purpose of this PS model is to provide estimation of pedestrian walking speeds over a wider range of flow conditions particularly when pedestrians encounter heavy opposing flow at signalized crosswalk. This estimation can be conveniently obtained by the newly developed PS model than observations on on-site as the latter is costly. This PS model can also show the effects of changes in some factors while the other factors are held constant.

### 5.1. The bi-directional pedestrian flow effects

This scenario is simulated by the PS model which considers different pedestrian flow demands together with different imbalance of directional pedestrian flows. The changes on the percentage of pedestrians crossing the crosswalk are investigated. The newly developed PS model can simulate pedestrian movements under different flow conditions ranging from unidirectional to imbalance directional split of pedestrian flow conditions.

Fig. 6 illustrates the simulation results showing the impacts of pedestrian flow and flow ratio on the chance of crossing. It should be noted that the pedestrian signal sequence and times were fixed. It can be seen from Fig. 6 that pedestrians will have 80\% chances to completely transverse the crosswalk under uni-directional flow condition (i.e. flow ratio = 1.0) when the pedestrian flow is low (i.e. 10 ped/m/min). However, if the imbalance of directional split of pedestrian flow increases (i.e. until flow ratio $=0.1$ ), a reduction on the chance of crossing of about 13\% will be obtained. The impact of bi-directional pedestrian flows on the chance of crossing is small when the pedestrian flow on the crosswalk is also low.

Moreover, if the pedestrian flow approaches to crosswalk capacity (i.e. 85 ped/m/min), pedestrians will only have 34\% chances to completely pass through the crosswalk when flow ratio equals to 1.0 . This percentage significantly reduces to 7\% when the flow ratio decreases to 0.1. This reduction can be partially explained by the fact that pedestrians in the minor flow direction (i.e. 0 < flow ratio < 0.5) are unable to select their walking speeds due to the heavy opposing flow. Hence, their walking speeds decrease while walking times increase as they required to weave through the heavy oncoming pedestrians. Therefore, their chances to completely pass through the crosswalk will be decreased.

It is also interesting to note in Fig. 7 that the chance of crossing reduces from 80\% to 34\% as the pedestrian flows at the crosswalk are approaching to its capacity under uni-directional flow condition. This reduction is more significantly observed under bi-directional flow condition. The chance of crossing of pedestrians reduces from 67\% to 7\% as the pedestrian flows increase from free-flow to at-capacity flow conditions.

In this study, it is observed that pedestrians in the minor flow direction may not have adequate time to cross a signalized crosswalk particularly when these pedestrians are facing heavy opposing flow and the total pedestrian flow on the crosswalk is close to its capacity. This calls for a need to review the current design walking speed for signalized crosswalk. Evidences showed that the walking speed is not only dependent on the total pedestrian volume but also depends on the bi-directional flow effects. It is suggested that different design walking speeds corresponding to different bi-directional flow conditions should be considered for determining the duration of the pedestrian green time at signalized crosswalks.

### 5.3. Average pedestrian delays

This newly developed PS model can estimate the pedestrian flow, average pedestrian delays and its variation during each signal cycle. This advancement can be used as a practical tool for the traffic engineers so as to minimize the pedestrian delays under certain pedestrian flow conditions. Pedestrian flow and average delay during each signal cycle were simulated. Fig. 8 illustrates the simulation results showing the average pedestrian delays together with pedestrian flows. It can be seen that the average delay is about 34 s which is about a half of the pedestrian red time (i.e. 77 s). Fig. 9 illustrates the variation of pedestrian delay. Average delays and its standard deviations for each signal cycle (i.e. per 2 min) are shown in Fig. 9. Variations of pedestrian delay are large when the average pedestrian delays are also large.

## 6. Conclusions

The newly developed pedestrian simulation (PS) model is capable for simulating pedestrian movements with consideration of the bi-directional pedestrian flow effects so as to determine the duration of pedestrian signal time. This advancement in model technique can be used for the assessment of the effects of each measure/scenario being considered. Hence, the benefit of each scenario can be evaluated. Simulation results highlighted the inability of the current design which does not accounted for the pedestrian flow volumes and different bi-directional flow effects. The simulation results demonstrated that different design walking speeds for signalized crosswalks are required with consideration of different bi-directional pedestrian flows. If the bi-directional flow effects were not accounted for in the design, pedestrians who entered the crosswalk facing heavy opposing flow under high flow conditions would not be able to complete their crossing before the end of the pedestrian flashing green times. Hence, safety of pedestrians is not ensured as they are leading to either risk crossing after the pedestrian flashing green or return and wait at the waiting area.

The development and calibration of the PS model had been outlined. Validation by using an independent set of observation data was carried out. The validation results clearly showed that the PS model also provides a comparatively good estimation not only on average walking speeds but also on their standard deviations. It is believed that the developed PS model for signalized crosswalks can serve as a practical tool for evaluating the pedestrian walking environment under different bidirectional flow conditions. The developed PS model can also contribute to the planning and design of pedestrian signalized crosswalks. Further study is required to calibrate a generalized walking time function for signalized crosswalk which takes account into the effects of the bi-directional pedestrian flow under various flow conditions.
