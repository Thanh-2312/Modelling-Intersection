# Multi-objective Cooperative Scheduling of CAVs at Non-Signalized Intersection 

Xing Zhao ${ }^{1}$, Jinxiang Wang ${ }^{1}$, Yifeng Chen ${ }^{2}$, and Guodong Yin ${ }^{1}$


#### Abstract

The cooperative scheduling problem of connected and automated vehicles(CAVs) at the non-signalized intersection is investigated in this paper. The mathematical model of the intersection is formulated with the hierarchical layout. Based on this model, an analytical control algorithm based on Pontryagin's minimum principle (PMP) is proposed for each CAV to solve the problem of multi-objective optimization considering the multi-constraints of the vehicle. In particular, the priorities of the optimization objectives are in this order: 1)guaranteeing vehicle safety, 2)alleviating traffic congestion, and 3)improving performance of fuel consumption or ride comfort. Low computational cost of the proposed algorithm guarantees that the algorithm with consideration of vehicle constraints can obtain the optimal solution quickly and steadily. Simulation results of high-speed CAVs traveling in the intersection area verify that the proposed algorithm is capable of achieving cooperative scheduling of CAVs at the non-Signalized intersection.


## I. Introduction

Intersections are the bottlenecks in transportation systems and the derivations of many traffic congestions and accidents . It was reported that in the United States at least 42 hours yearly delay for per passenger spent on the road and cost of an extra 3.1 billion gallons of fuel and 160 billion dollars totally in 2014 were intersection congestion related [1].

Thanks to tremendous progress in the field of computing, sensing, and communication technology in the past decade, it is now an opportunity to explore advanced technologies on vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V), on-board sensors, advanced driver assistance system (ADAS), and connected automated vehicles (CAVs). Based on these cutting-edge technologies, a fully autonomous driving systems will be conceived possibly [2], [3].

Ideally, two-way wireless communications between vehicles and the surroundings can be realized in CAVs , including V2V and vehicle-to-infrastructure (V2I) communications [4]. Based on inter-vehicle communications and onboard sensors, trajectory planning and path tracking can be achieved for each ego-vehicle of the CAVs. On the other hand, the intelligent cooperative technologies provide a novel way to solve the traffic conflicts and congestions at the intersections. Some previous studies have focused on automated intersection control and cooperation. Dresner et al. [5] designed a reservation-based structure of the

[^0]intersection area to control automated vehicles. The cells or points of divided intersection were called reserved area which no more than two vehicles occupied simultaneously. Li and Wang [6] proposed an approach of solution space to search the safety driving schedules from a spanning tree for CAVs at intersection. However, the high computational cost limits practical application of this method. Lee and Park [7] proposed an original concept of minimizing overlap to guarantee safety of each vehicle. Dai et al. [8] not only focused on ensuring the safe passage of vehicles and improving intersection throughput, but also considered quality of the travel experience from the passengers' perception. And the convex optimization method was applied to optimize the multivehicle performance with low overhead. Although previous researches have solved many problems theoretically, those complex algorithms are hard to be applied in practice. In [9], [10] simulations of CAVs at intersection validated that a simple analytical optimal closed-form solution had significant effectiveness on reducing both fuel consumption and travel time. But, due to the restriction of initial and final states of the vehicles and the unconstrained inputs in the optimization algorithm, the gap between the simulation and practical application is still wide.

The main contributions of this paper are (1) formulating the mathematical model of the intersection cooperation through redefining the conflict points and the intersection layouts and (2) proposing a new analytic multi-objective optimal control strategy for CAVs based on PMP and the logical priority of optimal order. Simulation results indicate that the proposed optimal control strategy can realize efficient cooperative scheduling for CAVs at non-signalized intersection, with improved performances of safety, mobility, riding comfort, and fuel efficiency for the CAVs.

## A. Intersection Layout

With consideration of limited space of the intersection, it is necessary to extend the space to a suitable distance out of the intersection for scheduling the CAVs. In the outwardfacing direction, the area near the intersection is named as the takeover zone as shown in Fig. 1, where the CAVs take over the full control authority. And depending on the variety of functions, the takeover zone is specifically divided into the detecting zone, the pre-control zone and the control zone. The length of the takeover zone, the detecting zone, the pre-control zone, and the control zone are identified as $L, L_{d}, L_{p}$, and $L_{c}$, respectively. In the detecting zone, each CAV rapidly receives and delivers the shared information through the base station. According to the information of its own and other CAVs, the following control strategy of the ego-vehicle are determined. In the pre-control zone, velocity of the lag CAV needs to be properly adjusted in order to avoid rear-end conflict with the preceding one at the control zone if necessary. In the control zone, each of the CAVs tracks the desired profile of velocity to reach the intersection zone at the desired entering time. Finally, the CAVs keep constant velocities within the intersection zone and depart from the intersection. For convenience, the area combining with the takeover zone and the intersection zone is called as the intersection area.

## B. Vehicle Constraints

To simplify the control process, only the longitudinal movement of the vehicles is considered in the takeover zone[13]. Such that the lateral movements such as overtaking and lane changing are not allowed. Thus, only longitudinal kinematics of the vehicle and the corresponding constraints are considered. Therefore each CAV can be regarded as a second order system described as

$$
\left[\begin{array}{c}
\dot{p} \\
\dot{v}
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{ll}
0 & 1 \\
0 & 0
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{l}
p \\
v
\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}{l}
0 \\
1
\end{array}\right] u
$$

where $p, v$, and $u$ are the displacement, velocity, and acceleration of the vehicle, respectively.

Considering actuator saturations and critical concerns of safety and ride comfort, constraints for the vehicle in the intersection include 1) the maximum longitudinal acceleration and deceleration, 2) the maximum longitudinal velocity, 3) the minimum time interval at the conflict point, and 4) the longitudinal velocity of the lag vehicle.

1) Maximum longitudinal acceleration and deceleration: Excessive acceleration or deceleration may cause discomfort to the driver and passengers, thus it is necessary to limit the acceleration and deceleration. On the other hand, the limitations of acceleration/deceleration need to consider the actuator saturation and vehicle stability. The constraint of acceleration/deceleration is defined as

$$
u_{\min } \leq u \leq u_{\max }
$$

where $u_{\text {min }}$ and $u_{\text {max }}$ are the maximum deceleration and acceleration, respectively.
2) Maximum longitudinal velocity: High longitudinal velocity may lead to uncontrollability of the vehicle, especially excessive lateral acceleration in cornering. For simplicity, the constraint of vehicle velocity departing from the control zone is defined due to finite length of the takeover zone. The specific velocity constraint is imposed as

$$
0 \leq v_{f} \leq v_{f \max }
$$

where $v_{f \text { max }}$ is the maximum allowable longitudinal velocity at the end of the takeover zone.
3) Minimum time interval at the conflict points: There were plenty of descriptions for the safety of CAVs in the intersection zone in previous literature. For example with the conservative algorithms, a large time interval between the preceding and the lag vehicles entering the intersection zone was determined to ensure the absolute safety [12]. A safe criteria was described as cost function in [3], [7], [15]. The conception of conflict point was proposed in [11]. Time difference between the potential conflict couple-vehicles to reach the conflict point must be greater than the safe time interval $\Delta t$. The benefit for this application of intersection safety is taking full advantage of the intersection space to improve the efficiency. Therefore, the minimum time interval is employed in this paper to avoid collision of CAVs at the conflict points. As shown in Fig. 2, the conflict model is modeled by using a common single-lane intersection with four approaches. The identifier of the path is denoted by $P_{a b}$, with subscription $a$ to identify the approach of CAV entering the intersection and $b$ to identify the exit for the CAV. All the conflict points can be indicated by the related path of designated CAVs. The identifier $C_{a b, c d}$ means the conflict point formed by crossing of path $P_{a b}$ and path $P_{c d}$. ## III. CONTROL ALGORITHM

In previous section, we have proposed a typical intersection model. In this section, the cooperative scheduling of the CAVs in the intersection area is addressed to derive an analytical solution to improve the performances of safety, efficiency and fuel consumption and ride comfort. Furthermore, the switching input is modified to eliminate the jerk, which is one of the disadvantage of the minimum principle.

## A. Optimal Control with Pontryagin's Minimum Principle

In [9], the constraints of control input and vehicle states were not considered. In this paper, the method of Pontryagin's minimum principle (PMP) [16] considering the constraints of control inputs is applied to derive the analytical solution for each CAV in the control zone. Considering the optimization of time and fuel consumption, the cost function is designed as

$$
\min J=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{f}}(k+|u|) d t
$$

where $t_{1}$ and $t_{f}$ are the time instants for CAV to enter and leave the control zone, respectively. $k$ is the time coefficient, and $|u|$ is the absolute value of the control input.

Considering (1) and (6), the Hamiltonian function can be generated for each CAV $V_{a b}^{i}$ as

$$
H_{i}=k_{i}+\left|u_{i}\right|+\lambda_{i}^{1} \cdot v_{i}+\lambda_{i}^{2} \cdot u_{i}
$$

where $\lambda_{i}^{1}$ and $\lambda_{i}^{2}$ are the co-state components. The necessary condition for PMP is

$$
H\left[x^{*}(t), u^{*}(t), \lambda(t)\right] \leq H \underset{u(t) \in \Omega}{\left[x^{*}(t), u(t), \lambda(t)\right]}
$$

where $x^{*}(t)$ is the optimal state, $u^{*}(t)$ is the optimal input, and $\Omega$ is the allowable region of the control input.

From (4)-(8), the relationship between control input $u$ and $\lambda_{i}^{2}$ can be deduced as

$$
u_{i}^{*}=\left\{\begin{array}{lr}
u_{\min } & \lambda_{i}^{2}>1 \\
0 & -1 \leq \lambda_{i}^{2} \leq 1 \\
u_{\max } & \lambda_{i}^{2}<-1
\end{array}\right.
$$

By using (9), we can obtain the displacement-velocity profile of the controlled CAV traveling within the intersection area. As shown in Fig. 3, the profile for the states of CAV can be switched according to the procedures within the control zone as follows:

1) At the beginning of current CAV $V_{a b}^{i}$ entering the control zone, the acceleration is switched to $u_{\text {max }}$. Later, the acceleration is switched to 0 at $t_{a b}^{i 2}$ and the CAV keeps a constant velocity. At the end of the control zone, the control input is switched to $u_{c \text { min }}$ at $t_{a b}^{i 3}$.
2) The current CAV $V_{a b}^{i}$ keeps a constant speed when departing from the control zone at $t_{a b}^{i f}$, and the identifier of current CAV is converted into $V_{a b, c d}^{j}$. Then, the current CAV reaches the conflict point $C_{a b, c d}$ at $t_{a b, c d}^{j}$.

Furthermore the relationship between $t_{a b, c d}^{j}$ and $t_{a b}^{i f}$ can be deduced as

$$
t_{a b, c d}^{j}=t_{a b}^{i f}+\frac{d_{a b_{-} c d}}{v_{a b}^{i f}}
$$

where $d_{a b \_c d}$ denotes the distance from the point $C_{a b, c d}$ to the entrance of the intersection, and $v_{a b}^{i f}$ indicates the velocity of $V_{a b}^{i}$ when departing from the control zone. It should be particularly noted that the values of $i$ and $j$ must satisfy $j \geq i$ for current CAV, because except for CAVs from path $P_{a b}$, CAVs from path $P_{c d} \in X_{a b}$ can also reach the conflict point $C_{a b, c d}$.

In this case, the fuel-time optimal mode can be applied. Specifically, with the increase of $k$ or decrease of $v_{f}$, both the maximum velocity of CAV and the time of acceleration/deceleration will increase. In this way, $k$ and $v_{f}$ can indirectly influence ride comfort and fuel consumption of the CAVs. To this end, $k$ and $v_{f}$ can be determined by

$$
\min f=\alpha_{1} \cdot v_{f}+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{k}
$$

where $\alpha_{1}$ and $\alpha_{2}$ are weights to balance the acceleration/deceleration time and the maximum velocity. And by combining (6), (13), and (14), the desired profile of control input considering efficiency, economy, and ride comfort can be solved analytically.

## C. Jerk elimination

One obvious disadvantage of using the minimum principle is instantaneous switching of the control input. Therefore, the first derivative of the acceleration becomes infinite at the switching point, leading to large jerk and bad travel experience for passengers. The sudden change of acceleration/deceleration at the switching point can be modified with a ramp to eliminate large jerk. This modification seldom influences the whole velocity profile, and consequently the time to reach the intersection zone is also not affected.

## C. Simulation results

The arriving time of the CAVs at the different conflict points with different initial conditions and the minimum interval time of each conflict point are listed in Table III. The last column indicates that the minimum interval time(MIT) is equal to or greater than the safe time interval $\Delta t$. So there are theoretically no collisions. Results of the velocity profiles and displacement profiles are shown in Fig. 5 and Fig.6, respectively. As can be observed from these figures, all CAVs have safely and efficiently passed through the intersection area. On can see in Fig. 5 that the time optimal mode is applied for the CAV $V_{21}^{1}$, because the $V_{21}^{1}$ is not disturbed by the other CAVs. Comparatively, CAV $V_{13}^{1}$ needs to be decelerated within the pre-control zone and the fuel-time optimal mode is applied within the control zone to prevent collision between $V_{13}^{1}$ and the preceding CAV $V_{12}^{1}$. What's more, owing to the modification of the switching input, the vehicle velocities vary smoothly as can be observed in magnified diagram of Fig. 5. And Fig. 6 also indicates that each CAV travels 700m from detecting zone to intersection zone orderly and smoothly. In particular, from the perspective of efficiency, the time interval between the first and last CAV entering into the detecting zone is 8.5s, and the time interval between the first and last CAV entering the intersection zone is 10.2s. As a result, the total delay of the CAVs traveling in intersection area is almost negligible. So, the congestion in the intersection area are potentially alleviated with the proposed algorithm.

## V. CONCLUSIONS

In this work, we have addressed the problem of multivehicle coordination at the non-signalized intersection. First of all, the mathematical model of the non-signalized intersection control is formulated by redefined conception of the conflict point and intersection layout. And the coordination and scheduling problem is formulated by two parts: 1) analytical solution solved with PMP, and 2) the determination of optimal mode. Simulation results verify that the analytical solution with low computational cost can be obtained, and safety of the CAVs are guaranteed with the proposed algorithm. And passing-though efficiency, fuel consumption, and ride comfort of the CAVs are also enhanced.
